What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Chollas View Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping

  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation process that uses nothing but clean, high-pressure water. There are no chemicals at any stage, which matters in an urban watershed like Chollas Creek that is already the subject of water-quality monitoring. Here is what happens:

  • Packers isolate a zone. Inflatable packers are lowered into the casing and inflated to seal off a target section of the well, concentrating the pressure where the water-bearing rock is.
  • High-pressure injection. Clean water is pumped into that zone at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000-plus PSI, flushing silt and mineral buildup out of existing fractures.
  • Fractures open and connect. The pressure widens fractures and can link the borehole to nearby water-bearing cracks, enlarging the volume of rock that feeds the well.
  • Development and testing. Afterward the well is surged, pumped to clear debris, and flow-tested to confirm any improvement.

Common Causes of Low Yield in This Area

For the private wells that do exist around Southeastern San Diego, declining yield usually traces back to a handful of causes tied to the local hard-rock and mixed sedimentary geology:

  • Fracture plugging. Wells in the harder rock of the surrounding mesas and canyons rely on thin fractures that gradually fill with fine sediment.
  • Mineral scaling. Calcium and iron deposits build up across the intake and along fracture faces, slowly choking flow.
  • Aging, neglected wells. Because so few wells remain in active use here, many are old and have sat with little maintenance, so screens and casings have corroded or partially clogged.
  • Low-yield formations. Some wells simply intersect only a few productive fractures, which is precisely the situation hydrofracturing can sometimes improve.
  • Long periods of disuse. A well that has not been pumped regularly can accumulate fine material and biological growth around the intake, restricting flow once it is put back into service.

What to Check Before You Call

A little homework helps you avoid paying for the wrong fix:

  1. Confirm it is really the well. Low pressure is often caused by a failing pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a bad pressure switch, all of which are far cheaper to repair than a yield problem.
  2. Note the timing. A shortage that appears only during heavy summer use and recovers overnight points to a recovery-rate issue, which hydrofracturing can address.
  3. Look at the water. Grit or cloudiness may indicate sediment moving through the formation and collecting at the intake.
  4. Check the pressure tank. If it feels solid with no air cushion, the bladder has likely failed.
  5. Find the well log. The original driller's report showing depth and water-bearing zones tells a technician a great deal about whether your well will respond.

Signs You May Need Hydrofracturing

Watch for several of these together, which point to a genuine yield problem:

  • Water output that has declined steadily over several years.
  • The well drawing down quickly and recovering slowly after heavy use.
  • The pump short-cycling more than it used to.
  • Air spitting from faucets when more than one fixture runs.
  • A relatively new rock well that never produced the flow you expected.
  • A noticeable change after a long dry stretch, when the surrounding formation has had less chance to recharge.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure equipment, properly sized packers, and the judgment to read a specific formation, so it is not a do-it-yourself job. Contact a licensed well contractor when your yield has clearly fallen, when a flow test confirms production below your needs, or when you are weighing rehabilitation against the cost of a new well. A professional begins with a casing inspection, usually by camera, both to verify the casing is sound and to judge whether the well draws from material that will respond. For an urban-fringe well that may have sat unused for years, that inspection is especially valuable because it often turns up simple, inexpensive problems before any major work is considered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there even private wells in Chollas View?

Very few. Chollas View is an urban neighborhood on City of San Diego municipal water, so private wells are rare. This page is written mainly for the handful of legacy, irrigation, or canyon-edge well owners in and around Southeastern San Diego who still depend on one.

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil and gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water to reopen existing fractures in shallow rock. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no deep shale formations. It is a long-established well-rehabilitation method, completely separate from oil and gas extraction.

Will hydrofracturing work on my well?

It depends on your geology. Wells set in fractured rock respond well, often improving two to five times, while wells in loose sand are generally weaker candidates. We inspect the casing and formation before recommending the treatment.

How long does the process take?

Most residential jobs finish in a single day. The high-pressure injection itself runs roughly four to eight hours depending on how many zones we treat, and we flow-test afterward, usually confirming results within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000 or more. For most low-yield wells it is the smart first step.

My well has sat unused for years. Can it be revived?

Possibly. We start with a camera inspection and a flow test. Sometimes the fix is as simple as a new pump or pressure tank; in other cases hydrofracturing can meaningfully restore yield. We tell you honestly which path makes sense.
