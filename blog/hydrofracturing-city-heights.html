What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation process that uses only clean, high-pressure water. No chemicals are used at any stage, which is reassuring in an urban watershed like Chollas Creek that is closely monitored for water quality. The steps are simple:

  • Packers isolate a zone. Inflatable packers are lowered into the casing and inflated to seal off a target section of the well, focusing the pressure on the water-bearing rock.
  • High-pressure injection. Clean water is pumped into that zone at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000-plus PSI, flushing silt and mineral buildup out of existing fractures.
  • Fractures open and connect. The pressure widens fractures and can link the borehole to nearby water-bearing cracks, enlarging the volume of rock that feeds the well.
  • Development and testing. The well is then surged, pumped to clear debris, and flow-tested to confirm any improvement.

Common Causes of Low Yield in This Area

For the few wells that exist around mid-city and the nearby canyons, declining yield typically traces to a handful of causes tied to the local mix of hard rock and sedimentary formations:

  • Fracture plugging. Wells in the harder rock of the surrounding mesas and canyon walls rely on thin fractures that gradually fill with fine sediment.
  • Mineral scaling. Calcium and iron deposits build up across the intake and along fracture faces, slowly narrowing the flow path.
  • Aging, idle wells. Because active wells are so uncommon here, many that remain are old and have sat with little maintenance, so their screens and casings have corroded or partly clogged.
  • Low-yield formations. Some wells intersect only a few productive fractures, which is exactly the situation hydrofracturing can sometimes improve.

Local Geology Around Mid-City and the Canyons

The mesas and canyons that define this part of San Diego are built from a layered mix of marine sedimentary formations capped by older terrace deposits, with harder, fractured rock appearing along canyon walls and the higher ground toward the east. A well's behavior depends entirely on which of those it taps. A borehole that reaches a fractured-rock zone moves water through cracks and seams, and those are precisely the fractures that hydrofracturing can clean out and reconnect. A shallow well sitting in loose terrace sand behaves very differently and is usually a poor candidate for the treatment. This is why no honest contractor can promise results sight unseen: the right answer depends on your specific well, its depth, and the formation it draws from, all of which a camera inspection and a baseline flow test will reveal.

What to Check Before You Call

A little homework keeps you from paying for the wrong fix:

  1. Confirm it is the well. Low pressure is often caused by a failing pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a bad pressure switch, all far cheaper to repair than a yield problem.
  2. Note the timing. A shortage that appears only under heavy summer use and clears overnight points to a recovery-rate issue, which hydrofracturing addresses.
  3. Look at the water. Grit or cloudiness can mean sediment is moving through the formation and collecting at the intake.
  4. Check the pressure tank. A tank that feels solid with no air cushion usually has a failed bladder.
  5. Find the well log. The original driller's report showing depth and water-bearing zones tells a technician a great deal about whether your well will respond.

Signs You May Need Hydrofracturing

Several of these together suggest a genuine yield problem:

  • Water output that has declined steadily over several years.
  • The well drawing down quickly and recovering slowly after heavy use.
  • The pump short-cycling more than it used to.
  • Air spitting from faucets when more than one fixture runs.
  • A relatively new rock well that never produced the flow you expected.
  • A noticeable drop after a long dry stretch, when the surrounding formation has had less chance to recharge.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure equipment, properly sized packers, and the judgment to read a specific formation, so it is not a do-it-yourself project. Contact a licensed well contractor when your yield has clearly fallen, when a flow test confirms production below your needs, or when you are weighing rehabilitation against the cost of a new well. A professional begins with a casing inspection, usually by camera, both to verify the casing is sound and to judge whether the well draws from material that will respond. For a well that may have sat idle for years near the urban fringe, that inspection is especially valuable, because it often uncovers simple, inexpensive problems before any major work is considered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there private wells in City Heights?

Almost none. City Heights is a dense urban neighborhood on City of San Diego municipal water, so private wells are extremely rare. This page is written mainly for the few legacy, irrigation, or canyon-edge well owners in and around mid-city and East County who still rely on one.

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil and gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water to reopen existing fractures in shallow rock. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no deep shale formations. It is a long-established well-rehabilitation method, completely separate from oil and gas extraction.

Will hydrofracturing work on my well?

It depends on your geology. Wells set in fractured rock respond well, often improving two to five times, while wells in loose sand are generally weaker candidates. We inspect the casing and formation before recommending the treatment.

How long does the process take?

Most residential jobs finish in a single day. The high-pressure injection itself runs roughly four to eight hours depending on how many zones we treat, and we flow-test afterward, usually confirming results within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000 or more. For most low-yield wells it is the smart first step.

My well has not been used in years. Can it be revived?

Possibly. We begin with a camera inspection and a flow test. Sometimes the fix is as simple as a new pump or pressure tank; in other cases hydrofracturing can meaningfully restore yield. We give you an honest recommendation either way.
