What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Loma Linda well.

Rusty Well Water in Loma Linda: A San Bernardino County Guide

Loma Linda sits in the southwestern San Bernardino Valley, tucked between Redlands, San Bernardino, Colton, and Grand Terrace, with the slopes of the San Bernardino Mountains rising to the northeast. The community overlies part of the Bunker Hill Basin, one of the largest groundwater basins in Southern California, replenished by rainfall and snowmelt off the mountains and by the headwaters of the Santa Ana River, Mill Creek, and Lytle Creek. With that abundant groundwater comes a familiar issue for well owners: water that runs rusty, orange, or reddish-brown from the tap. In almost every case, the cause is iron, manganese, or corroding metal somewhere in the system.

Rusty water is frustrating, but it is also one of the more fixable well problems once you identify the source. This guide explains how iron gets into Loma Linda-area groundwater, the signs that reveal where the rust is coming from, and what professional repairs realistically cost.

How Iron and Manganese Reach Bunker Hill Basin Wells

The Bunker Hill Basin is filled with thick alluvial deposits washed down from the surrounding mountains — sand, gravel, silt, and clay that hold enormous quantities of groundwater. As water percolates through those sediments, it dissolves trace minerals, including iron and manganese, out of the rock and soil. Both metals are common and naturally occurring, but once iron exceeds roughly 0.3 mg/L or manganese exceeds 0.05 mg/L, they begin to stain fixtures and laundry.

The orange color is a chemical reaction. Deep in the aquifer, iron exists in a dissolved ferrous form that is actually colorless. The moment that water reaches the surface and contacts oxygen — in a toilet tank, a pressure tank, or a glass on the counter — the iron oxidizes into ferric particles, the rust we recognize. Manganese behaves the same way but produces a darker, brown-to-black stain. Water that pours clear and develops an orange tint as it sits is the textbook sign of dissolved iron.

Common Causes in the Loma Linda Area

  • Dissolved aquifer iron: The mineral was always in the basin's groundwater; rising levels or aging filtration finally make it visible.
  • Iron bacteria: Microorganisms that feed on iron and leave a slimy, rust-colored residue, often with a swampy smell. They thrive in low-use wells.
  • Corroding steel casing: Older wells cased in steel rust from the inside and shed flakes into the water.
  • Galvanized plumbing: Aging galvanized iron pipe in older Loma Linda and Redlands-area homes corrodes internally and tints the supply.
  • Failing water heater: Rust only on the hot side usually traces to a corroding anode rod or tank, not the well.

Is Rusty Water a Health Concern?

Iron and manganese are classified as secondary contaminants — regulated by the EPA for taste, color, and staining rather than acute toxicity. A glass of slightly orange water will not harm a healthy adult. Still, rusty water carries real drawbacks:

  • A metallic taste that affects drinking water and cooking.
  • Stubborn orange staining on porcelain, grout, and laundry.
  • Mineral buildup that clogs pipes and shortens appliance life.
  • Iron-bacteria slime that fouls well screens and pumps.
  • Sudden discoloration that can signal a casing breach or surface intrusion worth investigating.

Because health guidance for manganese has tightened, especially for infants, a lab test is worthwhile when the water shows a brown-black tint.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Hot vs. cold: Rust limited to hot water points to the water heater.
  2. One fixture vs. whole house: Isolated rust suggests a corroded line or aerator.
  3. Clear-then-orange: Indicates dissolved iron oxidizing in air.
  4. Cloudy-orange from the tap: Indicates sediment, casing flakes, or pipe scale.
  5. Smell and slime: A musty odor with reddish sludge points to iron bacteria.

Collect a sample in a clean jar, note how quickly it discolors, and save it for your technician. A test strip gives a rough reading; a certified lab test gives the numbers that determine the right treatment.

Proven Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron and Manganese Filtration

For dissolved metals, an oxidizing or catalytic media filter converts iron and manganese to a filterable solid and backwashes them away. Lighter iron with hard water can sometimes be managed with a properly sized softener, while heavier loads call for air- or chemical-injection ahead of the filter. The correct system depends on your water's pH and flow rate.

Shock Chlorination and Well Cleaning

When iron bacteria are confirmed, a strong chlorine treatment disinfects the well and plumbing, usually paired with mechanical cleaning of the casing and screen. Persistent cases may require a maintenance chlorination program.

Casing and Plumbing Repairs

A badly corroded casing can be lined or replaced, and old galvanized house pipe can be swapped for PEX or copper to remove household rust permanently.

When to Call a Professional

Call a licensed well contractor when rusty water appears suddenly with no household explanation, when both hot and cold water are discolored throughout the home, when you see sand or grit with the rust, or when a strong sulfur smell accompanies it. These signs can indicate a failing casing, a pump drawing from a compromised zone, or an entrenched bacterial colony. A professional can test the water, inspect the casing with a downhole camera, evaluate pump performance, and target the actual cause rather than the symptom.

Why Local Experience Matters in San Bernardino County

Groundwater conditions vary widely across San Bernardino County. A well drawing from the deep, sediment-rich Bunker Hill Basin near Loma Linda behaves very differently from a fractured-rock well in the foothills or a shallow well closer to the Santa Ana River wash. The same rusty-water complaint can have a completely different cause and cure depending on where your well sits, how deep it is, and how its casing was built. An iron-removal system that performs beautifully in one part of the valley can foul or short-cycle in another if it is not matched to the water's pH, hardness, and flow rate. Technicians who work the San Bernardino Valley regularly recognize these patterns and can size a system to your specific water instead of guessing.

Protecting Your Well From Recurring Rust

After your water is clear again, a little routine care keeps it that way. We recommend an annual water test to catch rising iron or manganese before it stains, scheduled backwashing and media replacement on your filter, and periodic checks of the pressure tank and switch. Wells that sit idle for long stretches — irrigation supplies, guest properties, or seasonal homes — benefit from regular use, since stagnation is exactly what lets iron bacteria establish themselves. For homes still running on older galvanized plumbing, planning a gradual switch to PEX or copper stops the internal pipe rust that can re-tint otherwise clean water. These small habits protect both your water quality and the equipment you have already paid for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Loma Linda well water clear at first and orange later?

That delay is dissolved ferrous iron oxidizing in the air. It is colorless underground and turns into orange rust once exposed to oxygen. An iron filter is the usual remedy.

Does the Bunker Hill Basin naturally have iron?

Yes. The basin's thick alluvial sediments contain iron and manganese that groundwater dissolves as it moves through them, so iron staining is a common complaint for area well owners.

Can a water softener alone fix my rusty water?

A softener can manage light iron through ion exchange, but it is not designed for heavy iron, manganese, or iron bacteria. Testing tells us whether a softener is enough or a dedicated iron filter is needed.

What is the reddish slime in my toilet tank?

That is iron bacteria. It is not an acute health hazard but clogs screens and pumps over time. Shock chlorination eliminates the colony.

How much does water testing cost?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any repair or treatment system we install, and it measures iron and manganese to size the solution correctly.

Will treating iron also remove the metallic smell?

Usually yes, since the taste and smell come from the same iron and manganese the filter removes. If a rotten-egg odor remains, that indicates sulfur or bacteria, which we can also treat.
