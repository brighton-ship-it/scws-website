Common Coastal Well Emergencies We Handle

Cardiff's coastal setting adds a few wrinkles to the usual list of well failures. Salt air, humidity, and a high, shifting water table near the lagoon all play a role. These are the urgent problems we see most:

  • Complete loss of water — the tap runs dry with no warning. This is usually a stopped pump, an upstream electrical fault, a tripped safety, or a stuck check valve. It is the single most common reason people call our emergency line.
  • Submersible pump burnout — a motor that has been short-cycling or running against a failing check valve overheats and burns out. On coastal properties, corrosion at electrical connections can also strain the motor until it quits.
  • Corroded wellhead wiring and control boxes — the salt-laden marine air in Cardiff is hard on outdoor electrical components. We frequently find pitted terminals, corroded splices, and failed control boxes that would last far longer a few miles inland.
  • Pressure switch failure — a burned or corroded pressure switch is cheap to replace but a common cause of a pump that won't start or won't stop. Marine humidity accelerates that corrosion.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank — when the tank's air bladder fails, the pump cycles rapidly, which shortens its life dramatically. Replacing the tank restores steady pressure and protects the pump.
  • Salt-water intrusion and water-quality shifts — wells close to the shoreline or the San Elijo Lagoon can be sensitive to salt intrusion and seasonal water-level changes. A sudden change in taste, cloudiness, or flow warrants immediate testing.
  • Stuck check valve or lost prime — a check valve that fails open lets water drain back down the casing, so the pump loses prime and delivers air and sputter instead of steady water.

Several of these failures feed one another — a bad tank kills a pump, corrosion starves a motor — so pinning down the true root cause on the first visit is what prevents a repeat emergency a month later.

What to Check Before We Arrive

While our technician is en route, a few safe checks can occasionally restore water or speed up the diagnosis:

  • The breaker. Locate the dedicated well-pump breaker and confirm it hasn't tripped. Reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop — that signals a shorted pump or wiring fault, which coastal corrosion makes more common here.
  • The pressure switch. Inspect the small switch near the pressure tank for scorching or corrosion. Salt-air pitting on the contacts is a frequent Cardiff culprit.
  • The pressure tank. Tap the tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow up top and solid at the bottom; if it sounds full and heavy all the way up, the bladder has likely failed.
  • The gauge. Note the reading. Zero pressure with the pump running points one direction; normal pressure but no flow at the taps points another.

Please don't open the well cap, disturb wiring, or try to lift a submersible pump yourself. Those tasks involve heavy equipment and live electrical connections — that is what our crews are trained and equipped to handle safely.

I have no water — what can I safely check first?

Check the well pump breaker, the pressure switch near the tank, and the pressure tank's air charge. Reset the breaker once; if it trips again right away, leave it off and call us, because that usually means a shorted pump or wiring fault that is unsafe to keep resetting.

Does being near the coast and the San Elijo Lagoon affect my well?

It can. Coastal humidity accelerates corrosion on wellhead wiring and control boxes, and wells close to the lagoon or shoreline can be sensitive to salt-water intrusion and shifting water levels. We factor those coastal conditions into every diagnosis and recommendation in Cardiff.

Why does my pump keep short-cycling and could it burn out?

Rapid on-off cycling almost always traces to a waterlogged pressure tank or a bad pressure switch. That cycling overheats the pump motor and, if ignored, burns it out. Replacing the tank or switch early is far cheaper than replacing the pump after it fails.
