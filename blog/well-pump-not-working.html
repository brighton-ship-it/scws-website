Well Pump Not Working? Complete Troubleshooting Guide

Published February 2, 2026 | Troubleshooting | 16 min read

You turn on the faucet and nothing comes out. Your well pump isn't working, and suddenly your entire household is at a standstill. Before you panic—or call for expensive emergency service—there are several things you can safely check yourself that might get your water flowing again.

This guide walks you through step-by-step well pump troubleshooting for San Diego County homeowners. We'll cover safe DIY checks, common causes of pump failure, and when it's time to call a professional. By the end, you'll either have solved the problem yourself or know exactly what to tell the technician when they arrive.

⚠️ Safety First

Before troubleshooting:

  • If you smell burning or see smoke: Turn off the pump breaker immediately and call a professional
  • If the breaker keeps tripping: Do NOT keep resetting it—this is a fire hazard
  • Turn off power before opening any electrical components
  • If you're uncomfortable with electrical work: Skip straight to calling for help

Step 1: Check the Circuit Breaker

The most common reason for a well pump not working is a tripped circuit breaker. This is also the easiest thing to fix.

How to Check the Breaker

  1. Locate your electrical panel (usually in the garage, basement, or outside)
  2. Find the breaker labeled "Well Pump," "Well," or "Water Pump" (typically a 240V double breaker)
  3. If it's in the middle position or clearly tripped, push it firmly to OFF, then back to ON
  4. Wait a moment and listen for the pump to start

✅ If the pump starts:

Great! A single trip can be caused by a power surge or momentary overload. Monitor it—if it trips again soon, there's an underlying problem.

❌ If the breaker trips again immediately:

STOP. Do not keep resetting it. There's an electrical fault that could cause a fire. Call a professional immediately.

Why Breakers Trip

  • Power surge or lightning strike: Common during storms, usually a one-time event
  • Failing pump motor: Drawing excess current as it deteriorates
  • Water in the motor: Causing a short circuit
  • Damaged wiring: Between the panel and the pump
  • Overheating: Pump ran dry or worked too hard

Step 2: Inspect the Pressure Switch

If the breaker is fine, the pressure switch is the next most common culprit. This small device near your pressure tank tells the pump when to turn on and off.

⚡ Turn Off Power First!

Before opening the pressure switch cover, turn off the well pump breaker at the electrical panel. The pressure switch carries 240 volts—enough to cause serious injury.

How to Inspect the Pressure Switch

  1. Turn off the breaker at the electrical panel
  2. Locate the pressure switch (small gray or black box near the pressure tank with wires running to it)
  3. Remove the cover (usually snaps off or has a single screw)
  4. Look for:
    • Burnt or blackened contacts
    • Pitted or corroded contact points
    • Ants or other insects (very common in San Diego!)
    • Loose or disconnected wires
    • Signs of water damage or corrosion

Ant infestations are a surprisingly common cause of well pump problems in San Diego County. Ants are attracted to the electrical current and can interfere with the switch contacts. If you find ants, clean them out carefully and consider treating the area.

If the contacts look burnt or pitted, the pressure switch needs replacement. This is a $150-$300 repair that most well service companies can complete quickly. See our pressure switch guide for more details.

Step 3: Check the Pressure Gauge and Tank

Your pressure tank has a gauge that shows system pressure. This tells you a lot about what's happening:

Gauge Reading What It Means Likely Cause
0 PSI Pump isn't running or building pressure Electrical issue, dead pump, or major leak
Below 20 PSI Very low pressure Pump struggling, low well level, or leak
40-60 PSI Normal operating range System working correctly
Above 80 PSI Pressure too high Pressure switch not cutting off pump
Needle bouncing Pressure fluctuating rapidly Waterlogged pressure tank

Test Your Pressure Tank

A waterlogged pressure tank is a common cause of well pump problems. Here's how to check:

  1. Tap the tank from top to bottom with your knuckles
  2. A healthy tank sounds hollow at the top (air) and solid at the bottom (water)
  3. If it sounds solid all the way up, the tank is waterlogged—the bladder has failed

A waterlogged tank causes the pump to short-cycle (turn on and off rapidly), which burns out pump motors prematurely. If your tank is waterlogged, it needs replacement—learn more in our pressure tank troubleshooting guide.

Step 4: Listen for the Pump

With the power on and someone running water inside, go outside and listen near your wellhead or pressure tank area:

What You Might Hear

  • Nothing at all: Pump isn't getting power or motor is dead
  • Humming but not running: Capacitor may be bad or motor is seized
  • Clicking from pressure switch: Switch is trying to start pump but pump isn't responding
  • Normal pump sounds: Pump is working—problem is elsewhere

Submersible Pump Note

If you have a submersible pump (most common in San Diego County), you won't hear the pump itself—it's hundreds of feet underground. You're listening for the click of the pressure switch and checking if water flows when the switch engages.

Step 5: Check for Leaks

A leak in your plumbing system can cause pump problems by:

  • Making the pump run constantly (it can never build pressure)
  • Causing the pump to short-cycle (pressure drops quickly after it stops)
  • Eventually burning out the pump motor

Common Leak Locations

  • Toilets: A running toilet is a very common culprit—check all toilets by adding food coloring to the tank
  • Irrigation systems: Broken sprinkler heads or leaky valves
  • Water heater: Check around and underneath
  • Outdoor faucets: Hose bibs and connections
  • Underground pipes: Look for unexplained wet spots in your yard

Common Causes When Well Pump Is Not Working

Based on our 30+ years of experience servicing wells in San Diego County, here are the most common causes of pump failure, ranked by frequency:

1

Pressure Switch Failure

Contacts wear out, ants get inside, or corrosion builds up. Usually a $150-$300 fix.

2

Waterlogged Pressure Tank

Bladder fails, tank fills with water, pump short-cycles. Tank replacement costs $400-$1,200.

3

Capacitor Failure

Pump hums but won't start. The capacitor provides starting boost. Usually $100-$200 to replace.

4

Control Box Problems

The control box manages the submersible pump. Lightning damage is common. $200-$400 to repair/replace.

5

Pump Motor Failure

After 10-15 years, motors wear out. Pump replacement costs $1,500-$3,500+ depending on depth.

6

Well Running Dry

Especially during drought conditions. Pump runs but produces no water. May need well deepening.

When to Call a Professional

You should call a well service professional when:

  • The breaker keeps tripping (electrical fault)
  • You smell burning or see smoke (fire hazard)
  • Basic troubleshooting doesn't solve the problem
  • You have no water and need it restored quickly
  • You're uncomfortable working with electrical systems
  • The pump needs to be pulled from the well (specialized equipment required)

Preventing Future Pump Problems

Once your pump is working again, take steps to prevent future failures:

  • Install surge protection: Power surges are a leading cause of pump and control box damage
  • Schedule annual inspections: Catch problems early before they become emergencies
  • Monitor your system: Know your normal pressure range so you spot changes quickly
  • Address issues promptly: Small problems become big problems when ignored
  • Know your pump's age: Plan for replacement before it fails (10-15 year lifespan)

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well pump not working?

Common reasons include: tripped circuit breaker, failed pressure switch, blown capacitor, failed pump motor, waterlogged pressure tank, control box problems, or the well running dry. Start by checking the breaker and pressure switch—they're the most common causes.

What should I check first if my well pump stops working?

First, check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker. Reset it once if tripped. Next, check the pressure switch for burnt contacts or debris. Look at your pressure gauge—if it shows zero, the pump isn't running.

Why does my well pump breaker keep tripping?

A repeatedly tripping breaker indicates an electrical problem—do not keep resetting it. Causes include a failing pump motor, water in the motor, damaged wiring, or a short circuit. This requires professional diagnosis.

Can I fix my well pump myself?

Some issues are safe DIY fixes: resetting a breaker, replacing a pressure switch, or checking for leaks. However, submersible pump replacement requires special equipment—the pump is typically 200-600 feet underground. Electrical troubleshooting beyond basics should be left to professionals.

How do I know if my well pump needs to be replaced?

Signs you need replacement: pump over 10-15 years old, motor won't run with power confirmed, reduced output not caused by other issues, excessive noise or vibration, and repeated electrical failures.

Why is my well pump running but no water coming out?

If the pump runs but produces no water: the well may have run dry, pump impellers may be worn, there may be a drop pipe leak, or the check valve may have failed. A professional can diagnose which issue is causing the problem.

How much does it cost to fix a well pump that's not working?

Costs depend on the problem: pressure switch $150-$300, capacitor $100-$200, control box $200-$400, pump replacement $1,500-$3,500+. A diagnostic call typically costs $75-$150.
