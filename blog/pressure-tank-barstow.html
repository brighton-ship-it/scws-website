Pressure Tank Service for Barstow Properties

Barstow sits in the Mojave Desert at 2,100 feet elevation, where private wells supply most properties outside city limits. The local geology — primarily Mojave River Basin alluvium over crystalline basement rock — creates unique conditions for well systems. Wells here typically range from 200 to 600 feet deep, drawing from ancient aquifers recharged by rare desert storms and underground flow from the San Bernardino Mountains.

Pressure tanks in Barstow face extreme temperature swings (20°F winter nights to 110°F+ summer days), high total dissolved solids (TDS), and mineral-rich water that accelerates wear. Understanding these conditions matters when diagnosing problems and selecting replacement equipment.

Understanding Your Pressure Tank

A pressure tank stores pressurized water so your pump doesn't run every time you turn on a faucet. Inside, a rubber bladder separates water from air. As water enters, it compresses the air, creating pressure. When you open a tap, stored pressure pushes water out — no pump needed until the tank drains down to its cut-in pressure (typically 40 PSI).

Typical pressure settings: Cut-in 40 PSI, cut-off 60 PSI. Your pump runs for 30-90 seconds to refill the tank, then shuts off. This cycling should happen every few hours of normal use — not every few minutes.

Common Pressure Tank Problems in Barstow

Based on service history in San Bernardino County's high desert, here are the issues we see most:

1. Waterlogged Tank (Bladder Failure)

Symptom: Pump runs every 15-30 seconds, even with minimal water use. No air cushion left in tank.

Cause: The bladder inside tears or deteriorates (common after 7-10 years in Barstow's heat). Water fills the entire tank, eliminating the air cushion that creates usable storage.

Solution: Replace tank. Repair isn't practical — bladders aren't serviceable in modern tanks.

2. Low Air Pressure

Symptom: Pump cycles frequently, but not as extreme as waterlogged tank. Pressure gauge swings widely.

Cause: Air charge leaks out over time through the valve stem or bladder permeation (heat accelerates this).

Solution: Recharge air to 2 PSI below cut-in pressure (38 PSI if your cut-in is 40). Use a standard tire pump on the Schrader valve at the top of the tank. Turn off pump and drain tank first — recharging under water pressure can rupture the bladder.

3. Sediment Buildup

Symptom: Reduced flow, brown/gritty water from taps, tank feels sluggish.

Cause: Mojave Basin wells often produce fine sand or silt. Without proper filtration, sediment settles in the tank and pipes.

Solution: Install a sediment filter (5-10 micron) before the tank. Replace existing tank if heavily contaminated — sediment inside can't be flushed out in bladder-type tanks.

4. Scale/Mineral Coating

Symptom: Hard water deposits on fixtures, reduced tank performance, crusty buildup on fittings.

Cause: High TDS and mineral content (common in Mojave aquifers) deposits calcium/magnesium inside tank and pipes.

Solution: Install water softener for whole-house treatment. Pressure tanks themselves can't be descaled internally — replace if severely affected.

5. Pressure Switch Failure

Symptom: Pump won't turn on/off at correct pressures, or won't turn on at all.

Cause: Mineral deposits clog the pressure port, or contacts corrode (desert dust accelerates this).

Solution: Clean or replace switch. Switches cost $20-50; replacement takes 15 minutes for an experienced tech.

Rule of thumb: Tank should hold 3x your pump's per-minute flow rate in usable capacity (drawdown). A 60-gallon tank at 40/60 PSI only delivers ~18 gallons of usable water before the pump kicks on.

Installation and Replacement Process

Replacing a pressure tank is straightforward for a licensed contractor but involves risks (water damage, electrical) if done wrong.

Steps:

  1. Shut off pump at breaker (240V can kill).
  2. Drain tank via hose bib or drain valve.
  3. Disconnect plumbing — unions or threaded connections.
  4. Remove old tank and inspect mounting area for corrosion.
  5. Install new tank — use thread sealant (not tape) on metal NPT fittings.
  6. Reconnect plumbing and pressure switch (if moved).
  7. Charge air to 2 PSI below cut-in (38 PSI for 40/60 system).
  8. Test system — open taps, watch for leaks, verify pressure cycles.

Labor time: 1-2 hours for a standard replacement. Add time if plumbing needs updating or if the tank location is difficult (crawl space, tight utility room).

Preventive Maintenance for Desert Conditions

Barstow's extreme climate shortens equipment life. Regular maintenance extends it:

  • Check air pressure quarterly — heat causes air loss. Recharge as needed.
  • Inspect for leaks — fittings, valve stems, pressure relief valves.
  • Test pressure switch annually — clean contacts, adjust if needed.
  • Flush sediment filter monthly (if installed) to prevent clogs.
  • Listen for short cycling — if pump runs every few minutes, diagnose immediately to prevent pump burnout.
  • Drain tank fully once a year — removes any sediment that snuck past filters.

When to Replace vs. Repair

Replace if:

  • Tank is waterlogged (bladder failure)
  • Tank is 10+ years old
  • Visible rust or corrosion on tank body
  • Tank undersized for your pump
  • Repeated failures (chronic issues)

Repair if:

  • Tank just needs air recharge
  • Pressure switch failed but tank is fine
  • Simple plumbing leak at a fitting
  • Tank is less than 5 years old with minor issue

Bladder replacement isn't an option — modern tanks are sealed units.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do pressure tanks last in Barstow?

Expect 7-12 years. Barstow's extreme heat, mineral-rich water, and temperature swings shorten lifespan compared to milder climates. Quality brands (Amtrol, Goulds) last longer than budget models.

Can I install a pressure tank myself?

Technically yes if you're experienced with plumbing and electrical. However, mistakes risk water damage, pump burnout, or electrical hazards (240V pumps). A licensed contractor carries insurance and guarantees proper installation.

What size pressure tank do I need?

Depends on your pump's flow rate. A 1 HP pump (12-15 GPM) needs a 60-80 gallon tank minimum. Undersized tanks cause short cycling and premature pump failure.

Why is my pump running every 30 seconds?

Either your tank is waterlogged (bladder failed) or has lost its air charge. Check air pressure first — it's a cheap fix. If no air holds, the bladder is torn and the tank needs replacement.

How much does a new pressure tank cost?

Installed: $400-1,200 depending on size. A 60-gallon Amtrol costs around $350 for the tank plus $200-400 labor.

Can a pressure tank explode?

Extremely rare with modern tanks. Pressure relief valves prevent over-pressurization. Old tanks without relief valves (pre-1990s) can rupture if the pressure switch fails, but this is nearly impossible with current equipment.

What air pressure should my tank have?

2 PSI below your cut-in pressure. If your system is 40/60 (cut-in 40 PSI, cut-off 60 PSI), set air to 38 PSI. Check with pump OFF and tank drained.

Do I need a sediment filter before my pressure tank?

Yes, if your well produces sand or silt (common in Mojave Basin alluvial aquifers). A 5-10 micron filter protects the tank, pump, and fixtures from abrasive particles.

How often should I check my pressure tank?

Quarterly in Barstow's climate. Check air pressure, inspect for leaks, test pressure switch operation. Takes 10 minutes and can catch problems early.

Will a bigger tank reduce my electric bill?

Slightly. A larger tank reduces pump cycle frequency, which saves a few dollars per month. The main benefit is longer pump life — frequent cycling (short-cycling) kills pumps fast.
