What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Jurupa Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

How Hydrofracturing Boosts Yield

Hydrofracturing injects clean water at high pressure into your existing well to clean and widen the natural fractures in the surrounding rock. A technician isolates a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer and pumps water against it, often between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. The pressure clears silt and mineral scale from the existing cracks and connects the well to fractures that had grown too tight to deliver water. In the fractured rock of the Jurupa Mountains and the consolidated material along the foothills, opening those pathways is what lifts the flow rate.

The process uses only clean water. No chemicals are involved, keeping it entirely separate from oil-field fracking.

Why Jurupa Valley Wells Decline

On the rural edges of Jurupa Valley, low yield usually comes from:

  • Sediment and grit infiltration. Fine material from the alluvial plains and weathered foothill rock works into the screen and fractures, gradually restricting flow.
  • Drought drawdown. Western Riverside County rides through dry multi-year cycles, and as the regional water table falls a marginal well runs short in summer.
  • An underperforming new well. Drilling toward the foothills can miss the best fracture zones; hydrofracturing such a well is a routine remedy.
  • Mineral scaling. Hard local groundwater leaves deposits that slowly narrow the flow paths over the years.

Domestic wells in the foothill areas around Jurupa Valley commonly run 250 to 700 feet deep, deeper as the ground rises toward the Jurupa Mountains.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Note when you run short. Shortages only during heavy simultaneous use indicate a yield-and-recovery problem — hydrofracturing's specialty.
  • Watch the pressure tank. Quick on-off cycling usually means a waterlogged tank or a failing pressure switch, both cheap repairs.
  • Notice a pump that never stops. Continuous running without building pressure can mean the well is drawn down faster than it recovers.
  • Check the taps for air. Sputtering faucets suggest the water level is reaching the pump intake during use.
  • Locate your well log. Depth, static level, and the original tested yield give you a baseline against today's performance.

When to Call a Professional

If recovery has slowed, the well runs dry under normal demand, or output has fallen well below the original log, schedule a professional flow test. Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure equipment and an experienced operator, so it is not a homeowner project. A licensed contractor baselines your yield, inspects the casing with a camera, and confirms your Jurupa Valley well is a candidate before any pressurized work begins.

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Jurupa Valley

A hydrofracturing treatment typically costs $3,000 to $8,000, depending on depth and the number of zones, with the $125 diagnostic credited toward the job if you proceed. A new turnkey well in this part of Riverside County generally runs $18,000 to $42,000 with drilling, casing, pump, and surface equipment included.

If the issue turns out to be equipment, the repairs are far cheaper: $2,500 to $5,500 for a pump, $600 to $1,500 for a tank, and $150 to $350 for a pressure switch. Because hydrofracturing costs a fraction of a new well and often restores 2 to 5 times the prior flow, most Jurupa Valley owners try it first.

When hydrofracturing restores yield on a Jurupa Valley well, simple upkeep makes the gain last. Are there still well-water homes in Jurupa Valley?

Yes. Much of the city is on municipal water, but rural and large-lot properties — especially toward the Jurupa Mountains and the older Mira Loma and Glen Avon parcels — still rely on private wells. Wells reaching into the fractured foothill rock benefit most from hydrofracturing.

How much does hydrofracturing cost in Jurupa Valley?

Most treatments run $3,000 to $8,000 depending on depth and zones treated, far below the $18,000 to $42,000 cost of a new well. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward the work.

Will it work on a foothill well?

Wells drawing from fractured rock near the Jurupa Mountains respond best. Wells set in loose alluvium are weaker candidates, so we run a flow test and inspect the casing before recommending the work.

Does the process use chemicals?

No. It injects only clean water at high pressure and adds nothing to your aquifer. It shares a name with oil-field fracking but is a completely different, chemical-free process.

How long does it take?

A typical treatment takes 4 to 8 hours on site, and we usually verify the improvement within 24 to 48 hours by re-testing the well's flow.

How much can my yield improve?

In good fractured rock, owners commonly see 2 to 5 times their previous flow. We measure your yield before and after so the improvement is documented rather than guessed.
