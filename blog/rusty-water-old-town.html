What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Old Town well.

Rusty Well Water in Old Town San Diego: Causes and Cures

Old Town is the historic birthplace of San Diego, tucked into the valley where the San Diego River bends toward Mission Bay, surrounded by the slopes of Presidio Hill and the older neighborhoods that climb away from the riverbed. Most of Old Town runs on city water, but the river valley and the rural and unincorporated parcels of San Diego County beyond it still rely on private wells. When that well water comes up rust-orange, paints brown rings in the toilet, or leaves a metallic taste on the tongue, the explanation is nearly always iron, pulled either from the river-valley groundwater or from corroding metal in an aging well and its plumbing.

Southern California Well Service has chased down discolored water across San Diego County since 2020. The valley sediments and the surrounding granitic hillsides that feed Old Town-area wells are naturally iron-bearing, and many of the older properties in this historic district carry plumbing and well infrastructure that has had a very long time to corrode. Sorting out which of those is responsible is the first and most important step toward clear, clean water.

Why Iron Turns Your Water Rusty

Iron is one of the most common elements in the ground beneath San Diego, and it dissolves readily into groundwater as that water moves through soil and weathered rock. Underground, this iron is in a clear, ferrous state and is completely invisible. The moment it reaches the oxygen at your faucet, however, it oxidizes into the solid orange particles we recognize as rust. That is why a glass of seemingly clear well water can develop an orange tint after sitting out. In the Old Town area, the rusty water we diagnose generally comes from one of these sources: dissolved ferrous iron straight from the aquifer; already-oxidized ferric iron forming inside the well or pipes; manganese, which produces darker black or purple staining and often travels with iron; iron bacteria, the organisms responsible for the reddish slime that builds up in toilet tanks; and plain old corrosion of steel casings, drop pipes, and galvanized household plumbing.

Because Old Town is one of San Diego's oldest districts, corrosion looms large among the causes we find. Decades-old galvanized service lines rust from the inside until the whole house runs orange, and aging water heaters shed rust into the hot water while the cold stays clear. On the river-valley parcels, seasonal swings in the water table can stir iron-rich sediment into the well during dry stretches, changing the water's color seemingly out of nowhere.

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

The EPA classifies iron and manganese as secondary, aesthetic contaminants, which means they are evaluated on taste, color, and odor rather than acute health risk. Iron at the concentrations typical of San Diego wells will not make a healthy person sick, but it is far from harmless to your home: it stains laundry and porcelain permanently, gives water a metallic flavor, scales up pipes and water heaters, and provides food for the iron bacteria that clog plumbing. Manganese deserves a closer look, since elevated levels carry genuine health questions. A simple water test is the only way to know your real numbers and choose the right treatment.

What to Check Before You Call

A little observation goes a long way toward a fast, accurate diagnosis. Notice whether the rust appears in hot water only, which usually implicates the water heater rather than the well. Check whether the problem is whole-house or limited to one fixture, since a single rusty tap typically means a localized pipe. Look for reddish slime in the toilet tank, the signature of iron bacteria. Note the timing, because rust that shows up only when the pump first kicks on often means sediment settling in the pressure tank. And think back to any recent changes, such as a dry spell, heavy rain, or recent pump work, all of which can point us straight to the cause.

Solutions That Restore Clear Water

Iron and Manganese Filtration

For dissolved iron and manganese coming from the aquifer, a backwashing oxidizing filter is the proven workhorse. We size it to your measured iron load so it keeps pace without wasting water, and for lower iron combined with hardness, a properly sized softener may also do the job.

Shock Chlorination and Well Cleaning

When iron bacteria are producing slime, filtration alone will not fix it. Shock chlorination disinfects the well, pump, and plumbing to destroy the colony, after which we set a simple maintenance schedule to prevent its return.

Casing and Plumbing Repairs

Where a corroded steel casing is shedding rust, lining or replacing it stops the contamination at the source. Inside an older Old Town home, replacing galvanized pipe with PEX or copper eliminates household rust for good.

Sediment Pre-Filtration

An inexpensive sediment filter ahead of any treatment system catches particulate rust and protects the more costly equipment downstream.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Old Town well water turn orange after it sits out?

That is dissolved ferrous iron, which stays clear underground and only oxidizes into orange rust when it meets air. An oxidizing iron filter is the standard remedy.

Is iron in my San Diego well water a health hazard?

Iron is an aesthetic contaminant, so it is not an acute health risk at typical levels, though it stains and tastes metallic. We test manganese at the same time since that one warrants closer attention.

Why is only my hot water rusty?

That usually means the water heater, not the well. A corroding anode rod or rusting tank discolors hot water while the cold stays clear; servicing or replacing it normally fixes it.

My Old Town home is old. Could the pipes be the cause?

Very possibly. Decades-old galvanized plumbing rusts from the inside and can turn the whole house orange. Replacing it with PEX or copper eliminates that source of rust.

Do you charge for water testing?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and we credit it toward any system or repair we install. It measures iron and manganese so treatment is sized correctly.

Will a water softener remove my rusty water?

A softener can handle modest iron along with hardness, but higher iron loads call for a dedicated oxidizing filter. We test your Old Town water to recommend the right system.
