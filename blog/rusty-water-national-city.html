What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your National City well.

Why Well Water Turns Rusty Around National City

Rust is simply iron made visible. San Diego County's decomposed-granite aquifers naturally carry iron and manganese, and there are four main pathways that bring that color to your tap.

Dissolved Iron Coming Out of the Aquifer

Underground, where oxygen is scarce, iron stays dissolved and invisible as ferrous iron. The instant your water meets air, it oxidizes into rust-colored ferric particles. That is why a glass that looks clear at first can turn orange as it sits. Manganese behaves the same way but stains black or purple-brown. National City-area wells drilled into granite formations frequently carry enough of both to leave persistent stains on sinks and laundry.

Iron Bacteria

Iron bacteria are harmless-to-drink but troublesome organisms that feed on iron in groundwater and leave behind a rusty, slimy sludge plus a marshy odor. They clog screens and pipe interiors and are one of the most common reasons South Bay well water turns intermittently orange or develops gel-like deposits in toilet tanks.

Corroding Steel Casing

Older National City-area wells often used steel casing. After a few decades underground, steel corrodes and sheds rust flakes straight into the water. If your rust appears mainly when the pump first starts, an aging casing is a strong candidate.

Household Pipes and Water Heater

Sometimes the rust never touches your well at all. Galvanized steel plumbing in older South Bay homes corrodes internally, and a water heater with a worn anode rod or rusting tank discolors only the hot water. A simple check separates a well problem from a plumbing problem.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Hot vs. cold: If only hot water is rusty, suspect the water heater. If both are rusty, the source is the well or main line.
  • The jar test: Cloudy-orange immediately means oxidized iron; clear-then-orange as it sits means dissolved iron from the aquifer.
  • Timing: Rust that appears at pump start and clears suggests casing or sediment; constant rust suggests dissolved iron.
  • Odor and texture: A swampy smell or slimy film points to iron bacteria.
  • Stain color: Reddish-brown is iron; black specks are manganese.

Jot down what you observe so our technician can zero in faster.

Call a licensed C-57 well contractor when rusty water is constant rather than occasional, when it comes with falling pressure, when you notice an iron-bacteria odor, when grit or sand accompanies the color, or when your well is more than 25 years old and may have a failing casing. How Iron Damages Your Plumbing and Fixtures Over Time

Beyond the obvious staining, untreated iron and manganese slowly build up inside pipes, water heaters, dishwashers, and irrigation lines. Iron bacteria accelerate this by producing slime that traps minerals and narrows the inside diameter of your plumbing year after year. Left unaddressed, that buildup reduces flow, shortens the life of appliances, and can eventually restrict your well's delivery enough that it mimics a failing pump. Catching a rust problem early in National City almost always costs far less than waiting until fixtures are ruined and pressure has dropped.

Could My Rusty Water Signal a Bigger Well Problem?

Sometimes rust is the first visible symptom of something larger, such as a casing that is corroding through, a screen that is failing and letting sediment in, or a water table that has dropped enough during San Diego County's dry years that the pump is drawing from a more mineral-laden zone. That is why we never treat color in isolation. When we come out to a National City-area well, we look at the whole system, the pump, the pressure tank, the casing age, and the recent yield, so that the rusty water you called about does not turn into a no-water emergency a few months later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rusty well water safe to drink in National City?

Iron and manganese at typical concentrations are aesthetic concerns under the EPA's secondary standards rather than acute health hazards, but rusty water can carry iron bacteria and mask other issues. A full water test is the only way to be sure.

Why does only my hot water look rusty?

Clear cold water with rusty hot water almost always means your water heater, usually a corroded anode rod or rusting tank, rather than the well.

Can a water softener fix rusty water?

A softener handles only light dissolved-iron loads and quickly fouls if iron bacteria are present. Most South Bay wells with real iron problems need a dedicated oxidizing filter, sometimes paired with a softener.

My water is rusty only sometimes. Why?

Intermittent rust usually points to iron bacteria or sediment stirred up during pump cycling. Testing and a casing inspection reveal which.

How long does iron-bacteria treatment last?

Shock chlorination often controls iron bacteria for a long period; severe or recurring cases may need a continuous treatment system. We recommend the least invasive effective option.
