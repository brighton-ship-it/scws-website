The Sweetwater Valley Groundwater Setting

Bonita's wells draw from the alluvial deposits along the Sweetwater River corridor, a shallower and more variable setting than the deep fractured-rock wells of the backcountry. That alluvial geology means water levels and quality can shift with the seasons and with reservoir operations upstream. Many valley wells are older irrigation borings that have run for decades, so age-related failures — tired pumps, brittle wiring, worn pressure tanks — are common. Understanding this local setting helps us diagnose Bonita problems quickly and recommend the right fix. It also shapes water chemistry: valley wells can run harder or carry more sediment than customers expect, which is why a softener or sediment filter is sometimes part of a lasting solution rather than just a bandage on the immediate failure.

Common Urgent Failures on Bonita Wells

  • Burned-out submersible pump — Older valley pumps eventually fail, often during a summer irrigation demand spike. Replacement runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower — Bonita's shallower alluvial wells often land at the lower end.
  • Failed pressure switch — Worn contacts leave the pump unable to start or stop. A quick, affordable fix at $150 to $350.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank — A failed bladder short-cycles the pump and can burn it out. Tank replacement runs $600 to $1,500 and protects the pump.
  • Tripped breaker or damaged control box — Aging irrigation-well wiring and surges knock out control boxes and capacitors; repairs run $400 to $900.
  • Irrigation-system failure during heat — A dead well in peak summer threatens pastures, horse paddocks, and mature landscaping within days. We respond same-day whenever we can.
  • Sediment surges — Alluvial wells can pull sand that clogs fixtures and drip systems. Emergency sediment filtration runs $300 to $900.

What to Check Before We Arrive

While you wait, safely check the well pump breaker and reset it once; if it trips again, leave it off and tell us. Look at the pressure gauge — no pressure with the pump running points to the pump or a low water level, while a rapidly bouncing needle points to a failed tank. For an irrigation well, confirm the controller and valves are actually calling for water and nothing simply switched off. Do not open the well cap or attempt to pull the pump yourself. If you smell anything burning near the controls, shut the breaker off and call.

Don't Wait for Total Failure

Most Bonita well emergencies announce themselves first. Watch for slowly weakening pressure, a pump running longer than usual, air spitting from a hose bib, sand in the water, unusual noises, or a rising electric bill. On the valley's older irrigation systems, these signs often precede a burned-out pump by weeks. Addressing them on your schedule is far cheaper than an emergency call and protects the landscaping and animals that depend on the well. If you notice any of these, book a maintenance check before the well quits entirely.
