Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your North Park well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

What Makes Avocado Irrigation Different

A backyard well serves a house in short bursts. An avocado grove well runs for hours at a time through the warm season, and the whole system must be built for that duty:

  • Submersible pump sized for sustained gallons-per-minute, set at the right depth in a casing that commonly reaches 200 to 600 feet in San Diego County foothills.
  • Pressure tank and switch that cycle far more often than a household's and therefore wear out sooner.
  • Storage tank and booster or constant-pressure system to hold water and deliver even pressure across every drip line.
  • Sediment filtration and water treatment to protect emitters and keep chloride, iron, and manganese in check.
  • Drip lines and micro-sprinklers matched to tree age and the slope of the ground.

Common Well Issues in the North Park Service Area

The problems that bring San Diego County growers to us tend to repeat:

  • Mineral scale from hard, granite-derived groundwater building up on pumps, tanks, and emitters.
  • Chloride sensitivity: avocados show leaf-tip burn at chloride levels other crops tolerate, and aging wells in dry years often see chloride climb.
  • Drought-driven yield loss as the regional water table drops.
  • Short-cycling from worn pressure switches that overheat and kill pump motors.
  • Sediment migration of fine decomposed granite that plugs filters and starves the drip system.

Steps a Grower Can Take Before Calling

  1. Watch the pressure tank and switch for rapid clicking or constant cycling.
  2. Reset the breaker once; a repeat trip means an electrical or motor fault, not a nuisance trip.
  3. Compare a handful of emitters across the block to tell a filter problem from a well problem.
  4. Note any change in the water's color, smell, or grit content.
  5. Inspect the trees for midday wilt, tip burn, or premature fruit drop.

Record what you saw and when it began so we can arrive prepared.

Why Water Testing Comes First for Avocado Growers

Before recommending any treatment hardware, we start with data. A proper irrigation-water test for an avocado grove looks at chloride, sodium, total dissolved solids, hardness, iron, manganese, pH, and the sodium adsorption ratio. Each of these tells us something different. Chloride and sodium drive the leaf-tip burn that avocados are notorious for. Hardness and iron predict how quickly scale and staining will foul pumps and emitters. The sodium adsorption ratio warns whether the irrigation water will degrade soil structure over time, slowly choking the root zone even when the trees look fine for a season or two.

With those numbers in hand, the right fix becomes obvious rather than a guess. Sometimes the answer is simple blending with a cleaner source, sometimes it is leaching irrigation to flush accumulated salts below the root zone, and sometimes it is a softener or dedicated treatment system. Just as often the test shows the water is fine and the real problem is mechanical, which saves a grower from buying treatment they do not need. We would rather tell a North Park-area grower the truth and earn the next call than sell hardware that solves nothing. That habit, built over more than 30 years in San Diego County, is why so much of our work comes from referrals among neighboring growers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does North Park really have an agricultural history?

Yes. In the 1890s the land northeast of Balboa Park that became North Park was purchased specifically to plant a lemon grove, and citrus and avocado growing were part of this area of San Diego County for decades. Where growers in and around the North Park area still keep groves on private wells, that tradition continues.

How deep are avocado wells in this part of San Diego County?

Foothill wells in San Diego County commonly run 200 to 600 feet deep through decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, while valley-bottom wells in alluvial soil can be shallower. The right depth depends on where the productive water-bearing fractures sit under your specific parcel.

What is the first sign my well water is hurting my avocados?

Leaf-tip burn is usually the first sign of chloride or salt stress, which avocados are unusually sensitive to. Midday wilting and premature fruit drop follow. We test North Park-area well water for chloride and other avocado-critical parameters and recommend blending, leaching, or treatment.

Why does my pump keep short-cycling?

Short-cycling, where the pump turns on and off rapidly, is most often a failing pressure switch or a waterlogged pressure tank. It overheats and destroys pump motors if ignored. A pressure switch runs $150 to $350 and a tank $600 to $1,500; our $125 diagnostic pinpoints which it is and is credited toward the repair.

Yes. As a licensed C-57 water well drilling contractor we drill new turnkey wells ($18,000-$42,000), hydrofracture low producers ($3,000-$8,000), repair pumps and pressure systems, install water treatment, and decommission old wells, all from one company.
