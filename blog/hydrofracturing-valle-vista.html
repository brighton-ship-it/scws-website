What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Valle Vista Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Valle Vista Is Strong Country for Hydrofracturing

Valle Vista is an unincorporated community in Riverside County, tucked just east of Hemet along State Route 74 as the road climbs toward the foothills of the San Jacinto Mountains. It is a semi-rural place—older homes, scattered groves, and properties that back right up against the granite foothills at the mountain front. That setting is good news if your well has been slowing down, because Valle Vista is genuinely well-suited to hydrofracturing. The wells here are typically finished in fractured granitic rock at the base of the mountains, and fractured rock is exactly what this technique is designed to improve.

That is not a sales line; it is geology. As you move east out of the Hemet basin into Valle Vista, the loose valley sediments give way to hard crystalline rock and decomposed granite. Water in this terrain travels through cracks and fracture networks in the rock, and over years those fractures fill with silt and mineral scale, slowly strangling a well's output. Hydrofracturing reverses that. Of the four communities we serve in this corridor, Valle Vista is one of the most reliable areas for a successful treatment.

How the Process Works

Hydrofracturing—also spelled hydrofracking—rejuvenates a rock well using clean water and pressure, nothing else. We lower inflatable packers into the well and inflate them to isolate a section of the borehole. Then we pump clean water into that isolated zone at high pressure, usually 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. The pressure flushes accumulated silt and scale out of the natural fractures and works them open wider, reconnecting the well to water-bearing cracks that had been choked off. The whole treatment generally runs four to eight hours, and because it uses only water, there is nothing introduced into your aquifer.

It is worth saying clearly that this bears no relation to oil-and-gas fracking. There is no chemical mixture and no sand proppant—just water opening the fractures that are already there in the granite beneath Valle Vista.

Why Valle Vista Wells Respond Well

The honest version of the applicability question is short here: most Valle Vista wells are good candidates because most are finished in fractured granite at the mountain front. Still, it is worth confirming your own well rather than assuming, and it's easy to do:

  • Check the driller's log (well completion report). If it describes "granite," "decomposed granite," "fractured rock," or "bedrock"—which is typical out here—your well is a classic candidate. The log is filed with the state and is often in your home-purchase documents.
  • Confirm the construction. Foothill wells like these are usually cased through the soil and then open-hole in solid rock below. That open rock section is what we treat. Wells screened entirely in loose sand and gravel (uncommon in Valle Vista proper, more typical down in the Hemet flats) would not benefit.
  • Watch the recovery behavior. Fractured-rock wells often pump down and then recover slowly, producing a modest steady flow. That signature is normal for the formation and a good fit for treatment.
  • Camera survey when needed. If your well's records are missing, a downhole video shows the open rock face and visible fractures so we can target the best zones.

Even in a favorable area, we still run the diagnosis. If a particular Valle Vista well turned out to be sitting in sediment rather than rock, we would tell you and recommend something else. But that is the exception here, not the rule.

Protecting Your Investment After Treatment

Once a Valle Vista well is producing well again, a little follow-through keeps it that way. Fractures in granite re-accumulate fine sediment and mineral scale over time, so periodic flow checks—comparing your gallons-per-minute against the baseline we recorded—catch a slow decline before it becomes a crisis. Matching your pump to the well's true sustainable yield matters too; an oversized pump can draw a fractured-rock well down faster than it recovers, which stresses both the pump and the aquifer. Many foothill homeowners here also add a modest storage tank so the well can refill steadily through the day rather than being asked to deliver peak demand all at once. None of this is exotic, and it stretches the years between treatments considerably. We're glad to walk you through a simple maintenance rhythm that fits how your household actually uses water.

It also helps to keep your well records together in one place—the original driller's log, any pump replacement paperwork, and the before-and-after flow numbers from each service visit. In foothill communities like Valle Vista, where wells change hands with the property, that history is genuinely valuable: it tells the next owner, and any contractor, exactly what the well is and how it has behaved over the years.

What to Expect From Start to Finish

We start with a pre-treatment flow test to record your baseline yield. Where it helps, a camera survey checks the casing and identifies the fracture zones to target. We then isolate those zones with packers and bring pressure up in stages, watching the formation accept water. After treatment we re-test the flow—usually confirming the improvement within 24 to 48 hours—and provide a documented before-and-after comparison so you can see the gain. In sound fractured-rock wells like those common in Valle Vista, a 2x to 5x improvement is a realistic outcome, though every well differs and we won't promise an exact number.
