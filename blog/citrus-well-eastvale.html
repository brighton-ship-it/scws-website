Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

Citrus Well Service for Eastvale Groves in Riverside County

Eastvale sits in the northwest corner of Riverside County, on the flat alluvial floor of the Chino Basin near the Santa Ana River, with Norco and Corona just to the south. For most of the twentieth century this was open dairy land, and that agricultural heritage still shapes the soils and water table beneath today's groves. The deep, well-drained alluvial deposits that once supported pasture and feed crops are well suited to citrus, and a number of Eastvale property owners now keep orange, lemon, and mandarin trees on parcels that range from a few backyard rows to several planted acres. What ties all of them together is the same thing every grower in the region depends on: a private well that delivers clean, reliable water on demand. At Southern California Well Service, we have spent more than 30 years drilling, equipping, and maintaining agricultural wells across Riverside County, and we understand exactly what an Eastvale citrus operation needs from its water system.

Citrus is unforgiving when water is interrupted. A mature grove that misses irrigation during a July heat spell can drop fruit, stress its canopy, and lose a season of production. That is why we treat well service for citrus as a specialty rather than an afterthought. As a licensed C-57 water well contractor with a 4.9-star reputation, we design systems sized to the real demand of a grove, not a generic residential rule of thumb.

How Much Water a Citrus Grove Actually Needs

Water demand for citrus is larger and steadier than most new growers expect. A mature, full-canopy grove in inland Riverside County typically uses on the order of 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the heaviest draw concentrated in the long, hot stretch from late spring through early fall. To put that in well terms, sustaining a few acres of producing trees through peak summer evapotranspiration can call for a sustained delivery of many gallons per minute, hour after hour, day after day.

The other demand that catches growers off guard is frost protection. When a cold snap threatens an Eastvale grove on a clear winter night, sprinkler-based frost protection can require very high instantaneous flow, often in the range of 50 or more gallons per minute per acre, all running at once for several hours before dawn. A well and pump sized comfortably for summer irrigation may still be undersized for a hard freeze if frost protection runs off the same system. We plan for both scenarios so that the night you need water most is not the night your system runs dry.

Matching Flow to Your Grove

Before recommending equipment, we look at planted acreage, tree age and density, your irrigation method, and whether you intend to use the well for frost protection. Drip and micro-sprinkler systems deliver water efficiently to the root zone and lower total demand compared with flood irrigation, but they place a premium on consistent pressure and clean water. We size the well yield and pump to your peak day, then build in headroom.

Well, Pump, and Storage Sizing for Citrus

An agricultural citrus well is a different animal from a household well. Three pieces have to work together: the well itself, the pump, and storage.

  • Well yield: The borehole and its screen need to produce enough sustained flow to meet peak demand without drawing the water level down past the pump. We evaluate this with a proper pumping test rather than guesswork.
  • High-capacity pump: Most producing groves are served by a high-capacity submersible pump, or in higher-volume settings a vertical lineshaft turbine. These are sized to the grove's gallons-per-minute requirement and the well's depth to water, then matched to the right motor and controls.
  • Storage: Ponds and storage tanks let a moderate-yield well bank water overnight so it can be released at high flow during the irrigation window or a frost event. Storage is often the most cost-effective way to meet a high peak demand without drilling for an unnecessarily large sustained yield.

For frost protection in particular, a storage pond paired with a booster pump is frequently the smartest design, because it lets you deliver the brief, intense flow a freeze demands without oversizing the well.

Water Quality for Citrus: Salinity, Chloride, and Boron

Citrus is one of the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so water chemistry matters as much as water quantity. Three concerns come up again and again in Riverside County wells:

  • Salinity (EC): Elevated total salts in irrigation water raise the salinity of the root zone over time, reducing water uptake and slowly stunting trees. Citrus tolerates less salinity than many crops, so even moderate readings deserve attention.
  • Chloride: Citrus is notably sensitive to chloride, which accumulates in leaves and shows up as marginal leaf burn and defoliation. The Chino Basin's history of intensive agriculture means chloride is worth testing for specifically.
  • Boron: Boron is an essential micronutrient at trace levels but becomes toxic to citrus at concentrations only modestly above that, with problems often appearing above roughly 0.5 to 1 part per million. There is a narrow margin between not enough and too much.

We pull water samples, interpret the lab results in the context of your specific trees, and, where needed, recommend treatment, blending, or leaching strategies to protect the grove. The right fix depends on your numbers, so we keep recommendations general until we have tested your actual well.

Drought, Groundwater, and SGMA

Southern California's climate swings between wet years and multi-year droughts, and groundwater is the buffer that carries growers through the dry stretches. Under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, basins across the state are managed for long-term sustainability, and growers who depend on wells benefit from understanding how their basin is governed and what that may mean for pumping over time. Practically, the most resilient Eastvale groves are the ones with efficient irrigation, healthy wells, and enough storage to ride out a hot, dry season without strain. We help growers position their water systems for that resilience.

Common Citrus Well Issues We See in Eastvale

  • Pumps undersized for peak summer demand, so the grove falls behind on water during the hottest weeks.
  • No frost-protection capacity, leaving trees exposed on cold winter nights.
  • Clogged drip emitters and micro-sprinklers from sediment or mineral scale.
  • Declining yield or sand in the water as a well ages and its screen fouls.
  • Pressure tank and control problems from mineral-rich groundwater.
  • Water chemistry drift that quietly damages trees before the grower connects it to the well.

Maintenance That Keeps a Grove Producing

The cheapest repair is the one you prevent. We recommend periodic pump and motor checks, annual water testing, inspection and flushing of filtration and emitters, and a pumping test every few years to track well yield before a slow decline becomes an emergency. Catching a failing pump or a fouling screen early is far less costly than losing irrigation in the middle of a heat wave.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when you notice falling pressure or flow, air or sand in the line, a pump that short-cycles or runs constantly, rising power bills tied to the pump, or any change in how your water looks, smells, or behaves. If you are planting a new grove or expanding an existing one, bring us in before you put trees in the ground so the water system is sized correctly from day one. Frequently Asked Questions

How many gallons per minute does my Eastvale citrus grove need?

It depends on planted acreage, tree maturity, irrigation method, and whether you run frost protection. Mature citrus uses roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre each year, concentrated in summer, and frost protection can demand 50 or more gallons per minute per acre. We size your system to your specific peak day.

Can one well handle both summer irrigation and winter frost protection?

Sometimes, but frost protection's brief, intense flow often exceeds what a well sized for irrigation can sustain. Pairing the well with a storage pond and booster pump is usually the most economical way to cover both needs.

Why is my citrus showing leaf burn even though I water regularly?

Marginal leaf burn frequently points to water chemistry rather than a lack of water. Chloride, salinity, or boron in the well water can accumulate in the trees. A water test tells us which one and guides treatment.

How deep are wells in the Eastvale area?

Depth varies across the Chino Basin and depends on your exact parcel, so we do not quote a fixed number. We evaluate local conditions and test before recommending a design.

How often should I have my agricultural well serviced?

Plan on annual water testing and pump checks, with a pumping test every few years to monitor yield. Regular maintenance catches small problems before they cost you a season of fruit.

Do you offer emergency service if my grove loses water?

Yes. We provide same-day emergency response across Riverside County so your trees are not left without water during a heat wave or pump failure.
