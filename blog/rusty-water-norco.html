Rusty Water from Your Norco Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Norco well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in Riverside County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Norco well.

How Norco Well Water Turns Rusty

Rust is iron that has met oxygen and changed color. The alluvial aquifer beneath Norco carries iron and manganese naturally, and that mineral reaches your taps and troughs by four main routes.

Dissolved Iron and Manganese

Down in the aquifer, where oxygen is scarce, iron dissolves invisibly as ferrous iron. The instant the water meets air in your pressure tank, trough, or glass, it oxidizes into rust-colored ferric particles. Manganese behaves the same way but stains black or purple-brown. The Temescal and Chino subbasin sediments under Norco often carry enough of both to leave persistent staining on fixtures and equipment.

Iron Bacteria

Iron bacteria are naturally occurring organisms that feed on iron and leave a rusty slime plus a marshy odor. They are not the kind of bacteria that make you ill the way coliform does, but they clog screens, pipe walls, trough valves, and irrigation lines, and they are a common reason Norco wells turn orange on and off or develop gel-like deposits.

Corroding Steel Casing

Many older Norco wells used steel casing. After decades in the ground, that steel corrodes and drops rust flakes straight into the water. If your rust appears mostly when the pump first starts, an aging casing is a strong suspect.

Pipes and Water Heater

Not all rust starts at the well. Galvanized steel plumbing in older Norco homes corrodes from the inside, and a water heater with a worn anode rod or rusting tank discolors only the hot water. A quick test separates a well problem from a household plumbing problem.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Hot vs. cold: Rusty hot water with clear cold water points to the water heater. Both rusty points to the well or main line.
  • The jar test: Cloudy-orange immediately is oxidized iron; clear-then-orange as it sits is dissolved iron from the aquifer.
  • Timing: Rust at startup that clears suggests casing or sediment; steady rust suggests dissolved iron.
  • Check the troughs and irrigation: Slime or orange buildup on valves and emitters is a strong iron-bacteria clue.
  • Stain color: Reddish-brown is iron; black specks are manganese.

Noting these details before we arrive shortens the diagnostic visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rusty well water safe for my horses and livestock?

Iron and manganese at typical concentrations are aesthetic rather than acutely toxic, and animals will often drink rusty water, but iron bacteria slime can foul troughs and automatic waterers, and rust can mask other contaminants. A water test tells you exactly what your animals are drinking.

Is rusty well water safe for my family to drink?

At common concentrations, iron and manganese are EPA secondary (aesthetic) concerns, not acute hazards, but we always recommend a full test before assuming household water is safe.

Why is only my hot water rusty?

Clear cold with rusty hot water almost always means the water heater, usually a corroded anode rod or rusting tank, not the well.

Will a water softener remove the rust?

Softeners handle only light dissolved iron and foul fast with iron bacteria. Most Norco wells need a dedicated oxidizing filter, sometimes paired with a softener.

Iron bacteria keep clogging my troughs. Can you stop it?

Shock chlorination knocks the colony back, and for heavy-use equestrian properties we can add continuous treatment to keep troughs and irrigation flowing. We recommend the least invasive option that actually works.

Can rusty water damage my irrigation system?

Yes. Iron deposits and iron-bacteria slime are notorious for plugging drip emitters, micro-sprinklers, and trough float valves across large Norco lots. Treating the water at the source protects the entire irrigation network, not just the house.
