Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Yield Wells in the Loma Linda Area

Loma Linda sits in the San Bernardino Valley in San Bernardino County, between Redlands and the city of San Bernardino, near the Santa Ana River and over the productive alluvial groundwater basins of the region, including the Bunker Hill basin. The valley floor here is filled with thick alluvial deposits, and the older Pleistocene-age alluvium is the principal water-bearing unit that supplies water to more than a thousand wells across the San Bernardino area. That makes Loma Linda's hydrogeology fundamentally different from the fractured-rock mountains nearby: there is generally abundant groundwater in the basin, so when a Loma Linda-area well runs as a low-flow or low-yield well, the problem is usually with the well itself, its screen, its depth, or its pump, rather than a lack of water underground. That is encouraging, because well-specific problems are often very fixable.

The first step, as always, is to measure what your well truly delivers rather than what the pressure tank makes it appear to deliver.

How to Test Your Well's Flow and Yield

Two numbers matter: the flow at your fixtures and the sustained yield of the aquifer. For a quick check, run an outdoor hydrant into a five-gallon bucket and time the fill. Twenty seconds is 15 GPM; one minute is 5 GPM; two minutes or more puts you at 2.5 GPM or less.

The measurement that counts is a sustained yield (drawdown) test. Because a pressure tank stores water and can briefly hide a weak well, we pump steadily, watch the water level fall, and time the recovery. A Loma Linda home generally needs 3 to 5 GPM of dependable supply, more with irrigation. Given the productive basin beneath the valley, a properly constructed and maintained well here often exceeds that comfortably, so a low number usually points to a treatable well or equipment issue rather than the aquifer.

It helps to keep low yield and low pressure separate, because they feel identical at the tap. Pressure is how forcefully water moves through your pipes; yield is how much the well can supply over time. A home can show fine pressure briefly and then run short when the well cannot sustain the draw. Measuring sustained yield, not a quick burst, reveals which one you face.

Why Loma Linda Wells Run Low

  • Clogged or encrusted screen. This is the most common culprit on the valley floor. The area's mineral-rich water deposits calcium, iron, and manganese scale on screen slots and gravel pack, slowly choking a well that the aquifer could otherwise supply.
  • Biofouling. Iron and sulfur bacteria build slimes that plug the intake, frequently alongside staining or a sulfur odor common to basin water.
  • Declining or fluctuating water table. Basin levels rise and fall with wet and dry cycles and regional pumping. A shallow or marginal well can lose saturated screen during drawn-down periods.
  • A pump set too high or sized wrong. A pump hung above the productive zone loses prime when the level dips, and an oversized pump can draw a well down faster than even a good aquifer refills it, causing short cycling.
  • Screen finished in finer material. Where a well is perforated across silt or clay layers within the alluvium, it releases water slowly even with coarse, productive zones nearby.

Because the underlying basin is so productive, many low-flow complaints in Loma Linda turn out to be problems that have crept up slowly over many years. A screen does not clog overnight; it closes gradually as mineral scale builds, so homeowners often adapt to a slowly weakening well without realizing how much capacity has been lost. A proper evaluation frequently uncovers yield that has been quietly choked off and can be restored, which is why we always recommend measuring before assuming the worst.

Solutions and the Tradeoffs

Well Rehabilitation

On the Loma Linda valley floor, rehabilitation is usually the first and most rewarding step, precisely because the aquifer is productive and the problem is most often a fouled screen. Mechanical brushing, surging, and chemical treatment clear scale and bacteria and frequently restore much of the original yield. At roughly $1,500 to $5,000, it is the most cost-effective fix for a well that has gradually slowed over the years.

Deepening or Rescreening

If a well is too shallow or screened across fine material, deepening or rescreening into a coarser, more productive zone of the older alluvium can substantially improve yield. Because the basin is deep and water-rich, this often pays off; we confirm with nearby well logs first.

Hydrofracturing

Hydrofracturing is a fractured-bedrock technique and is generally not the right tool on Loma Linda's alluvial valley floor. We note it for completeness, but here rehabilitation, rescreening, and pump work usually deliver better results. Where bedrock wells exist on the valley margins, it runs about $3,000 to $8,000.

Storage Cistern With a Booster or Constant-Pressure Pump

Even where the aquifer is strong, a storage cistern with a booster or constant-pressure pump can be the right call for a well that is temporarily weak or for a property with sharp demand peaks. A slow well fills a large tank, commonly 2,500 to 5,000 gallons, around the clock, and the booster delivers strong, even pressure on demand. Plan on roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed. The tradeoff is footprint and a little extra equipment, but it buffers demand and provides reserve while you decide whether to rehabilitate or rescreen.

Right-Sizing the Pump

Sometimes the cure is simply matching the pump to the well, setting it at the correct depth, or adding a flow-control valve. A properly sized pump and pressure tank cost $2,500 to $5,500 and resolve many low-water complaints on the valley floor.

When to Call a Professional

A drop in flow, a short-cycling pump, air sputtering at the taps, or rusty, cloudy, or sandy water all mean it is time for a professional yield evaluation. On a productive basin like this, low yield usually signals a fouled or aging well that cleaning can revive, so diagnosing before you spend is especially worthwhile. Frequently Asked Questions

Loma Linda sits over a productive basin, so why is my well low?

On the valley floor the groundwater is usually abundant, so a low well almost always points to the well itself, most often a screen clogged with mineral scale or bacteria, a shallow setting, or a pump issue. The good news is these are very treatable.

Is well cleaning really enough to restore my flow?

Frequently, yes. Because the aquifer here is strong, rehabilitation that clears scale and biofouling from the screen often recovers much of the original yield. It is usually the first and most cost-effective step on the Loma Linda valley floor.

Should I consider hydrofracturing in Loma Linda?

Usually not. Hydrofracturing works in fractured bedrock, while Loma Linda sits on a deep alluvial basin. Rehabilitation, rescreening, or pump work typically delivers better results here.

How do I tell if it is the well or the pump?

A sustained yield test answers it. If the well draws down fast and recovers slowly, the well or its screen is the limit; if the level holds but pressure is weak, the pump or tank is to blame. Our $125 diagnostic measures both.

What does it cost to improve a low-yield well?

Rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000, a storage-plus-booster system $3,000 to $7,000, and a right-sized pump and tank $2,500 to $5,500. Deepening and rescreening are quoted by depth, and the diagnostic determines the best path.
