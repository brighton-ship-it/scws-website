What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Twin Peaks Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Twin Peaks wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Mountain Wells in Twin Peaks

Twin Peaks sits high in the San Bernardino Mountains along Highway 189, tucked between Crestline, Blue Jay, and Lake Arrowhead in the rim-of-the-world country of San Bernardino County. Up here at roughly 5,200 feet, homes are scattered among the pines on steep granite slopes, and many of them rely on private wells rather than a municipal connection. Mountain wells like these are drilled straight into hard, fractured granite, which makes Twin Peaks one of the better places in all of Southern California for hydrofracturing to deliver a real, lasting improvement in well yield.

When a Twin Peaks well starts to fall behind, especially in the dry, hot stretch of late summer and early fall before the winter snows recharge the mountain, the instinct is often to assume the well is finished and a new one is the only answer. In fractured-granite terrain that is rarely true. More often the existing fractures have simply silted up or the borehole never intersected enough of them, and high-pressure hydrofracturing can reopen and extend that network for a fraction of the cost of redrilling on a tight, sloped mountain lot.

How Hydrofracturing Works in Mountain Granite

The process is straightforward and uses only clean water. We set an inflatable packer downhole to isolate a section of the well, then inject water at controlled pressures, commonly between 1,000 and 3,000 PSI. In the dense crystalline granite of the San Bernardino Mountains, that pressure does two things: it scours years of accumulated fine sediment and mineral scale out of the existing fractures, and it pries tight cracks open far enough to connect to water-bearing seams the original drill bit passed close to but never linked into. Because all of a mountain well's water arrives through these fractures, even a modest improvement in the network can translate into a large jump in gallons per minute.

A typical Twin Peaks job runs four to eight hours. We log a baseline flow rate first, treat one or more zones, then re-develop and re-test so you can see the measured before-and-after numbers. No chemicals, sand, or additives ever enter the well.

Common Causes of Low Yield in the Twin Peaks Area

  • Silted fractures. Decades of pumping pull fine material into the cracks closest to the bore, gradually choking flow.
  • Seasonal swing. Mountain water tables rise with winter snowmelt and fall through the dry summer, so a marginal well can keep up in spring and run short by September.
  • A borehole that hit few fractures. In granite, two nearby wells can yield very differently depending on luck of the draw. Hydrofracturing improves the odds for the weaker one.
  • Iron and mineral scaling. Mountain groundwater can carry enough dissolved minerals to slowly plate the fracture faces and reduce permeability.

Why Mountain Granite Responds So Well

The San Bernardino Mountains are built from the granitic and metamorphic rock of the southern California batholith, pushed up and fractured over millions of years by the same tectonic forces that raised the range. Unlike a sediment-filled valley where water saturates a porous layer of sand and gravel, a mountain like Twin Peaks stores and moves its groundwater almost entirely through the cracks in solid rock. That is the single biggest reason hydrofracturing performs better here than almost anywhere else we work. There is no loose formation to collapse, the rock holds an open fracture cleanly, and high pressure can propagate a crack a meaningful distance to tie the well into seams it could not previously reach. When people hear the word fracturing they sometimes picture the heavy industrial process used in oil fields, but well hydrofracturing is far gentler, uses only water, and is aimed at restoring a drinking-water supply rather than extracting hydrocarbons.

What to Check Before You Call

Not every water shortage at a Twin Peaks cabin is a low-yield well. Run through these first:

  • Check whether a pressure tank or pressure switch is the real problem. Frequent short pump cycles usually mean tank or switch trouble, not the aquifer.
  • In winter, rule out a frozen or partially frozen line, which is common at this elevation and mimics a dead well.
  • Notice whether you only run short during heavy use. A recovery-limited well does fine on light demand and fades when several fixtures run at once.
  • Watch for air sputtering at the taps, a sign the water level is being drawn down to the pump.

Planning Around the Mountain Seasons

Timing matters in Twin Peaks. The mountain recharges through winter and spring as snow and rain soak into the fracture system, and water levels are typically highest in those months and lowest by early fall. If your well is borderline, the worst time to discover it is on a hot September weekend with a full house. We encourage owners who noticed shortages last summer to schedule testing and any hydrofracturing well before the dry season returns, so the improved yield is in place when demand peaks. Winter access can be limited by snow on the rim-area roads, which is another reason to plan treatment for the milder shoulder seasons when our crews can reach your lot safely and work efficiently.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing on a steep, forested mountain lot demands specialized high-pressure equipment, downhole packers, and crews who know how San Bernardino Mountain granite behaves and how to get a rig safely onto a tight rim-area parcel. It is not a DIY job. Reach out when your yield has dropped noticeably, when a recently drilled well disappointed, or before you commit to the considerable expense of a new well up here. What the Treatment Day Looks Like

On the day of service, our crew arrives early to get equipment positioned before the afternoon mountain weather rolls in. We pull the pump if it is in the way, run a baseline flow and recovery measurement, and then begin staged pressure work, watching how the formation accepts water and adjusting as we go. Experienced crews can read a lot from how a granite well takes pressure, and that judgment is a large part of why professional treatment outperforms a one-size-fits-all approach. After the pressure stages, we redevelop the well to clear loosened material, reinstall the pump, and run a final flow test. You receive a documented comparison of yield before and after. Most Twin Peaks homeowners are back to normal water use the same day, with the well continuing to settle and clear over the following day or two.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Twin Peaks well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

Almost certainly, if it is drilled into the fractured granite typical of the San Bernardino Mountains. Hard crystalline rock is the ideal formation for this technique.

How much improvement should I expect?

In mountain granite we frequently see yields improve two to five times. We document a before-and-after flow test so the gain is measurable rather than a guess.

Can you reach my home on a steep mountain lot?

In most cases, yes. We assess access during the diagnostic visit and bring equipment suited to the rim-area roads and grades. If access is truly impossible we will tell you up front.

Will hydrofracturing affect my water quality?

No chemicals are used, only clean water. You may see brief cloudiness right after treatment as loosened sediment clears, then the water returns to normal.

How long do results last at this elevation?

Often many years. Some mountain wells benefit from a second treatment after a decade or more as fractures slowly re-silt, but many never need it.

What happens if my well does not respond?

A minority of wells do not improve. If yours is one, we will discuss deepening, screen cleaning, or a new well, and your diagnostic fee is applied toward that next step.
