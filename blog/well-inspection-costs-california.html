Why Well Inspections Matter

Well inspections serve two critical functions: they verify your water supply is safe and adequate, and they identify developing problems before they become expensive emergencies. Unlike municipal water that's continuously monitored and treated, private wells depend entirely on the homeowner for maintenance and quality assurance. A well that worked fine last year can develop bacterial contamination, declining yield, or equipment problems without obvious symptoms. Annual or biennial inspections catch these issues early, when they're often simple and inexpensive to address. For real estate transactions, inspections protect both buyers and sellers by establishing the well's current condition with documented evidence.

What Professional Inspectors Evaluate

A thorough well inspection examines multiple components. The wellhead inspection checks seal integrity, cap condition, and protection from surface contamination. Pump and motor evaluation tests operation, amp draw, pressure output, and cycling behavior. The pressure tank check verifies proper air charge and bladder condition. Flow rate measurement determines if the well produces adequate water—typically at least 3-5 GPM for residential use. Water quality testing at minimum checks bacteria and nitrates, with comprehensive tests adding minerals, pH, hardness, and potential contaminants. The inspector documents everything in a written report, providing a baseline record of your well's condition.

Real Estate Transaction Inspections

When buying or selling property with a private well, inspections are essentially mandatory. Lenders typically require them, and buyers' due diligence demands knowing what they're purchasing. Real estate well inspections must meet specific standards: current water quality testing showing potable water, verified adequate yield (usually 3+ GPM), proper wellhead construction and protection, and functional pump and delivery systems. Inspectors often find issues requiring resolution before transactions close—this protects buyers from inheriting problems and helps sellers address issues proactively. Budget $450-$600 for transaction inspections and schedule them early in the buying process to allow time for any required repairs.

Inspection Frequency and Preventive Value

How often should you inspect your well? At minimum, test water quality annually—California health officials recommend yearly bacteria testing for all private wells. Comprehensive professional inspections every 2-3 years catch mechanical and structural issues before they fail. More frequent inspections make sense for older wells, wells in areas with known contamination risks, or when you notice any changes in water quality, quantity, or pressure. The preventive value is substantial: catching a failing pressure tank during inspection costs $50 plus the tank replacement, versus emergency weekend service calls that double labor costs. Regular inspections extend equipment life and protect water quality.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a well inspection cost in San Diego County?

Professional well inspections in San Diego County typically cost $350-$600 in 2026, depending on inspection depth and testing included. Basic visual and operational inspections run $350-$450. Comprehensive inspections with water quality testing, flow rate measurement, and video camera inspection cost $500-$750. Real estate transaction inspections meeting lender requirements usually fall in the $450-$550 range.

What does a well inspection include?

Standard inspections cover: visual wellhead examination, pump operation check, pressure tank evaluation, flow rate measurement, electrical system inspection, and basic water quality assessment. Comprehensive inspections add detailed water testing, drawdown recovery test, and sometimes camera inspection of the well bore. The inspector provides a written report documenting findings, current condition, and any recommended repairs or concerns.

When do I need a well inspection?

Schedule inspections when: buying property with a well (essential due diligence), selling property to satisfy buyer requirements, experiencing water quality or quantity changes, before pump or system problems become emergencies, annually or biennially as preventive maintenance, and after any seismic activity or nearby construction. Many lenders require inspections for mortgage approval on properties with wells.

Can I do a well inspection myself?

Homeowners can perform basic visual checks—looking for damaged wellheads, unusual sounds, pressure fluctuations, or water quality changes. However, professional inspections involve specialized equipment and expertise homeowners lack: flow meters, pressure gauges, water testing supplies, and camera equipment. More importantly, licensed inspectors understand what they're seeing and can identify problems that untrained eyes miss. For real estate transactions, professional inspections are always required.

What problems do well inspections commonly find?

Common findings include: deteriorating wellhead seals allowing contamination, undersized or worn pressure tanks, failing pump components, bacterial contamination requiring shock chlorination, mineral or scale buildup reducing flow, and electrical issues like corroded connections or improper grounding. Many problems are inexpensive to fix when caught early—one reason regular inspections save money long-term despite their cost.
