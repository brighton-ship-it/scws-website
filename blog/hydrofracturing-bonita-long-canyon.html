What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Bonita Long Canyon Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Restoring Low-Yield Wells in Bonita Long Canyon

The Bonita Long Canyon area sits in the rolling hills above the Sweetwater River valley in the southwestern corner of San Diego County, where homes back up to open space and many properties still depend on private wells for irrigation, livestock, and household water. If your well here has slowed down—filling the pressure tank more slowly, running short during summer, or recovering sluggishly after a long draw—hydrofracturing is often the most cost-effective way to bring it back without the expense of drilling a brand-new borehole.

Wells in this part of the county are typically finished in fractured and decomposed granitic bedrock. A broad belt of granite runs north-to-south through the middle of San Diego County, and the Sweetwater drainage cuts right through it. That geology matters: in hard crystalline rock, water does not sit in a giant pool—it travels through a network of thin cracks and fractures. Over years of pumping, those pathways can silt up, scale over with mineral deposits, or simply never have been well-connected to begin with. Hydrofracturing reopens and extends that fracture network so more groundwater can reach your well.

How Hydrofracturing Works

The process is mechanical, not chemical. We lower an inflatable device called a packer into the well casing and seal off a specific section of the borehole. Then we inject clean water into that isolated zone at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. That pressure does two things at once: it flushes out the silt, fine clay, and mineral scale that have been choking the existing fractures, and it pushes those fractures open wider and farther into the surrounding bedrock—sometimes connecting your well to water-bearing cracks it could never previously tap.

Because we isolate one zone at a time with the packers, the pressure is concentrated exactly where it can do the most good rather than being lost up the casing. On deeper wells we may treat several zones in sequence. The entire job typically takes four to eight hours, and the only thing going down the hole is water—no proppant sand, no gels, no additives of any kind.

Why Bonita Long Canyon Wells Lose Yield

A few patterns show up repeatedly on wells in the Sweetwater hills:

  • Fracture clogging: Fine sediment and mineral scale gradually plug the hairline cracks that feed a granite well. The well did not run out of water—the doorways to it narrowed.
  • Marginal original completion: Some wells were drilled into rock that simply did not intersect many good fractures. They produced just enough at first, then fell behind as demand grew or drought lowered the water table.
  • Drought drawdown: Southern California's dry cycles lower regional water levels, and a well that was always marginal becomes a problem well during the driest months.
  • Increased demand: Adding landscaping, a pool, animals, or an accessory dwelling can outrun a well that was sized for a smaller household decades ago.

Signs Your Well May Need Hydrofracturing

You do not have to wait until the taps run dry. Watch for these early indicators:

  • The pump runs longer than it used to for the same water use, or short-cycles on and off rapidly.
  • Water pressure sags in the afternoon or whenever two fixtures run at once.
  • Faucets sputter and spit air, a classic sign the well is being drawn down to the pump intake.
  • You hear the pump struggling to keep up after laundry, irrigation, or filling a stock tank.
  • Annual production is clearly lower than it was a few years ago.

A simple way to check on your own is to time how long it takes the pump to refill the pressure tank during a quiet period versus during peak demand. A dramatic difference points to a recovery problem—exactly what hydrofracturing addresses.

When to Call a Professional

Declining yield can come from several sources, and not all of them are the formation. A failing pump, a waterlogged or failed pressure tank, a stuck pressure switch, or a hole in the drop pipe can all mimic a "dry well." That is why we start with a $125 diagnostic (credited toward any work we perform) to pin down the real cause before recommending hydrofracturing. If the problem is a $300 pressure switch, you should not be paying for a fracture job. If the well genuinely cannot keep up with demand and the borehole is structurally sound, hydrofracturing is usually the smart next step before anyone talks about drilling new.

What to Expect on Treatment Day in Bonita Long Canyon

When we arrive, the first step is always measurement. We run a pre-treatment flow test to establish exactly how much water your well is producing right now, then perform a video inspection of the casing. The camera survey tells us two important things: whether the casing is sound enough to treat safely, and where the productive fracture zones are located so we can target the packers precisely. There is no point pressurizing a section of barren rock when the camera shows the water-bearing fractures are forty feet deeper.

From there we set the packers, treat each target zone in sequence, and develop the well to flush loosened material back to the surface. Before we leave, we run a post-treatment flow test—usually you can see the difference the same day, and recovery typically stabilizes within 24 to 48 hours. You receive a documented before-and-after comparison so the result is on paper, not just a verbal promise.

A Note on Bonita's Water Context

The Sweetwater River watershed has long been a working agricultural and rural-residential area, and many of its wells are decades old. Older wells in fractured granite are precisely the ones that benefit most from rehabilitation, because they have had the most time to accumulate scale and silt in their fractures. If your well was drilled in the 1970s, 80s, or 90s and has slowly tapered off, do not assume it is finished—it may simply need its fracture network cleaned and reopened.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does hydrofracturing take on a Bonita Long Canyon well?

Most residential jobs in the Sweetwater Valley area run four to eight hours from setup to the final flow test. Wells with deep static water levels or multiple fracture zones to isolate can take a little longer. We measure your baseline yield first, treat each target zone, then re-test before we pack up so you know exactly what changed.

Will hydrofracturing contaminate my well or groundwater?

No. Unlike oil-and-gas fracking, water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure—no sand, gels, or chemical additives. We purge and disinfect the well afterward and recommend a bacteria test before you return it to service, which is standard for any well that has been opened up.

My Bonita well is in fractured granite. Is that a good candidate?

Generally yes. The decomposed and fractured granitic bedrock common across the Sweetwater drainage is exactly the formation hydrofracturing was designed for. Hard crystalline rock holds open fractures well after treatment. Wells finished in loose valley sand or gravel are not good candidates, which is part of what our assessment confirms before we proceed.

How much does hydrofracturing cost compared to a new well?

Hydrofracturing typically runs $3,000-$8,000. A new turnkey well in this part of San Diego County usually lands between $18,000 and $42,000 depending on depth and casing. Because rehabilitating an existing borehole is far cheaper, it is almost always worth trying first.

How long do the results last?

When a well responds, the improvement is usually durable—many wells hold their gains for years. Longevity depends on how aggressively the well is pumped and on the surrounding geology. We document before-and-after yields so you have a baseline if production drifts again down the road.

Do I need a permit for hydrofracturing in San Diego County?

Work on an existing well is handled differently than new drilling, but San Diego County DEH has specific requirements. As a licensed C-57 contractor (CSLB #1086994) we handle the compliance side and make sure the well is properly sealed and reported where required.
