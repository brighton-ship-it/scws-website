What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Diego Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in San Diego, California

San Diego is the second-largest city in California and the urban heart of San Diego County, stretching from the Pacific coastline at La Jolla and Point Loma inland through neighborhoods like Mira Mesa, Rancho Bernardo, and San Carlos. The overwhelming majority of homes and businesses inside the city are served by the Public Utilities Department on treated, imported municipal water, not by private wells. That single fact shapes everything you need to know about hydrofracturing here: for most San Diego addresses, well-yield enhancement simply does not apply, because there is no private well to enhance. We want to be straight with you about that before you spend a dollar.

Where private wells do exist around San Diego, they tend to sit on the rural and semi-rural margins of the city and county — the back canyons toward Lakeside and El Cajon, the higher ground east of Rancho Penasquitos, and the agricultural pockets that push toward Poway and the San Pasqual area. Those wells are the only ones for which hydrofracturing is even a candidate, and only when they are drilled into the region's fractured crystalline bedrock rather than a coastal sand or river-valley aquifer.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing (sometimes written "hydrofracking," though it has nothing to do with oil-and-gas fracking) is a well-rehabilitation method used on hard-rock wells. A low-yield bedrock well produces water through a network of thin natural fractures in granite and similar crystalline rock. Over years of pumping, those fractures clog with fine sediment, iron and manganese scale, and mineral precipitate, and the trickle of water that reaches the borehole slows down.

To reverse that, our crew lowers an inflatable packer into the cased borehole, seals off a target zone, and injects clean water — no chemicals — at pressures from roughly 1,000 up to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes debris out of the existing fractures and nudges hairline cracks open so they connect to a wider volume of water-bearing rock. When it works, the well draws from more fractures than before and recovers faster after each pumping cycle.

When It Helps in San Diego — and When It Doesn't

This is where San Diego's geology matters. Hydrofracturing only helps wells finished in fractured crystalline or granitic bedrock. Much of urban and coastal San Diego sits on marine sedimentary formations and alluvial fill — sand, silt, and gravel along the San Diego River, Mission Valley, and the coastal terraces. Wells in loose, granular aquifers gain little or nothing from hydrofracturing, because there are no rock fractures to open; pushing high-pressure water into sand just disturbs it. For those situations, screen rehabilitation, well deepening, a properly designed replacement well, or water treatment are the better tools.

If you are on city water, none of this is relevant and you should not be sold a fracturing job. If you own one of the genuine hard-rock wells on the rural edge of the region — typically drilled 200 to 600 feet into decomposed granite — then hydrofracturing is worth evaluating. The honest answer for most San Diego addresses, though, is that it is a weak fit, and we will tell you so during the diagnostic.

It also helps to understand where the productive bedrock actually lies around the city. The Peninsular Ranges Batholith — the same belt of Cretaceous granite that builds the mountains east of San Diego — underlies the higher inland ground, but it dives beneath thick coastal and valley sediments as you move west toward Mission Bay, Pacific Beach, and downtown. A well drilled in Tierrasanta or the eastern reaches of Scripps Ranch may reach fractured granite; a shallow well near the San Diego River floodplain almost certainly will not. This is why two homes only a few miles apart can have completely different best options, and why we insist on reading the well log before quoting a fracturing job.

The Hydrofracturing Process, Step by Step

  1. Diagnostic and flow baseline: We measure your current yield in gallons per minute and review the well log to confirm it was drilled into bedrock. Our diagnostic is $125, credited toward the work if you proceed.
  2. Camera inspection: A downhole video survey shows casing condition, water level, and visible fracture zones so we treat the right depths.
  3. Packer placement: Inflatable packers isolate the target interval below the casing.
  4. High-pressure injection: Clean water is injected to flush and open fractures, usually over four to eight hours.
  5. Post-treatment test and report: We re-measure yield, develop the well to clear debris, and hand you a documented before/after comparison.

Realistic Results and Expectations

On genuine fractured-bedrock wells, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, often two to five times the starting flow. That is a meaningful range, not a guarantee — a well that was a poor producer because it simply missed the productive fracture network may improve only modestly. We never promise a specific gallon-per-minute figure before testing, and any contractor who does should give you pause. On a non-bedrock well, expect little benefit, which is exactly why the up-front geology check matters.

Better Alternatives When Hydrofracturing Is a Poor Fit

Because so many San Diego wells sit outside ideal bedrock conditions, it is worth knowing the alternatives we recommend instead. If your well draws from sand or river-valley gravel, the issue is often a clogged or corroded screen, and a mechanical and chemical screen rehabilitation can restore much of the lost capacity. If the water table itself has dropped, deepening the existing borehole or drilling a new, properly sited well may be the only durable fix. And if your problem is water quality rather than quantity — iron, manganese, hardness, or bacteria — a treatment system solves what no amount of fracturing ever could. We would rather steer you to the right repair than sell you the wrong one, and that honesty is a big part of why our customers across the county come back to us.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Are you actually on a well? Most San Diego homes are on city water — confirm first.
  • Well log: Does it say granite, granodiorite, or "hard rock"? That is a good sign. "Sand," "gravel," or "alluvium" usually is not.
  • Yield history: Has your gallons-per-minute declined over the years, or was it always low?
  • Recovery time: Does the well take a long time to recover after heavy use?
  • Pump behavior: Short-cycling or air spitting at the tap often points to a yield or pump issue worth diagnosing.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when your well is running short during peak demand, when recovery has slowed noticeably, or when you are weighing hydrofracturing against the larger expense of a new well. Because hydrofracturing involves high pressures inside your casing, it is not a do-it-yourself project — improper isolation can damage the casing or grout seal. A licensed contractor protects both your well and your water quality.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most San Diego homes are on city water — does hydrofracturing apply to me?

Probably not. If you receive a monthly water bill from the City of San Diego, you are on municipal water and have no well to enhance. Hydrofracturing applies only to private hard-rock wells, which are found mainly on the rural edge of the county.

Is well hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. We inject only clean water to clear and open existing fractures in a water well. There are no chemicals and no new deep boring.

How do I know if my well is in bedrock?

Your well completion log usually states the formation. Granite, granodiorite, or "hard rock" are good candidates; sand, gravel, or alluvium generally are not. We confirm this during the diagnostic.

How long does the job take?

Most treatments run four to eight hours on site, with post-treatment flow verification over the following day or two.

What if hydrofracturing isn't right for my well?

We will tell you honestly and point you to better options — well deepening, a new well, screen rehabilitation, or water treatment, depending on what your well needs.

Do you offer free estimates?

Yes. We provide a free estimate, and the $125 diagnostic is credited toward any work you authorize.
