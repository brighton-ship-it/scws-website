What is Well Video Inspection?

A well video inspection uses a specialized downhole camera to see inside your well. This non-invasive diagnostic tool lets us examine your well's condition without pulling the pump or guessing at problems. For Hemet well owners, video inspection answers critical questions about well health and helps avoid costly mistakes.

Hemet sits in the San Jacinto Valley, where most residential wells range from 200-600 feet deep, tapping into alluvial basin aquifers and fractured granite bedrock. Wells in this area face unique challenges: seasonal water level fluctuations, iron and manganese mineral deposits, occasional sand intrusion from poorly constructed screens, and seismic stress from nearby faults. A downhole camera inspection reveals these issues clearly — no guessing, no expensive trial-and-error repairs.

How the Camera System Works

Our well camera system consists of:

  • High-resolution camera head — Waterproof, LED-lit, with 360-degree pan capability
  • Cable reel and depth counter — Precisely tracks camera position as it descends
  • Surface monitor and recorder — Displays live video and records footage for review
  • Measurement tools — Casing calipers and sonar attachments for dimensional analysis

The camera is slowly lowered into the well while we watch the video feed in real time. We can pause at any depth to examine details, measure casing diameter, and capture still images of problem areas. The entire inspection is recorded so you can see exactly what we see.

When You Need Video Inspection

Recurring Pump Failures

If you've replaced your pump more than once in a few years, something else is wrong. Common hidden problems a camera reveals:

  • Casing collapse — Crushed casing pinching the pump housing
  • Sand intrusion — Damaged well screen allowing fine sand to enter, abrading pump impellers
  • Corrosion holes — Perforations in the casing allowing surface water and contaminants to enter
  • Improper pump depth — Pump set too close to bottom, sucking up sediment

Reduced Water Production

If your well used to produce 15 GPM but now struggles to hit 8 GPM, a camera inspection can identify:

  • Screen clogging — Iron bacteria, mineral scale, or silt blocking perforations
  • Collapsed screen section — Crushed or bent screen reducing water entry
  • Sediment accumulation — Sand, silt, or mineral deposits at the bottom of the well
  • Aquifer intrusion — Clay or silt layers migrating into the well due to poor screen placement or regional groundwater changes

Sandy or Dirty Water

Sand in your water damages everything: faucets, toilets, appliances, and pumps. A camera shows why:

  • Screen failure — Corroded or mechanically damaged screen allowing formation sand through
  • Gravel pack failure — In gravel-packed wells, the filter pack may have settled or washed away
  • Pump vibration damage — Improperly secured pumps can vibrate against the casing, creating fine metal and mineral particles

Before Buying Property

Buying a home or ranch in Hemet with a well? Don't rely on a simple flow test. A camera inspection reveals:

  • True well condition — Beyond what a surface inspection or pump test shows
  • Casing lifespan — Steel casing corrodes over time; a camera shows remaining wall thickness
  • Hidden problems — Cracks, collapses, or screen damage the seller may not know about
  • Negotiating leverage — Documented problems justify price reductions or repair credits

After Earthquake or Ground Movement

Hemet is near the San Jacinto Fault zone. After significant seismic activity, wells can suffer:

  • Casing offsets — Joints separating or misaligning
  • Screen damage — Crushed or bent sections
  • Sediment dislodgement — Earthquake vibrations can collapse sediment into the well

Dropped Objects

If a tool, pump component, or debris has fallen into your well, a camera pinpoints its location and helps plan retrieval.

What We Can See

Casing Condition

The camera reveals:

  • Corrosion — Rust pits, thinning walls, perforations
  • Cracks — Stress fractures from settling or seismic activity
  • Holes — Corrosion breaches allowing surface water intrusion
  • Joint integrity — Welded or threaded joints may separate over time
  • Mineral deposits — Iron oxide (rust) and calcium carbonate scale buildup

Screen Condition and Water Entry Points

The well screen is where water enters the well. The camera shows:

  • Screen slot openings — Clogged, damaged, or clear
  • Biofouling — Iron bacteria (reddish-brown slime) or sulfur bacteria (black slime) coating screens
  • Mineral scale — Calcium carbonate, iron hydroxide, or manganese oxide encrustation
  • Mechanical damage — Crushed, bent, or corroded screen sections
  • Active water entry zones — We can see where water flows into the well, identifying productive zones

Obstructions and Debris

The camera identifies:

  • Collapsed casing sections — Crushed or buckled zones
  • Dropped tools or components — Pump parts, well caps, wrenches, wire
  • Sediment fill — Sand, silt, or gravel accumulation at the well bottom
  • Foreign objects — Rocks, tree roots (in shallow dug wells), or animal intrusion

Static Water Level and Pump Placement

The camera accurately measures:

  • Static water level — The resting water surface when the pump is off
  • Pumping water level — The dynamic water level when the pump is running (drawdown)
  • Pump depth and position — Verify the pump is set at optimal depth (typically 20-50 feet below static water level)

Pump Condition (Before and After Pulling)

Before pulling a pump for replacement, the camera can inspect:

  • Pump housing condition — Visible damage, corrosion, or deformation
  • Check valve function — Sometimes visible valve failures can be spotted
  • Wire and cable routing — Check for chafing, kinks, or damage

After pulling a pump, we can inspect the now-empty well bore for damage caused by the pump (casing wear, screen abrasion) or by the retrieval process.

The Inspection Process: What to Expect

Step 1: Pre-Inspection Consultation

When you call to schedule a video inspection, we'll ask about your well history:

  • Well age and depth — Helps us bring the right camera cable length
  • Casing diameter — Determines camera head size
  • Current problems — Guides what we focus on during inspection
  • Pump status — Whether it's already pulled or needs removal

Step 2: Site Setup

On inspection day, we bring:

  • Camera system — Reel, monitor, recorder, power supply
  • Pump-pulling equipment — If needed: crane or tripod, pipe wrenches, torque bars
  • Safety equipment — Well cap removal tools, disinfection supplies

Setup takes 15-30 minutes. We'll stage equipment near the wellhead and review findings in real time on the surface monitor.

Step 3: Camera Deployment

The camera descends slowly (10-20 feet per minute), pausing at any points of interest. We're looking for:

  • Casing joints — Threaded or welded connections, potential weak points
  • Water entry zones — Where aquifer water flows into the well
  • Obstructions or damage — Anything abnormal
  • Static water level — Precisely measured

We record the entire descent and capture still images at key depths.

Step 4: Review and Report

After the camera returns to the surface, we review findings with you on-site. You'll receive:

  • Digital video file — Full recording of the inspection
  • Written report — Depth measurements, findings, and recommendations
  • Still images — Key problem areas or points of interest
  • Repair cost estimates — If problems are found

A $300-$600 inspection often saves thousands by:

  • Avoiding unnecessary pump replacements — If the problem is casing damage, a new pump won't fix it
  • Identifying screen rehabilitation needs — Chemical or mechanical cleaning ($800-$1,500) may restore production without drilling a new well ($15,000-$40,000)
  • Documenting well condition for property sales — Avoid post-sale disputes and justify pricing
  • Preventing future failures — Knowing what's wrong lets you plan proactive repairs instead of emergency breakdowns

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you inspect my well without pulling the pump?

It depends. If your well has an access pipe (observation tube) or annular space alongside the pump, we may be able to camera inspect while the pump is still installed. However, for a thorough inspection of the full well bore, pump removal is typically required.

How long does a video inspection take?

For a 200-400 foot well with the pump already removed, expect 1-2 hours on-site. This includes camera deployment, video recording, and report generation. If we need to pull the pump first, add 3-4 hours for pump removal and reinstallation.

What if the camera finds major problems?

We'll provide a detailed report with repair or replacement options. Minor issues like screen biofouling can often be treated chemically ($800-$1,500). Casing damage may require well abandonment and drilling a new well ($18,000-$40,000). We'll help you understand your options and costs before you commit to anything.

Can the camera damage my well?

No. The camera is a passive observation tool. It's carefully lowered on a cable and does not contact or scrape the casing. It's the safest diagnostic method available.

Do I get a copy of the video?

Yes. We provide a digital video file (USB or download link) and a written report with still images, depth measurements, and findings. This documentation is valuable for property records, insurance claims, and future service planning.

Can a video inspection tell me if my well will go dry?

Not directly. The camera shows well construction and condition, not aquifer capacity. However, it can reveal static water levels, sediment accumulation, and screen clogging — all indicators of well performance. To assess aquifer yield, we recommend a pump test (measuring flow rate and drawdown over time).

Should I inspect my well before or after buying property?

Before closing escrow, during your inspection contingency period. This gives you negotiating power. If you wait until after purchase, you own the problem and bear the full cost of repairs.

How often should I video inspect my well?

Most wells don't need regular camera inspections. Schedule one when you experience problems (pump failures, reduced flow, water quality issues), before property transactions, or after significant seismic activity. For commercial or high-value agricultural operations, consider inspections every 10-15 years as preventive maintenance.
