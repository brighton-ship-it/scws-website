You turn on the faucet and nothing comes out—or worse, sputtering bursts of air followed by dirty water. Your shower pressure has dropped to a trickle. The washing machine takes forever to fill. For California well owners, these are the dreaded signs that your well might be going dry. With California's ongoing drought cycles, declining groundwater levels, and increasing demand on limited aquifers, dry wells have become one of the most common and stressful problems facing rural and semi-rural homeowners across the state. Whether you're in San Diego County, Riverside, the Central Valley, or the Sierra foothills, understanding why wells go dry and what to do about it can mean the difference between a manageable repair and a water crisis. This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know: the early warning signs, immediate emergency steps to take, proven solutions ranked by cost and effectiveness, when to deepen versus drill new, water hauling options, California drought resources, and preventive measures to protect your well from future drought impacts.

⚠️ Critical: If your well has completely stopped producing water, turn off your pump immediately to prevent damage from dry running. A pump running without water can burn out in minutes, adding thousands of dollars to your repair costs.

Signs Your Well Is Going Dry

Wells rarely go dry overnight—they give warning signs weeks or even months in advance. Recognizing these early symptoms gives you time to investigate, plan, and choose the best solution before you completely lose water.

Early Warning Signs

  • Sputtering faucets with air bursts: This is often the first sign—when you turn on a faucet, you get spurts of air mixed with water. This means your pump is drawing air along with water because the water level has dropped close to or below the pump intake.
  • Reduced water pressure: Gradual pressure decline throughout the house, especially during peak usage times (morning showers, evening dishwashing), indicates insufficient water supply.
  • Sediment or sand in water: When water levels drop, pumps can draw from closer to the well bottom, pulling in sediment that would normally settle below the intake.
  • Pump cycling frequently (short cycling): If your pump turns on and off rapidly, it's struggling to maintain pressure because water recovery is too slow to keep up with demand.
  • Longer pump run times: If your pump runs longer than usual to fill the pressure tank, the well may be recovering water slowly.
  • Water tastes or smells different: Changes in taste or odor can indicate you're drawing from a different aquifer layer as water levels drop.
  • Cloudy or dirty water after rain: This can signal that surface water is infiltrating your well due to dropping water levels creating negative pressure.

Advanced/Critical Warning Signs

  • Complete loss of water during peak times: No water during morning showers or when multiple fixtures run simultaneously
  • Pump won't build pressure: Pressure gauge shows very low or zero pressure even with pump running
  • Circuit breaker trips repeatedly: Pump working too hard and overheating
  • Pump makes grinding, rattling, or unusual noises: Could indicate cavitation from lack of water or pump damage from running dry
  • Water only available after long wait periods: Well recovers slowly overnight but runs out during daily use

💡 When to Act

Don't wait until you completely lose water. If you notice any early warning signs, schedule a well inspection immediately. A professional can measure your static water level (water level when pump isn't running) and recovery rate (how fast water refills) to determine if you're facing a dry well situation. Early intervention gives you more options and typically costs less than emergency repairs after complete well failure.

Why Wells Go Dry in California

Understanding why your well is going dry helps determine the right solution. California wells face unique challenges that have intensified in recent decades.

1. Drought and Reduced Aquifer Recharge

California's recurring droughts mean less rainfall and snowmelt to recharge aquifers. When precipitation is below normal for extended periods, groundwater levels decline. The state has experienced some of the most severe droughts on record in the past 15 years, with groundwater basins in many regions reaching historic lows.

Impact: Shallow wells (under 200 feet) are especially vulnerable. Wells that were adequate in wetter decades may no longer reach reliable water sources.

2. Aquifer Depletion and Overdraft

Many California aquifers are in "overdraft"—more water is being pumped out than is naturally recharging. This is especially severe in agricultural areas where irrigation demands are high, but it affects residential areas too. Once groundwater levels drop significantly, they may take years or decades to recover even with above-average rainfall.

Central Valley fact: Some areas have seen groundwater levels drop 50-100+ feet over the past few decades, causing thousands of wells to go dry.

3. Neighboring Wells (Interference/Drawdown)

If new homes are built nearby and tap into the same aquifer, increased pumping lowers the water table for everyone. This "cone of depression" effect means each well draws down the water level around it—when multiple wells operate in the same area, they interfere with each other.

Rural development impact: Areas that were once sparse are now subdivided. What worked with one well per 5-10 acres may not work with one well per acre or less.

4. Seasonal Water Table Fluctuations

Water tables naturally rise in winter/spring (wet season) and drop in summer/fall (dry season). If your well was drilled during a wet period or barely deep enough during average conditions, it may go dry seasonally during drought years.

Typical variation: Water levels can fluctuate 10-50+ feet between wet and dry seasons in many California regions.

5. Geological Changes and Well Aging

  • Well screen clogging: Sediment, mineral deposits, or bacterial growth can block perforations that allow water into your well
  • Collapsing formations: Earthquakes or ground settling can shift geological formations and reduce water flow
  • Fracture closure: In bedrock wells, fractures that once provided water can close or shift over time
  • Land subsidence: In areas with severe groundwater depletion, the land itself can sink, altering aquifer characteristics

6. Climate Change Impacts

Climate change is altering California's precipitation patterns—less snowpack, more variable rainfall, longer dry periods, and more intense but shorter wet periods. This reduces aquifer recharge even in years with "average" total rainfall because precipitation comes in brief, heavy storms that cause runoff rather than gradual infiltration.

📊 Southern California Water Table Decline

According to California Department of Water Resources data, groundwater levels in San Diego County's inland valleys have declined an average of 20-40 feet over the past 30 years, with some monitoring wells showing drops exceeding 100 feet in the same period. This trend is accelerating, not slowing.

Immediate Emergency Steps When You Lose Water

If your well has stopped producing water or is barely functioning, take these steps immediately to prevent further damage and assess your options:

Step 1: Turn Off Your Pump Immediately

Critical: A submersible pump running without water will overheat and burn out in 15-30 minutes. Turn off the pump at the breaker panel. If your breaker keeps tripping, do NOT keep resetting it—this is your pump telling you it's in distress.

Step 2: Verify It's Not a System Failure

Before assuming your well is dry, rule out other issues:

  • Check for blown fuses or tripped breakers (could indicate electrical problem, not dry well)
  • Inspect pressure tank gauge—if it shows pressure but no water flows, you may have a failed pressure switch or tank bladder
  • Check for leaks in pipes or pressure tank
  • Verify pump hasn't failed for other reasons (bearing failure, electrical issue)

See our guide on troubleshooting no water from well for a complete diagnosis checklist.

Step 3: Drastically Reduce Water Usage

  • No laundry, dishwasher, or long showers
  • Flush toilets only when necessary
  • Turn off automatic irrigation systems
  • Use paper plates to avoid washing dishes
  • Shower at gym or friend's house
  • If pump can run briefly, collect water in containers when available

Step 4: Call a Well Professional for Assessment

A well service company can:

  • Measure your static water level (how deep water is in the well)
  • Measure total well depth to see how much unused depth remains
  • Test recovery rate (how fast the well refills after pumping)
  • Determine pump depth and whether it can be lowered
  • Review well construction logs if available
  • Recommend the most cost-effective solution for your specific situation

Cost: Well inspection and water level measurement typically runs $150-$300—money well spent before making major repair decisions.

Step 5: Arrange Temporary Water Supply

If repairs will take days or weeks, you'll need temporary water:

  • Water hauling/delivery: Have water trucked in and pumped into a storage tank ($200-$400 per 2,500-gallon load in Southern California—see section below for details)
  • Bottled water: For drinking and cooking only (expensive for all household use)
  • Neighbor's hose: If nearby and willing, temporarily run a hose (compensate them for water costs)
  • Public water sources: Some counties offer emergency water filling stations during drought

Step 6: Research and Plan Your Long-Term Solution

Don't make hasty decisions under pressure. Once you have temporary water and know your well's condition, carefully evaluate the solutions below. Get multiple quotes if considering major work like deepening or new drilling.

Solutions for Dry Wells (Ranked by Cost)

The right solution depends on your well's depth, construction, geology, how far the water table has dropped, and your budget. Here are your options from least to most expensive:

1. Lower the Pump Deeper (If Possible)

How it works: If your well has unused depth below the current pump position, and water exists at that depth, lowering your pump can be a quick, cost-effective fix. A well professional pulls the pump, extends the electrical wiring and drop pipe, and reinstalls the pump deeper in the well.

When this works: Your well is deeper than originally needed and has extra depth available, the water table has dropped but water still exists below your current pump depth, your pump and equipment are in good condition and worth keeping.

Limitations: You can only lower the pump if there's unused well depth. If the pump is already near bottom, this isn't an option. Also, submersible pumps have maximum depth ratings—you may need a stronger pump if going very deep.

Pros: Lowest cost solution if viable, relatively quick (same-day to 2 days), no drilling or well construction needed, preserves existing well investment

Cons: Only works if unused depth exists, temporary solution if water table continues dropping, doesn't increase total well depth or capacity

Cost: $800-$2,500 depending on depth, access, pump size, and whether new wiring or pipe is needed

2. Hydrofracturing (For Bedrock Wells)

How it works: Hydrofracturing (or "hydrofracking") uses high-pressure water to create or enlarge fractures in bedrock, increasing the number of water-bearing fractures connected to your well. A specialized truck pumps water down the well at 1,000-3,000+ PSI, fracturing the surrounding rock.

When this works: Wells drilled into bedrock (not sediment/sand wells), existing water-bearing fractures are present but limited, low GPM (gallons per minute) flow rate even though water level is adequate, geological surveys suggest nearby fracture zones.

Effectiveness: Results vary widely. Some wells see dramatic improvement (1-2 GPM increasing to 10-20 GPM), while others see modest or no improvement. Success depends on local geology—if no water exists in nearby formations, fracturing won't help.

Pros: No drilling required, can dramatically increase well yield, relatively quick procedure (1-2 days), less expensive than deepening or drilling new

Cons: Results unpredictable, doesn't work for sediment wells, won't help if no water-bearing formations exist nearby, temporary solution if water table drops further

Cost: $2,000-$5,000 depending on well depth, rock type, and required pressure

3. Deepening the Existing Well

How it works: A drilling rig is brought in to drill deeper through the existing well casing or beside it, extending the well to reach deeper water-bearing zones. The casing is extended, new screen/perforations are added, and the well is developed and tested.

When this works: Geological surveys or nearby wells indicate water-bearing zones exist below current well depth, existing well casing is in good condition (typically less than 20 years old), deepening would be less than 50% of a new well's cost, property access allows for drilling equipment.

Considerations: Not all wells can be deepened—old wells with corroded casing or unusual construction may not be suitable. A well professional will inspect and advise. Sometimes a new well adjacent to the old one is easier and cheaper than deepening.

Pros: Cheaper than new well if viable, can reach significantly deeper water sources, extends life of existing well investment, usually faster than full new well drilling

Cons: Still expensive, not all wells can be deepened, no guarantee deeper zones have adequate water, old casing condition may be unknown until work begins

Cost: $5,000-$15,000+ depending on how much depth is added, geology, and casing/screen requirements. Typically $50-$150 per foot plus mobilization.

4. Drilling a New Well

How it works: A new well is drilled at a different location on your property or near the old well, going deep enough to reach reliable water zones below current water table levels. This is a complete new installation including casing, screen, pump, pressure system, and all equipment.

When this is best: Existing well is old (30+ years) with deteriorating casing, deepening would cost nearly as much, geological data suggests better water at a different location, you need to relocate well away from contamination sources, existing well is already very deep and further deepening isn't viable, you want a permanent solution with modern materials.

Depth considerations: In areas experiencing water table decline, drilling significantly deeper than your old well (100-200+ feet deeper) ensures you won't face the same problem in a few years. In Southern California, many new wells are 400-600+ feet deep to reach stable aquifers.

Pros: Most reliable long-term solution, modern materials and construction, can be located optimally, often required if old well is beyond repair, includes all new equipment with warranties

Cons: Most expensive option, time-consuming (1-3 weeks for permits, drilling, testing, and completion), requires permits and compliance with current regulations, no guarantee you'll hit adequate water (though geological surveys reduce risk)

Cost: $15,000-$50,000+ in California depending on required depth, geology, location accessibility, pump depth, and equipment specs. See our San Diego well drilling cost guide for detailed breakdowns.

5. Install Water Storage Tanks

How it works: Large water storage tanks (1,000-10,000+ gallons) are installed to capture water during periods when the well is producing and provide a buffer during peak usage or when the well temporarily runs dry. A booster pump delivers water from the tank to your house.

When this works: Your well produces adequate water but recovers slowly (low GPM), you experience seasonal water shortages but the well recovers during wet months, combined with pump timer to fill tanks during off-peak hours (night), supplementing a marginal well to extend its usability.

Reality check: Storage tanks don't create water—they just help manage limited supply. If your well is producing almost no water, storage won't solve the problem. This works best for wells with slow recovery rates but adequate total daily production.

Pros: Relatively affordable compared to well work, provides buffer against short-term shortages, can extend marginal well's viability, can be used with water hauling as interim solution

Cons: Doesn't solve underlying water shortage, requires space for tank(s), needs maintenance and periodic cleaning, can freeze in cold climates (insulation needed), requires separate booster pump system

Cost: $2,000-$8,000+ for tanks, installation, and booster pump system depending on capacity and setup complexity

Water Hauling as a Temporary Solution

If you need water immediately while planning permanent repairs, or if you're waiting for permits and drilling, water hauling provides a temporary lifeline.

How Water Hauling Works

A water truck delivers potable water to your property and pumps it into a storage tank (you'll need a tank—rental available if you don't have one). The water is then pumped into your home's plumbing system via a booster pump, just like well water.

Costs in San Diego & Riverside Counties

  • Water delivery: $200-$400 per 2,500-3,000 gallon load (approximately $0.08-$0.16 per gallon)
  • Mileage/distance fees: $50-$150 for remote properties beyond standard service area
  • Tank rental (if needed): $50-$150/month for 2,500-5,000 gallon tank
  • Booster pump system (if you don't have one): $500-$1,500 to install

How long does a load last? Average household uses 300-400 gallons/day. With strict conservation (200-250 gallons/day), a 2,500-gallon load lasts 10-14 days. With normal usage, expect 6-8 days per load.

Monthly cost estimate: $800-$1,600/month for full water hauling dependency

Finding Water Haulers

  • Search "water hauling [your county]" or "potable water delivery"
  • Contact local well drilling companies—many offer water hauling services
  • Check with county water districts—some maintain lists of approved haulers
  • Ask your well service company for referrals
  • Ensure the water is certified potable (safe for drinking/household use)

💰 When Hauling Makes Sense

Water hauling is expensive—$800-$1,600/month adds up fast. Within 6-12 months, you'll have spent enough to pay for lowering a pump or significantly contributed to well deepening costs. Use hauling as a bridge solution while you arrange permanent repairs, not as a long-term strategy unless absolutely necessary.

When to Deepen vs. Drill a New Well

This is one of the most common dilemmas facing well owners with dry wells. Here's how to decide:

Deepen Your Existing Well When:

  • Well is relatively new (less than 20 years old) with casing in good condition
  • Geological surveys or nearby well data confirm water-bearing zones exist 50-200 feet below your current depth
  • Deepening cost is less than 50-60% of new well cost
  • Current well location is still optimal (no contamination concerns, good access)
  • You only need to go 50-150 feet deeper to reach reliable water
  • Well casing diameter allows for deeper drilling equipment

Drill a New Well When:

  • Existing well is over 30 years old with uncertain casing integrity
  • Well would need to be deepened 200+ feet to reach water (approaching new well cost)
  • Geological data shows no promising water zones directly below current well
  • Current well location has issues (contamination nearby, poor access for maintenance)
  • Pump, pressure tank, and other equipment need replacement anyway
  • Deepening cost exceeds 60-70% of new well cost
  • You want modern construction with warranty and guaranteed depth
  • Old well has chronic problems beyond just depth (collapsing, clogging, etc.)

📋 Get Multiple Professional Opinions

Well drilling is a significant investment. Get at least 2-3 quotes from different companies, and ask each: "Given my well's condition and local geology, would you recommend deepening or drilling new, and why?" Contractors with geological knowledge and local experience will give you honest assessments. Beware of anyone who automatically recommends the most expensive option without explaining why alternatives won't work.

Emergency Drought Assistance: During severe drought declarations, California sometimes offers grants or low-interest loans for well repairs, deepening, or drilling. Check the Drought Portal for current programs.

County-specific programs: Some counties offer assistance to low-income households facing well failures

Tax deductions: Well repairs and improvements may be tax-deductible as necessary home maintenance—consult your tax advisor

Preventive Measures to Protect Your Well

While you can't control the drought, you can take steps to protect your well and reduce the risk of it going dry:

1. Monitor Water Levels Annually

Have your well professional measure and record your static water level at least once per year—ideally before summer (after spring recharge) and after summer (lowest point). Tracking this over years reveals trends. If your water level is dropping 5-10 feet per year, you'll know to plan ahead rather than being surprised by sudden failure.

Cost: $100-$200 per measurement; worth every penny for early warning

2. Reduce Water Consumption

  • Fix leaks immediately: A running toilet can waste 200+ gallons/day
  • Install low-flow fixtures: Showerheads, faucet aerators, toilets
  • Drought-tolerant landscaping: Native plants require far less water than lawns
  • Efficient appliances: High-efficiency washers and dishwashers use 50% less water
  • Limit outdoor watering: Water early morning or evening to reduce evaporation
  • Harvest rainwater: Capture roof runoff for garden use (check local regulations)

Typical California household can reduce usage from 400 gallons/day to 200-250 gallons/day with conservation efforts—essentially doubling your well's effective capacity.

3. Install Water Storage

Even if your well is currently adequate, adding storage tanks provides a buffer against future water table decline or temporary shortages. Storage also reduces peak demand on your well, allowing it to recover between usage periods.

4. Avoid Over-Pumping

Don't pump your well faster than it recovers. If your well produces 5 GPM, using 10 GPM for extended periods draws down the well faster than it refills, potentially damaging the aquifer around your well. Learn your well's sustainable flow rate and respect it.

5. Maintain Your Well System

Regular maintenance catches problems early and ensures your well operates efficiently:

  • Annual well inspection and water level measurement
  • Water quality testing every 1-3 years
  • Pump inspection every 3-5 years
  • Clean or replace sediment filters regularly
  • Watch for early warning signs and address them immediately

6. Consider Drilling Deeper from the Start

If building new or replacing a well, drill deeper than the minimum required. Going an extra 100-200 feet costs relatively little during initial drilling but provides insurance against future water table decline. A well that's 500 feet deep instead of 300 feet has a much larger buffer against drought.

Water Table Trends in Southern California

Understanding regional water table trends helps you plan for your well's future. Southern California faces some of the state's most severe groundwater challenges:

San Diego County

Inland valleys (Ramona, Valley Center, Julian, Alpine, Lakeside) have seen water tables drop 20-40 feet on average over the past 30 years, with some monitoring wells showing 100+ foot declines. Coastal areas are more stable due to imported water supplementing local supplies, but inland well owners face increasing vulnerability.

Riverside County

Western Riverside (Temecula, Murrieta, Menifee) and desert areas (Coachella Valley, Desert Hot Springs) experience severe overdraft. Some areas have water tables dropping 3-10 feet per year during drought periods. Agricultural pumping compounds residential well issues.

Central Valley

California's agricultural heartland faces the most extreme groundwater depletion—some areas have seen 50-150+ foot water table drops over several decades. Thousands of wells have gone dry, requiring deepening or replacement.

Future Outlook

Climate models predict more frequent and intense droughts for California. Groundwater basins are being managed under the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), but recovery will take decades. Well owners should plan for continued water table decline rather than assuming recovery to historical levels.

⚠️ Planning Ahead Is Essential

If your well is currently marginal (sputtering, low pressure, slow recovery), don't wait until it fails completely. Plan and budget for deepening or drilling while you still have water. Emergency repairs cost more, force rushed decisions, and leave you without water during the work. Proactive well owners save money and avoid water crises.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the signs my well is going dry?

Common signs your well is going dry include: sputtering faucets with air bursts, reduced water pressure throughout the house, sediment or sand in your water, the pump cycling on and off frequently (short cycling), unusual pump noises or longer run times, cloudy or dirty water after rain, and complete loss of water during peak usage times. If you notice any of these symptoms, test your well's water level immediately—the sooner you address declining water levels, the more options you have and the less expensive the solution typically is.

Why is my California well going dry?

California wells go dry for several reasons: extended drought reducing aquifer recharge, multiple nearby wells drawing from the same aquifer (drawdown interference), seasonal water table fluctuations (lowest in late summer/fall), over-pumping exceeding the aquifer's recharge rate, geological changes or shifting formations, and shallow wells being more vulnerable to drought. Southern California has experienced declining groundwater levels for decades, with some areas seeing water tables drop 50-100+ feet. Climate change is intensifying drought frequency and duration, making dry wells increasingly common across the state.

What should I do immediately if my well runs dry?

If your well runs dry, take these immediate steps: (1) Turn off your well pump to prevent damage from dry running, (2) Check your electrical panel—don't keep resetting breakers, (3) Verify it's not a pump or pressure system failure, (4) Reduce water usage drastically, (5) Call a well professional to measure water level and assess the situation, (6) Arrange temporary water hauling if needed (typically $200-$400 per 2,500-gallon load in Southern California), (7) Stop using water-intensive appliances, and (8) Shower at a gym or friend's house while evaluating permanent solutions. Acting quickly prevents pump damage and gives you time to choose the right long-term solution rather than making emergency decisions.

How much does it cost to fix a dry well in California?

Dry well solutions range dramatically in cost: lowering your existing pump deeper ($800-$2,500) is the cheapest option if your well has unused depth; hydrofracturing to increase fractures and water flow ($2,000-$5,000) works for bedrock wells; deepening your existing well ($5,000-$15,000+) depends on depth added and geology; drilling a new well ($15,000-$50,000+) is the most expensive but most reliable long-term solution; and installing water storage tanks ($2,000-$8,000+) can help wells with slow recovery. The right solution depends on your well depth, geology, water table level, and how much the water level has dropped. A professional well inspection and water level measurement ($150-$300) is essential before choosing a solution.

Should I deepen my existing well or drill a new one?

Deepen your existing well when: it's less than 20 years old, the casing is in good condition, deeper water-bearing zones exist below your current depth (confirmed by geological surveys), and deepening would cost less than 50% of a new well. Drill a new well when: your current well is old with deteriorating casing, geological surveys show no promising zones below, you need to relocate away from contamination sources, your pump and equipment need replacement anyway, or the well is already extremely deep. In many cases, especially with wells over 300-400 feet, drilling new is more cost-effective than deepening. A well professional can measure your static water level, review well logs, and compare options. Sometimes deepening 50-100 feet solves the problem; other times you'd need to go 200+ feet deeper, making new drilling the better investment.

How much does water hauling cost in Southern California?

Water hauling costs in San Diego and Riverside counties typically run $200-$400 for a 2,500-gallon truckload (about $0.08-$0.16 per gallon). A typical household uses 300-400 gallons per day, meaning one truckload lasts 6-8 days with normal usage or 10-14 days with strict conservation. Monthly costs for full water hauling dependency range from $800-$1,600+. Some haulers charge mileage fees for remote properties, adding $50-$150 per delivery. Tank rental (if you don't have storage) costs $50-$150/month for a 2,500-5,000 gallon tank. While water hauling works as a temporary solution during drought or while arranging well repairs, it's expensive long-term—usually more than financing a permanent well solution within 6-12 months.

Can my well recover after a drought?

Many wells partially recover after drought when winter rains recharge aquifers, but recovery is never guaranteed and often incomplete. Factors affecting recovery include: aquifer type (unconfined aquifers recharge faster than confined), well depth (deeper wells tap more stable water sources), local geology and soil permeability, total rainfall amounts, how many neighboring wells are competing, and whether the water table has permanently declined due to long-term over-pumping. Southern California's ongoing groundwater overdraft means some areas see water tables drop permanently each year even after wet winters. If your well has gone dry, waiting for seasonal recovery is risky—water tables may not return to previous levels, and you could face another dry season without water. A well professional can measure water level trends and help you decide if waiting is viable or if you need immediate intervention.

How can I prevent my well from going dry?

Prevent your well from going dry with these strategies: (1) Monitor water levels annually—measure static water level before and after summer to track trends, (2) Reduce water consumption—fix leaks, install low-flow fixtures, drought-tolerant landscaping, (3) Install water storage tanks to capture water during high-recharge periods and reduce peak demand on your well, (4) Avoid over-pumping—spread usage throughout the day rather than heavy morning/evening peaks, (5) Maintain your well system—regular inspections catch problems early, (6) Track local groundwater trends through county water agencies, (7) Consider a backup water source for critical needs, and (8) If building new, drill deeper than minimum requirements to provide a buffer against future drought. Southern California well owners should assume water tables will continue declining and plan accordingly—shallow wells under 200 feet are increasingly vulnerable.
