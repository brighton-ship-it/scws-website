What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Running Springs Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Running Springs

Running Springs straddles the Rim of the World at roughly 6,080 feet, where Highways 18 and 330 meet in the heart of the San Bernardino Mountains. It is a forested community of full-time mountain homes and weekend cabins, neighboring Arrowbear and Smiley Park, with Green Valley Lake and Big Bear farther up the ridgeline. At this elevation there is almost no soft sediment to drill into; wells reach water through the fractured granite and crystalline bedrock that forms the spine of the range. That geology is precisely why hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) is so often the right answer when a Running Springs well begins to fall short.

How Water Moves Through Running Springs Bedrock

A hard-rock well does not tap a wide aquifer. It produces only as well as the network of cracks and fracture zones it happens to cross in otherwise solid granite. Snowmelt and winter rain recharge that rock slowly, percolating down through the same fractures rather than soaking into a sandy basin. When those seams are open and well-connected, a well thrives; when they are tight or partly plugged, the same well struggles even though there is water in the mountain around it. Hydrofracturing is built for exactly this situation: it reopens and extends the fracture network so your borehole can capture more of the water moving through the rock.

Why Yield Declines Over the Years

The water-bearing seams in a Running Springs well gradually narrow as fine mineral scale, silt, and iron and manganese deposits accumulate along the fracture faces. The decline is usually slow and seasonal rather than sudden. Homeowners tend to notice it first in late summer and early fall, the driest part of the mountain year, when a well that comfortably ran the house now fades under an evening load, recovers slowly, and leaves the pump cycling on and off. In a string of dry years, the seams feeding a marginal well draw down first, so a well that was perfectly adequate when it was drilled in a wetter decade can fall behind as both the climate and the fracture pathways shift. Reopening those pathways frequently restores enough flow to carry a household through the seasonal low point.

The Treatment, and Why Granite Responds So Well

Hydrofracturing uses only clean water. We isolate a target interval with inflatable packers, then pump water into that sealed zone at pressures that commonly run from about 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure clears sediment out of existing fractures and gently widens and extends the natural cracks, reconnecting the borehole to seams that had silted shut. Because Running Springs bedrock is true fractured granite, there is solid rock to hold the new openings apart once they form, which is why mountain wells respond so much better than wells in loose, sandy ground. No chemicals are used and nothing is added to your aquifer.

Signs a Running Springs Well May Be a Candidate

  • Year-over-year decline: production keeps dropping even in normal-snow winters.
  • Slow recovery under load: the well draws down during heavy use and takes hours to refill.
  • A weak new borehole: a recently drilled well found granite but came in with disappointing flow.
  • Pump short-cycling: the pump starts and stops repeatedly because the well cannot keep pace.
  • Late-season rationing: you stretch water every September and October.

Rule Out the Simple Causes First

Before concluding the well is failing, check the easy things. A low or waterlogged pressure tank, or a worn pressure switch, can imitate a yield loss and is far cheaper to fix. A single weak fixture usually means a clogged aerator or a plumbing restriction, not a well problem. At 6,000-plus feet, frozen lines and tanks in winter can mimic a dry well until they thaw. And a pump drawing air or pushing sandy water may signal a dropping water level reaching the pump, or a worn pump, rather than the aquifer. When the well truly recovers more slowly than it once did, runs low only under sustained demand, and has declined gradually over seasons, that pattern points squarely at a tightening fracture network, the problem hydrofracturing solves.

When to Bring in a Professional

If your well has clearly lost ground over the years, if a newer hole came in weak, or if you are deciding between a new well and saving the existing one, that is the time for a licensed contractor to run a flow test and camera inspection. A new well in the San Bernardino high country is a five-figure gamble, so a proper diagnosis is well worth its modest cost. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward the work if you move forward.

The Step-by-Step Process

  1. Baseline flow test: we measure current yield and check static and pumping water levels.
  2. Downhole camera survey: a video inspection shows casing condition and the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Packer isolation: we seal the chosen interval so the pressure concentrates on that zone.
  4. High-pressure injection: clean water clears and extends the fractures, usually four to eight hours on site.
  5. Post-treatment flow test: we re-measure yield after the well settles and provide a documented before-and-after comparison.

What Hydrofracturing Will and Will Not Do

Honest expectations make the difference between a satisfied customer and a disappointed one. Hydrofracturing does not create new groundwater and it cannot undo a dry snow year; what it does is reconnect your borehole to the water already moving through Running Springs granite by clearing and widening the seams that feed it. On a genuine fractured-rock well that has gradually lost yield, that is usually enough to restore a comfortable supply and a seasonal cushion. It will not save a well whose level has truly dropped below the pump in a deep drought, where lowering the pump is the better fix, and it is not suited to a well drawing from loose decomposed-granite sand. Nearly all wells along the Rim sit in solid fractured rock, so most are strong candidates, but we verify that with a flow test and camera survey before recommending treatment.

After Treatment: Water Quality and Mountain Maintenance

Expect cloudy water for the first hours to a day after treatment as the opened fractures flush silt and fine mineral material; it clears as the well develops, and we run the well to help move that sediment out. San Bernardino granite can carry naturally elevated iron and manganese, and opening new seams may shift the mineral balance briefly until the well stabilizes, so service any iron filter or softener afterward. At Running Springs elevations, freeze protection for the wellhead, pressure tank, and exposed lines is essential to keeping your restored flow reliable through winter. A periodic flow check helps you notice any renewed slip in production early, when a planned re-treatment is far simpler than an emergency call during a winter storm that has closed the highway.

Cost Compared to a New Well in Running Springs

Drilling a new well along the Rim is costly and uncertain, especially given the depth needed to reach reliable seams and the challenge of getting a rig onto steep forest lots. A turnkey new well generally runs from about $18,000 to $42,000. Hydrofracturing an existing well usually costs between $3,000 and $8,000 and improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of the fractured-rock wells we treat, with typical gains of two to five times the starting flow. For most Running Springs homeowners, it is the obvious first step before committing to a new borehole.
