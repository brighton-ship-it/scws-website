Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level

Sand and Sediment in Highland Well Water: A Local Guide

Highland sits in the heart of San Bernardino County, in the Inland Empire just east of the city of San Bernardino at the foot of the San Bernardino Mountains. Most private wells in and around Highland draw from the Bunker Hill / San Bernardino groundwater basin, one of the largest alluvial groundwater basins in the region. This is a deep basin of sand and gravel laid down over thousands of years by the Santa Ana River and by the City Creek and Plunge Creek alluvial fans that spread out of the mountains and into the valley. Because the aquifer your well taps is built from loose, water-worn sand and gravel rather than solid rock, sand and fine sediment in your water is a genuine and very local concern.

If you have started seeing grit settle in the bottom of a glass, found sand in the bottom of your toilet tank, or heard your pressure tank ticking with sediment, you are not alone. Across Highland, East Highlands, Mentone, and the neighboring communities of San Bernardino and Redlands, homeowners on private wells regularly call us about sand. The good news is that sandy water is almost always fixable once a technician who understands the local geology pins down the cause. This guide walks through why it happens here, the damage it does, how we diagnose it, how we fix it, what it costs, and when it is time to call a professional.

Why Highland Wells Produce Sand

The single biggest reason Highland wells are prone to sand is geology. The Bunker Hill / San Bernardino basin is an alluvial sand-and-gravel formation, fed by the Santa Ana River and the City Creek and Plunge Creek fan deposits that wash down off the San Bernardino Mountains. When a well is screened in a zone with a high fraction of fine sand, that sand wants to move toward the well every time you pump. The higher East Highlands and foothill areas sit on coarser fan material and fractured bedrock above the main basin, but even those wells can pull fine material when conditions change. A few of the most common causes we see:

  • A worn or failing pump: An aging submersible pump with worn impellers and bearings can begin pulling sand it once kept out, and the abrasive sand then accelerates its own decline.
  • An oversized or over-drawn pump: A pump that moves more water than the aquifer can comfortably deliver creates high velocity at the screen, dragging fine sand in with the water.
  • A failed or corroded screen: The well screen is the filter that keeps formation sand out. Decades of mineral-rich Inland Empire water can corrode or encrust a screen until it tears or develops gaps.
  • The wrong screen slot size: In a fine, sandy aquifer like the Bunker Hill basin, a screen with slots sized too large will let fine sand pass straight through.
  • Excessive drawdown: Pumping the water level down too far, especially during dry spells, increases the suction at the screen and mobilizes sand from the surrounding gravel pack.
  • A new, undeveloped well: A freshly drilled well naturally produces some sand for the first days or weeks until it is properly developed and the fine material around the screen is drawn out.
  • The sandy aquifer itself: Some Highland wells are simply screened in a sandier interval of the basin, so sand control becomes part of normal operation rather than a one-time repair.

Identifying which of these is at work matters, because the fix for a worn pump is very different from the fix for a torn screen or an over-drawn aquifer. That is why diagnosis comes before any repair.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is abrasive, and abrasive material moving through a water system at pressure does real harm over time. The most expensive casualty is usually the submersible pump itself: sand grinds away at impellers and seals, robbing the pump of efficiency until it fails outright, often years before it should. Sediment also collects in the pressure tank, fouls the bladder, and clogs the pressure switch so the system short-cycles. Downstream, sand scores the seats in faucets and toilet fill valves, wears out the solenoids in dishwashers and washing machines, settles in the water heater where it bakes onto the elements, and plugs aerators and fixture screens throughout the house. Beyond the nuisance and the appliance repair bills, persistent sand can signal a structural problem in the well, such as a breached casing or a failing screen, that only gets worse and more costly the longer it is left alone.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Highland

We start with measurement, not guesswork. A proper diagnosis tells us whether you are dealing with a pump problem, a screen problem, or an aquifer problem, and it keeps you from paying for a repair you do not need.

  • Jar and settling test: We collect water samples and let the sediment settle so we can see how much sand is present, how coarse it is, and whether it is steady or surging.
  • Sand concentration check: Measuring the actual sand load over a timed sample tells us how serious the problem is and whether filtration alone will be enough.
  • Amp draw and pump depth review: Checking the pump's amperage and confirming how deep it is set relative to the screen reveals whether the pump is overworked or set too low into the sediment zone.
  • Pull and inspect the pump and screen: When the data points to hardware, we pull the pump and inspect it and the screen for wear, corrosion, and damage.
  • Downhole camera inspection: A camera survey lets us look directly at the casing and screen to find tears, holes, encrustation, or collapsed sections without guessing.
  • Flow and drawdown testing: Measuring yield against how far the water level drops shows whether the well is being over-pumped for its capacity in the Bunker Hill basin.

Our diagnostic visit is a flat $125, and that amount is credited toward any repair we perform, so the assessment pays for itself the moment you move forward.

How We Fix It

The right fix depends entirely on the cause, and in many Highland homes the best result comes from combining a downhole correction with point-of-entry filtration. Our common solutions include:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters: These spin sand out of the water using centrifugal force before it ever reaches your pressure tank, an excellent match for wells screened in the sandy basin.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration: Spin-down screens and staged sediment cartridges polish out finer grit, protecting fixtures and appliances throughout the house.
  • Pump resizing and repositioning: Installing a correctly sized pump and setting it at the proper depth above the sediment reduces velocity at the screen and stops sand from being dragged in.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment: Cleaning, surging, and redeveloping the well removes accumulated fines and restores the natural gravel-pack filter around the screen.
  • Screen repair or relining: Where the screen is torn or corroded, we repair, reline, or replace it so the well filters formation sand the way it was designed to.

Preventing Sand Problems

Once your water runs clean, a little routine care keeps it that way. Avoid over-pumping by matching your household demand and any irrigation to the well's tested capacity, especially during the dry Inland Empire summer when water levels in the basin drop. Have the pump checked periodically so worn parts are caught before they start pulling sand. Service sand separators and sediment filters on schedule so they keep doing their job. Watch for early warning signs, a faint grittiness, cloudy water after heavy use, or a pressure tank that cycles more than it used to, and address them while they are small. Wells in the alluvial Bunker Hill basin reward steady, moderate use and regular attention.

When to Call a Professional

A spin-down filter at the wellhead is something many homeowners can maintain, but sand is often a symptom of a deeper problem that belongs in professional hands. Call us when sand appears suddenly or gets noticeably worse, when you also notice dropping pressure or air spitting from the taps, when the pump runs constantly or short-cycles, or when filters clog faster than you can clean them. Pulling a pump, running a downhole camera, redeveloping a well, or repairing a screen all require specialized equipment and a licensed well contractor. Running a pump in sandy water while you wait only shortens its life, so it pays to have it looked at promptly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Highland well water normal?

A brand-new well can produce some sand for the first days or weeks until it is developed. In an established well, a sudden or steady appearance of sand is not normal and should be checked, because it usually points to a worn pump, a failing screen, or over-pumping of the sandy basin.

Why does my water get sandy only when I use a lot of water?

Heavy use draws the water level down and increases the suction at the screen, which mobilizes fine sand from the surrounding gravel in the Bunker Hill basin. This often means the pump is over-drawing the well or is set too deep, both of which we can correct.

Will a sediment filter alone fix the problem?

A filter protects your home and appliances and is often part of the solution, but it treats the symptom. If the sand is coming from a torn screen, a worn pump, or excessive drawdown, the underlying cause still needs to be addressed so the filter is not overwhelmed.

Can sand really ruin my pump?

Yes. Sand is abrasive and grinds down the impellers and seals in a submersible pump, causing it to lose efficiency and fail well before its normal lifespan. The sooner sand is controlled, the longer your pump lasts.
