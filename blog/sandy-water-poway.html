Sand in Your Poway Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Poway well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Poway well.

Sand and Sediment in Poway Well Water

If you have turned on a tap at your Poway home and noticed gritty particles in the glass, a fine layer of sand settling at the bottom of the bathtub, or a sputtering faucet that spits out grit, you are dealing with one of the most common and most misunderstood well problems in inland San Diego County. Sand in well water is rarely something that simply appears overnight without a cause. In Poway, "The City in the Country," the explanation almost always traces back to the ground beneath your feet. This part of north county sits on the decomposed granite and fractured crystalline bedrock of the Peninsular Ranges, and that geology has everything to do with why local wells produce sand.

Unlike a deep sandy alluvial basin where water is drawn from thick, clean layers of waterborne sand and gravel, Poway wells are completed in weathered granite. As granite breaks down over millennia it produces a coarse, gritty material called decomposed granite, or DG. Fine DG sand and grit migrate naturally toward a pumping well, and when conditions change in or around the well, that grit starts coming up with your water. Whether your home sits in a valley pocket off Espola Road, on a hillside near Iron Mountain, or up toward Mount Woodson, understanding why the sand is there is the first step to getting rid of it for good. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years diagnosing and fixing exactly this problem across Poway and the surrounding communities.

Why Poway Wells Produce Sand

The root cause of sandy water in Poway is the local geology. The Peninsular Ranges that run through this region are made of crystalline rock, and most private wells here are drilled into decomposed granite and fractured bedrock rather than a forgiving sand-and-gravel aquifer. Wells in the area typically run anywhere from about 200 to 500 feet deep or more, and the conditions vary dramatically depending on where your property sits. In the valley-bottom alluvial pockets around Green Valley, wells tend to be shallower, often in the 150 to 300 foot range, and noticeably more productive, sometimes yielding 10 to 20 gallons per minute or better. On the hard-rock ridges near Iron Mountain and Mount Woodson, wells are commonly drilled 400 to 500-plus feet through tough fractured granite and may yield only 1 to 3 gallons per minute. These low-yield hard-rock wells are especially prone to sand problems because there is little margin for error in how the pump is sized and set.

Within that geological context, here are the specific reasons a Poway well starts producing sand and grit:

  • A worn or failing pump pulling fines. As a submersible pump ages, worn impellers and seals create turbulence that lifts fine DG sand off the bottom of the well and carries it up with the water.
  • An oversized pump over-drawing a low-yield well. This is extremely common on Iron Mountain and Mount Woodson hillside wells. A pump that moves more water than the fractured granite can supply creates excessive drawdown and literally vacuums fine grit into the intake.
  • A failed or corroded well screen. Older Poway wells often have steel screens or perforated casing that corrode over time. Once the openings enlarge or the screen fails, the barrier that kept DG sand out is gone.
  • The wrong screen slot size for fine DG. Decomposed granite produces very fine particles. A screen with slots sized for coarse gravel will pass fine DG sand straight through into the well.
  • Excessive drawdown. When the water level in the well is pulled down too far, water velocity at the formation face increases and drags fines into the borehole.
  • A new well that has not been fully developed. A freshly drilled or newly serviced well needs proper development to flush fines out of the formation. Until that is done, some sand production is normal.
  • Drawing from a DG or sandy zone. If the pump intake sits within or just above a pocket of loose decomposed granite, it will keep pulling that material no matter how new the equipment is.

The contrast between Green Valley's alluvial pockets and the hard granite ridges is the key local nuance. A more productive Green Valley well that suddenly starts producing sand often points to a mechanical failure such as a corroded screen or a failing pump. A low-yield ridge well, on the other hand, frequently produces sand simply because it is being asked to deliver more water than the rock can give, and the fix involves matching the equipment to the well's true capacity.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is abrasive, and well systems are not built to tolerate it. Left unaddressed, even a small amount of grit causes expensive, cascading damage throughout your water system. The submersible pump is usually the first and most costly casualty. Sand grinds away at the impellers and bearings, and a pump that should last 15 years or more can fail in a fraction of that time. Once that pump is several hundred feet down a Poway well, replacing it is a significant job.

Beyond the pump, sand scores valves and seats throughout your plumbing, causing fixtures to leak and fail. It clogs sediment filters quickly, sending you through cartridges far faster than normal. Grit settles into the bottom of your pressure tank, reducing its usable capacity and causing the pump to cycle more often. It collects in the bottom of your water heater, where it bakes into a hard crust that reduces efficiency and shortens the heater's life. Sand abrades faucet aerators, damages the seals in your dishwasher and washing machine, and leaves a persistent gritty residue in tubs and sinks. Every one of these problems gets worse the longer sandy water circulates through the system, which is why prompt diagnosis pays for itself.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Poway

Fixing sandy water correctly starts with figuring out exactly where the sand is coming from and how much is being produced. A guess leads to a wrong repair, so we work through a methodical diagnostic process on every Poway service call:

  • Jar and settling test. We collect water at the wellhead and at the fixtures and let it settle in a clear container to measure how much sand is present and how fine the particles are. Very fine, uniform grit usually means DG; coarser material can point to a screen or casing failure.
  • Sand concentration measurement. We quantify the sand load so we can tell the difference between a minor nuisance and a level that will quickly destroy equipment.
  • Pump amp draw and set depth. We check the pump's electrical draw and confirm how deep it is set in the well. A pump set too low, near accumulated sediment, is a frequent culprit.
  • Pulling and inspecting the pump and screen. When needed, we pull the pump to inspect it and the screen or casing for wear, corrosion, and abrasion damage.
  • Downhole camera inspection. A camera survey lets us see the inside of the casing and screen, locate breaches, corrosion, or sediment buildup, and identify exactly which zone is contributing sand.
  • Flow and drawdown testing. We measure how far the water level drops at a given pumping rate to see whether the well is being over-pumped relative to its true yield, which is especially important on low-yield hard-rock wells.

How We Fix It

Once we know the source and severity of the sand, we match the solution to the well. There is no single fix for every Poway property; the right approach depends on whether the problem is mechanical, geological, or a matter of over-pumping. Our common solutions include:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters. A centrifugal sand separator spins grit out of the water before it reaches your pressure tank and plumbing, protecting the entire downstream system. This is often the right first line of defense for wells that produce fine DG.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration. A spin-down filter at the wellhead plus staged sediment filtration captures finer particles that a separator misses, keeping fixtures and appliances clean.
  • Proper pump sizing and placement above the sediment. We resize and reposition the pump so it draws from clean water above the sediment zone and does not over-draw the well. On hillside wells near Mount Woodson, right-sizing the pump to the rock's actual yield is frequently the single most important fix.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment. Surging, jetting, and airlifting clean the formation and screen, flush out accumulated fines, and restore a well that has not been properly developed or has filled with sediment over the years.
  • Screen repair or relining. Where a corroded or failed screen is letting DG sand through, we repair, replace, or reline the screen with the correct slot size for fine decomposed granite.
  • Adjusting output for low-yield DG wells. For wells that simply cannot sustain a high flow, we set a pumping rate the formation can handle, sometimes paired with storage, so the well delivers clean water without dragging in grit.

Preventing Sand Problems

The best way to deal with sandy water is to keep it from becoming a problem in the first place. Prevention on a Poway well comes down to respecting the geology and maintaining the equipment. Start with a right-sized pump matched to the well's true, tested yield rather than to wishful thinking about how much water the granite can deliver. Make sure any screen is correct for fine DG, with the appropriate slot size to hold back the fine particles this formation produces. Schedule periodic maintenance so worn pumps and aging screens are caught before they start lifting sand. Avoid over-pumping, especially during drought years when water levels drop and the temptation to push the well harder is greatest; over-drawing a stressed well is one of the fastest ways to start producing grit. Finally, an annual inspection that includes a quick water-quality check and a look at pump performance will flag a developing sand problem while it is still cheap to fix.

When to Call a Professional

Some homeowners are tempted to wait sandy water out, hoping it clears on its own. Occasionally a brand-new well will shed a little sand during break-in, but persistent or worsening grit is a signal to call a professional right away. If you see visible sand in your water, notice your pump short-cycling or running longer than it used to, find grit in the bottom of your pressure tank or water heater, or are burning through sediment filters, do not keep running the system. Every hour a pump runs in sandy water shortens its life and risks a far more expensive failure hundreds of feet underground. Sand can also be the first warning sign of a structural problem with the casing or screen that only proper downhole equipment can diagnose. A licensed well contractor has the tools to pull the pump, run a camera, and test the well safely. Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Poway well produce sand when my neighbor's does not?

Conditions change over short distances in Poway because of the area's fractured granite geology. Your neighbor may have a well in a more productive Green Valley alluvial pocket, a deeper or shallower completion, a newer screen, or a properly sized pump. A well drilled into a DG-rich zone or one being over-pumped will produce sand while a nearby well in cleaner rock does not.

Is sand in my well water dangerous to drink?

Fine sand and grit are generally not a direct health hazard, but they are hard on your body's patience and very hard on your plumbing. Sand can also accompany other water-quality changes, so if you notice grit it is worth having the water and the well checked rather than assuming it is harmless.

Will a whole-house filter alone fix sandy water from my well?

A filter or sand separator protects your home's plumbing and appliances, and it is often part of the solution. But it treats the symptom, not the cause. If the sand is coming from a failed screen, an over-pumped low-yield well near Mount Woodson, or a pump set in the sediment, those underlying issues still need to be addressed or you will keep replacing filters and risking pump damage.

I just had a new well drilled near Iron Mountain and it is producing sand. Is that normal?

A small amount of sand during the first weeks of a new well's life can be normal as the formation settles, especially in fractured granite. It should clear with proper development. If the sand persists or worsens, the well likely needs additional development, a pump adjustment, or a screen review. We can test it and tell you whether it is break-in sand or a real problem.

How deep are wells in Poway, and does depth affect sand problems?

Poway wells typically range from about 200 to 500 feet or more. Valley wells around Green Valley tend to be shallower and more productive, while hillside wells near Iron Mountain and Mount Woodson are often deeper through hard granite with low yields. Depth itself matters less than how the well is completed and pumped; low-yield deep wells are more prone to sand when over-drawn.

Can drought make my Poway well start producing sand?

Yes. During dry years the water level in many inland San Diego County wells drops, and a pump that worked fine in a wetter year may begin over-drawing the well, increasing drawdown and pulling in fine DG sand. This is one of the most common reasons we get sand calls during prolonged Southern California droughts.
