Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • High-capacity agricultural wells
  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Twin Peaks well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How Mountain Wells Work in the San Bernardino Range

Wells in the Twin Peaks area draw from fractured granitic and metamorphic bedrock. Water moves through cracks and joints, so a productive well depends on intersecting the right fractures rather than tapping a broad, even aquifer. A submersible pump set deep in the borehole feeds a pressure tank, regulated by a pressure switch. Because lots are steep and forested, the placement and protection of that surface equipment matters as much as the well itself.

The Winter Factor

The biggest difference between a mountain well and a valley well is cold. At this elevation, exposed pressure tanks, above-ground pipe runs, and pressure switches are all vulnerable to freezing. A great many winter no-water calls in Twin Peaks turn out to be a frozen or split line rather than a failed pump. Proper insulation, heat tape, and thoughtful equipment placement prevent most of these problems. For cabins that sit empty part of the year, winterization is essential: an unheated pump house can drop below freezing within hours of a power outage, and a single cracked fitting can flood a crawlspace the moment the water comes back on. We build freeze resilience into every mountain system we service, from the wellhead to the point where water enters the home.

Common Well Problems We See in Twin Peaks

  • Freeze damage: Split pipes, cracked tanks, and frozen switches after cold snaps.
  • Pressure-switch and tank failures: The most common year-round service call.
  • Pump wear: Submersible pumps eventually fail, especially where sediment or mineral content is high.
  • Low yield: Fractured-rock wells can decline during dry years.
  • Access challenges: Steep, snowy driveways complicate service and equipment delivery.

What to Check Before You Call

If you lose water in cold weather, first check whether exposed lines or the pressure tank are frozen — thawing a line may restore flow. Confirm the pump breaker has not tripped. Listen for rapid pump cycling at the tank. Note whether the loss is sudden or gradual, and whether it began after a freeze. These observations help us bring the right parts up the mountain on the first trip.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when you suspect a failed pump, when freeze damage has cracked a tank or fitting, or when pressure problems persist after the obvious checks. Electrical work around water, pulling a pump from a deep borehole, and diagnosing a fractured-rock yield problem all call for a licensed C-57 contractor with the right rig and testing tools.

Water Quality in High-Elevation Wells

Mountain groundwater is often excellent, but it is not automatically pure. Fractured granite and decomposed rock can contribute hardness, iron, and manganese, and surface influence is a real concern where fractures connect to shallow water after heavy snowmelt. Iron and manganese show up as orange or brown-black staining on fixtures; hardness scales up water heaters and shortens appliance life. We test mountain wells for these parameters and, where needed, install sediment filtration ($300 to $900) or an iron, manganese, or softening system ($1,500 to $3,500). Because snowmelt can change a well's chemistry seasonally, periodic testing is a smart habit for any Twin Peaks homeowner who relies on private water.

Shared Systems and Private Wells

Not every Twin Peaks property has its own well. Some homes draw from small shared mountain water systems, while others rely on a single private well serving one parcel. Each arrangement has its own quirks: shared systems raise questions of pressure balancing and maintenance responsibility, while private wells put the entire burden of upkeep on the homeowner. We work with both, helping owners understand what they have, what condition it is in, and what it will take to keep clean water flowing reliably through every season at this elevation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do avocados grow in Twin Peaks?

No. Twin Peaks sits around 5,700 to 5,800 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, where winter freezes and snow are routine. Avocados are a frost-sensitive subtropical crop and cannot survive this climate. Our work here is residential and small-property well service for mountain homes, cabins, and rural parcels rather than avocado groves.

How deep are wells in the Twin Peaks area?

Mountain wells in the Lake Arrowhead and Twin Peaks area are typically drilled into fractured granitic and metamorphic bedrock. Depths vary widely with terrain, commonly from 200 to 600 feet, and yield depends on intersecting water-bearing fractures rather than a uniform aquifer.

Why does my mountain well freeze or lose pressure in winter?

At this elevation, exposed pressure tanks, above-ground piping, and pressure switches can freeze if they are not insulated or heat-taped. Loss of pressure in cold weather is often a frozen or split line rather than a pump failure. We winterize systems and repair freeze damage.

What does a new well cost in the San Bernardino Mountains?

Drilling in mountainous granite with limited access generally puts a turnkey well in the $18,000 to $42,000 range. Steep driveways, snow, and rock hardness influence the price. We assess access before quoting.

Can you reach Twin Peaks for emergency service?

Yes. We serve the San Bernardino Mountain communities and offer same-day emergency response when conditions allow. In winter, plowed access and chains may affect timing, so call early when a problem appears.

My cabin sits empty part of the year. How should I protect the well system?

Seasonal cabins benefit from proper winterization: insulating the pressure tank and exposed lines, adding heat tape, and in some cases draining vulnerable sections. We can set up a system that tolerates being left unattended through freezing weather.
