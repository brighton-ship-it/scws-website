What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Rialto Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Rialto

Rialto sits in the heart of the Inland Empire in western San Bernardino County, tucked between the San Gabriel Mountains to the north and the cities of Fontana, Colton, and San Bernardino on every other side. If you are reading this because a private well on your Rialto property is producing less water than it used to, the honest first thing to understand is what lies beneath your feet. Most of Rialto rests on the Rialto-Colton groundwater basin — roughly 40 square miles of deep, unconsolidated alluvial sand, gravel, and partly consolidated continental deposits washed down from the surrounding mountains and bounded by the Rialto-Colton and San Jacinto faults. That geology matters a great deal when you are deciding whether hydrofracturing is the right repair for your well.

We want to be straight with Rialto property owners from the start: hydrofracturing is a fractured-bedrock technique. It works by reopening cracks in hard crystalline rock. The Rialto-Colton basin is the opposite of that — it is a thick, water-bearing sand-and-gravel aquifer that supplies a large share of the Inland Empire's drinking water. For a well screened in that alluvium, hydrofracturing is usually a weak fit, and we will tell you so rather than sell you a treatment that physics will not reward. The good news is that there are usually better, more affordable fixes for the symptoms you are seeing, and we perform those too.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing (sometimes called hydrofracking, though it has nothing to do with oil and gas) is a well-yield enhancement method for wells drilled into fractured granite or other crystalline bedrock. A licensed crew lowers inflatable packers into the borehole to seal off a target section, then injects clean water at high pressure — commonly 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. That pressure pushes into the natural hairline fractures in the rock, flushes out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that has been choking them, and widens the network of cracks so groundwater can travel more freely into the well. No chemicals are used; it is just water and pressure, which is why it does not contaminate the drinking supply.

The technique shines on hard-rock wells where water arrives through fractures rather than through porous sediment. That is exactly why it is so effective up in the granite foothills and mountains of the county — and exactly why it rarely helps a sand-aquifer well down on the valley floor in Rialto. Understanding that distinction up front saves you from paying for a treatment that the formation around your well cannot reward.

It also helps to understand how Rialto's basin recharges. Water enters the Rialto-Colton system from mountain-front runoff out of the San Gabriels, seepage from the Santa Ana River and Warm Creek, imported water, and infiltrating rainfall. Because the basin is fault-bounded, groundwater does not flow freely in and out, which means local pumping, drought cycles, and recharge timing all influence what a sand-aquifer well produces in a given year. A well that seems to be "failing" may simply be reflecting a seasonal drop in the water table rather than a problem with the well itself — another reason a proper diagnostic beats a rushed treatment.

When Hydrofracturing Helps in the Rialto Area — and When It Doesn't

The deciding factor is your well log, not your address. A few scenarios:

  • Your well is screened in alluvial sand and gravel (typical for the Rialto-Colton basin). Hydrofracturing is usually not the answer. There is no fracture network to reopen. A declining sand-aquifer well is far more likely to respond to screen rehabilitation, a deeper pump setting, well deepening, or a regional water-table decline that no single fix can fully solve.
  • Your well was drilled into hard rock along the mountain-front edges of the basin. A small number of Rialto-area parcels near the San Gabriel foothills and toward Lytle Creek do reach crystalline bedrock. For those, hydrofracturing can be a strong candidate.
  • You are on municipal water and considering a backup or irrigation well. Most of Rialto is served by the City of Rialto and the West Valley Water District, so private domestic wells are uncommon here. If you are weighing a new well, we will help you understand realistic yields before you commit.

If you are not sure which describes your well, that is normal — and it is exactly what our diagnostic visit is for. The wrong assumption can cost thousands, so we start with evidence.

The Step-by-Step Process

  1. Diagnostic visit and well-log review. We read your driller's report (or pull it from state records), confirm whether your well is in rock or sediment, and measure current yield. Our diagnostic is $125, credited toward any work we perform.
  2. Downhole camera inspection. A video survey shows casing condition, perforation or screen blockage, and whether fracture zones are present at all.
  3. Pre-treatment flow test. We establish a baseline gallons-per-minute figure so any improvement can be measured objectively rather than guessed at.
  4. Packer isolation and injection (rock wells only). If hydrofracturing is appropriate, we isolate the target zone with inflatable packers and inject water at pressure for several hours.
  5. Post-treatment flow test and report. We re-measure yield, usually within 24–48 hours, and hand you a documented before-and-after comparison so you can see exactly what changed.

Realistic Results and Honest Expectations

On a genuine fractured-rock well, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70–80% of cases, often doubling to quintupling pre-treatment flow. On a Rialto sand-aquifer well, those numbers simply do not apply, and any contractor who quotes them for valley-floor alluvium is overpromising. We would rather lose the job than treat a well that cannot benefit. When hydrofracturing is not the fit, we will point you toward the repair that is — whether that is a new pump, a screen cleaning, or a deeper well.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Locate your well completion report — it tells us the depth and whether you are in rock or sand.
  • Note your well depth; Rialto-area domestic wells in the basin are frequently several hundred feet deep into alluvium.
  • Watch the pattern: does the well sputter only during summer peak demand, or has output declined steadily year over year?
  • Check whether your pump or pressure switch is the real culprit — many "low water" calls turn out to be equipment problems, not the aquifer.
  • Listen for a pump that short-cycles on and off, which often signals a waterlogged pressure tank rather than a failing well.

When to Call a Professional

Call us if your well runs dry under normal use, recovers slowly after pumping, has lost noticeable pressure compared with past years, or if your pump short-cycles. These symptoms can point to the aquifer, the screen, the pump, or the pressure tank — and the cure is different in each case. A licensed C-57 contractor can tell them apart with a camera and a flow test instead of guessing, which protects you from spending money in the wrong place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing help my Rialto well?

Only if your well is drilled into fractured bedrock. Most Rialto wells sit in the sand-and-gravel Rialto-Colton basin, where hydrofracturing usually does not help. We confirm this with your well log before recommending anything.

What should I do instead if my sand-aquifer well is weak?

Common fixes include screen rehabilitation, lowering or replacing the pump, deepening the well, or addressing a falling regional water table. We will identify which applies during the diagnostic visit.

Is hydrofracturing safe for my water?

Yes. It uses only clean, high-pressure water — no chemicals — so it does not contaminate your drinking supply.

How long does the process take?

On a suitable rock well, the injection itself takes about 4–8 hours, with verification flow testing over the following day or two.

Do most Rialto homes even have wells?

No — most of Rialto is on municipal water through the city and West Valley Water District. Private wells are more common on the rural and foothill edges of town.
