Low Water Pressure on San Diego County Wells: A Regional Guide

San Diego County is one of the most geologically varied counties in California, and that variety shows up directly in how private wells behave. A homeowner on the granitic backcountry slopes above Ramona faces very different pressure problems than a grove owner in Pauma Valley, a mountain property in Julian or the Pine Valley and Descanso corridor, or a desert household out in Borrego Springs east of the mountains. If your faucets have slowed to a trickle, your shower has lost its punch, or your irrigation no longer reaches the far end of the property, the cause almost always traces back to one of a handful of components in your well system, the aquifer feeding it, or the geology in between.

How Your Well and Pressure System Actually Works

Most San Diego County homes on wells share the same basic setup, and understanding it makes diagnosing low pressure far easier. A submersible pump sits deep in the well casing, often hundreds of feet down in the fractured-granite backcountry or in deeper desert aquifers like Borrego. That pump pushes water up to a pressure tank with an internal rubber bladder. The bladder holds a cushion of compressed air so the pump does not have to run every time you open a tap.

A pressure switch tells the pump when to turn on and off, typically cycling between about 40 psi and 60 psi (a common 40/60 setting). When you use water and the tank pressure drops to 40, the pump kicks on; when it climbs back to 60, the pump shuts off. If any link in that chain falters, the pump, the tank, the switch, or the plumbing and treatment downstream, you feel it as low or fluctuating pressure at the fixtures.

Common Causes of Low Pressure Across the County

Backcountry fractured-granite wells (Ramona, Julian, Alpine, Jamul, Descanso)

Much of inland San Diego County sits on decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock. Water in these formations moves through narrow fractures rather than a generous sandy aquifer, so many backcountry wells are naturally low-yield. When demand outpaces what the fractures can deliver, pressure sags, the pump runs long, and in dry years the water level can drop below the pump intake. Hydrofracturing, pressurizing the borehole to open existing fractures, can meaningfully improve yield on these wells and is often a smart alternative to drilling deeper or starting over.

Coastal and inland-valley wells (Fallbrook, Bonsall, Valley Center, Pauma Valley)

In the grove country of the north county and the coastal mesas, wells often draw from alluvial deposits and weathered granite. Here, mineral-rich water is common: hard-water scale, iron, and manganese gradually clog screens, foul pumps, and narrow the interior of pipes. Sediment intrusion after heavy rain is also a frequent pressure-killer.

Desert wells (Borrego Springs)

East of the mountains, Borrego Springs draws from a deep desert aquifer. These wells tend to be deep, and long-term basin decline means pump settings and equipment have to be matched carefully to changing water levels. A pressure problem out here is often really a water-level or pump-depth problem.

Causes that show up everywhere

  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: a ruptured bladder makes the pump cycle rapidly and pressure swing wildly.
  • Failing pressure switch: worn contacts or a clogged sensing port cause erratic cut-in and cut-out.
  • Worn or undersized pump: after years of service, impellers wear and output falls off.
  • Clogged sediment filter or treatment system: a neglected filter cartridge can strangle your whole house.
  • Mineral scale inside galvanized pipe: common in older rural homes with hard water.
  • Stuck check valve or a leak between the well and the house.

What You Can Safely Check Yourself

There are a few things a homeowner can inspect before calling a professional:

  • Read the pressure gauge at the tank. If it never reaches the cut-out pressure (around 60 psi) or swings rapidly, note what you see.
  • Check whether low pressure affects the whole house or just one fixture. A single slow faucet usually means a clogged aerator or fixture, not a well problem.
  • Inspect and replace a dirty sediment filter cartridge if your system has one.
  • Tap the pressure tank gently: a healthy tank sounds hollow on top and solid (water-filled) on the bottom. A tank that feels solid all the way up is likely waterlogged.
  • Look and listen for the pump short-cycling (clicking on and off every few seconds), which points to a tank or switch problem.

Important safety warning: the pressure switch and pump circuits carry 230 volts. Do not open the switch cover, adjust the springs, or touch any wiring. That voltage can be lethal, and a misadjusted switch can damage your pump. Leave electrical work and pressure-switch adjustment to a licensed professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my backcountry well lose pressure in late summer?

Fractured-granite wells around Ramona, Julian, and Alpine recharge slowly. By late summer the water level in the fracture system can drop, so the pump draws down faster than the rock can refill. A yield test tells us whether the well simply needs a lower pump setting, a holding tank, or hydrofracturing to open more fractures.

Is hydrofracturing worth it for a low-yield well?

Often yes. On fractured-granite wells common in San Diego County's backcountry, hydrofracturing frequently improves flow by opening and connecting existing fractures, at a fraction of the cost of drilling a new well. We evaluate the well first to confirm it is a good candidate.

My pump runs constantly but pressure is still low. What is wrong?

This usually means the pump cannot keep up, either because the well yield has dropped, the pump is worn, or there is a leak or stuck check valve in the line. It can also be a waterlogged pressure tank. A diagnostic visit isolates the cause quickly.

Could my low pressure be caused by hard water and minerals?

Yes. In Fallbrook, Bonsall, Valley Center, and Pauma Valley especially, mineral scale, iron, and manganese build up inside pipes, pump components, and filters, gradually choking flow. Proper filtration or softening restores pressure and protects your equipment.

How is a Borrego Springs desert well different to service?

Borrego wells are typically deep and draw from a declining desert aquifer, so pump depth and sizing matter enormously. Low pressure there is frequently a water-level issue rather than an equipment failure, and we set pumps to match current conditions.
