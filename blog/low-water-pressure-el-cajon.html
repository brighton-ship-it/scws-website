How a Well Water Pressure System Works

Four components keep your water flowing. The pump lifts groundwater from the aquifer. A pressure tank stores it under compressed air so the pump does not run every time you open a tap. The pressure switch starts the pump at its cut-in pressure and stops it at cut-out, commonly a 40/60 PSI range. The water delivered between those points is the drawdown. When drawdown shrinks, the pump short-cycles and faucet pressure turns weak and surging. Identifying which part is failing is what makes a repair last.

El Cajon Geology and Why Pressure Suffers

The El Cajon Valley is a broad basin floored by alluvial fill, mixtures of sand, gravel, and silt, over the decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock typical of San Diego County. Those alluvial layers hold groundwater in unconfined aquifers, often at depths of 100 to 300 feet, but the surrounding hard rock limits how quickly the basin recharges. During the region's frequent droughts, water tables in the valley drop, reducing yield and pressure. Because the valley collects runoff from the encircling hills, that runoff can carry sediment and minerals into wells, and iron bacteria are common locally. Fractured granite bedrock stores water in cracks but delivers it inconsistently, especially in dry months when irrigation demand spikes across El Cajon's residential and agricultural mix. This geology calls for solutions matched to the valley's specific subsurface.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in El Cajon

  • Pump scale from hard water: Calcium and magnesium from granite and alluvial deposits build up on pump components, cutting efficiency and pressure.
  • Pressure tank problems: Mineral-rich water corrodes tanks or clogs internal diaphragms, and older systems lose air charge and short-cycle.
  • Low yield during drought: Limited basin recharge means dry seasons frequently pull down groundwater levels and pressure.
  • Sediment in pipes and filters: Runoff-borne sand and silt, plus iron-bacteria biofilm, accumulate in plumbing and screens, restricting flow.
  • Aging or undersized infrastructure: Older distribution lines not matched to current demand lose pressure between the well and the home or irrigation system.

What to Check Before You Call

A quick self-check helps. Test pressure across fixtures: weak flow everywhere points to the pump or tank, while a single weak tap suggests a clog. Watch the pump for rapid cycling (tank or switch) versus constant running without pressure (mechanical failure or low yield). Look at the water: brown or gritty water points to sediment from valley runoff. Check the tank air charge with a tire gauge, pump off and tank drained, about 2 PSI below cut-in. Note the season, since a summer-only drop usually means yield. These details let us focus the diagnosis fast.

Preventive Maintenance for El Cajon Wells

Preventive care extends the life of your system in El Cajon's challenging valley environment. Schedule an annual inspection of the pump, tank, and pressure switch to catch issues early. Check the tank air charge monthly with a tire gauge, keeping it about 2 PSI below cut-in. Flush sediment regularly by opening a drain valve near the tank every few months to clear valley runoff, while staying mindful of drought conservation. Test water quality yearly for minerals, sediment, and bacteria so clogs and corrosion are addressed before they cut pressure. And protect electrical components with a surge protector on the pump controls to guard against the power fluctuations common in semi-rural El Cajon.

How deep are El Cajon wells and what do they yield?

Many El Cajon wells draw from alluvial fill in the valley floor at depths of roughly 100 to 300 feet, with typical yields around 5 to 12 gallons per minute. Surrounding hard granite limits recharge, so drought can lower yields significantly; a drawdown test confirms what your well can sustain.

Why is there sediment in my El Cajon well water?

Runoff from the granite hills around the valley carries sand and silt into the basin's groundwater, and iron bacteria are also common locally. Both can settle in pipes, filters, and well screens, restricting flow. Sediment filtration ($300-$900) and a screen cleaning usually resolve it.

My El Cajon well pressure drops in summer. Why?
