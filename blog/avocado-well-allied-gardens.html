Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • High-capacity agricultural wells
  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Allied Gardens well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well and Irrigation Realities for Allied Gardens Homeowners

Allied Gardens is a well-loved residential neighborhood in the eastern part of San Diego's Navajo community, bordered by San Carlos, Del Cerro, the College Area, and Grantville along Mission Gorge Road. Developed in the mid-1950s on land purchased from the Waring estate, it is a classic San Diego suburb of single-family homes on modest lots. That history matters when we talk about avocado wells, because it means one honest thing up front: Allied Gardens is on city water, and private agricultural wells here are extremely rare. This is part of the City of San Diego's municipal system, not farm country.

The genuine San Diego County avocado belt, including Fallbrook, Bonsall, De Luz, Pauma Valley, and Valley Center, lies far to the north. In Allied Gardens, the avocado story is a backyard one: a homeowner with a couple of Hass or Bacon trees in the garden, watered from the tap on a drip timer. We think you deserve straight talk rather than keyword-stuffed promises, so this guide focuses on the well and irrigation services that are actually relevant to homeowners in this neighborhood and the wider Navajo area.

How Irrigation and the Rare Private Well Work Here

For the vast majority of Allied Gardens homes, irrigation is simply a matter of an efficient drip or micro-spray system fed from the municipal supply, ideally on a smart controller to cope with San Diego's water-conscious climate. For the uncommon larger parcel that has a private well, the system works like any other: a submersible pump lifts water from the borehole, a pressure tank and switch maintain steady pressure, and filtration or softening conditions the water as needed. Because San Diego's backcountry geology is decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, the few wells in the broader region tend to be moderate-yield fractured-rock wells, so storage tanks are often added to buffer demand on a larger landscaped lot.

Common Local Scenarios

When we are called to the Allied Gardens and Navajo area, it is usually one of these:

  • Homeowners on city water wanting an efficient irrigation upgrade for fruit trees or landscaping.
  • A legacy well on a larger or older parcel that needs a pump or pressure-tank repair.
  • Owners researching a well for irrigation savings, who need an honest feasibility answer.
  • Hard water and scale affecting any private water system, shortening pump life.
  • Iron, sediment, or pressure problems on existing well equipment.

Frost-protection irrigation is not a concern in this mild urban microclimate, so our focus is efficiency, water quality, and sound equipment.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Water source: Confirm whether you are on city water or an actual private well, since it changes everything we recommend.
  • Pressure behavior: Note weak pressure or rapid pump cycling on any private system.
  • Water quality: Look for rust staining, cloudiness, or scale.
  • Irrigation coverage: Check for clogged emitters and dry spots in the landscape.
  • Records: Any well log or pump documentation, if a well exists, helps us move quickly.

When to Call a Professional

Call us if you have a private well that loses water or pressure, if water quality changes, or if you simply want a no-pressure assessment of whether a well makes sense for your property. Pump and well work is high-voltage and involves heavy downhole equipment, so leave it to a licensed C-57 contractor. We will give you an honest answer, even if that answer is to stay on city water.

Realistic Cost Ranges

For the work that comes up in this area: a pressure switch is $150-$350; a pressure tank $600-$1,500; sediment filtration $300-$900; an iron, manganese, or softening system $1,500-$3,500; and a constant-pressure or booster system $2,000-$4,500. A new turnkey well, which is seldom justified on an urban San Diego lot, ranges $18,000-$42,000, and decommissioning an old well is $1,500-$5,000.

Smart Irrigation for City-Water Homes and Backyard Trees

Most of what Allied Gardens homeowners actually want is healthy landscaping and maybe a few productive fruit trees, including the backyard avocado, without wasting water in San Diego's dry climate. That is a solvable problem on municipal water. A well-designed drip and micro-spray system on a smart controller delivers water to the root zone, adjusts for weather, and dramatically cuts waste compared with old spray heads. For backyard avocado or citrus, consistent moisture during fruit set, good drainage, and attention to water quality matter far more than the source of the water. If you do have a private water system anywhere on the property, we keep it efficient with the right pressure equipment and, where a water test justifies it, sediment or hardness treatment. The aim is a green, productive yard that respects both your water bill and the region's supply, with honest advice at every step.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do properties in Allied Gardens have wells for avocado irrigation?

Almost none do. Allied Gardens is an established residential neighborhood in the City of San Diego, part of the Navajo community alongside Del Cerro, San Carlos, and Grantville. Homes here are on the municipal water system, and private agricultural wells are essentially unheard of on these compact 1950s-era lots. Our well work in this part of San Diego County is for the rare large parcel, a legacy well elsewhere on a homeowner's property, or owners researching whether a well is even an option.

Can avocado trees grow in Allied Gardens?

Yes, as backyard trees. The mild inland-coastal San Diego climate in the Navajo area near Mission Gorge supports a few Hass or Bacon trees in a home garden. But this is ornamental and hobby growing on city water, not the commercial grove farming found in the avocado belt around Fallbrook, Bonsall, and Valley Center.

Would a private well make sense for my Allied Gardens home?

For a standard city lot, rarely. The cost of drilling, a pump, and ongoing maintenance usually outweighs the savings versus municipal water at residential volumes, and urban lots seldom have room or favorable geology. We give an honest assessment rather than pushing a well you do not need.

If I do have a well on a larger San Diego parcel, what can go wrong?

The usual issues are aging pumps and pressure tanks, low yield in San Diego's decomposed-granite and fractured-rock geology, hard water and mineral scale, and iron or sediment. We diagnose and repair all of these.
