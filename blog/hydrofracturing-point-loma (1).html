What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Point Loma Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Point Loma wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Water Wells and Hydrofracturing in Point Loma: An Honest Assessment

Point Loma is a hilly coastal peninsula in San Diego County, defined by the marine sedimentary rock of the Upper Cretaceous Point Loma Formation — layered sandstone, siltstone, and conglomerate that forms the dramatic bluffs above the Pacific. Neighborhoods such as La Playa, Roseville, Loma Portal, and Wooded Area are almost entirely served by City of San Diego municipal water through the Public Utilities Department. That single fact shapes everything about wells here: private domestic wells are uncommon, and the geology underfoot is not the fractured hard rock where hydrofracturing shines. We want to be straightforward with Point Loma property owners — in most cases here, hydrofracturing is not the right answer, and there are usually better places to put your money.

This guide explains when hydrofracturing might still apply, why Point Loma's setting makes it a weak fit, and what alternatives typically serve coastal San Diego properties far more effectively.

What Hydrofracturing Is — and Why It Needs the Right Rock

Hydrofracturing uses clean water injected under high pressure to reopen and clean the natural fractures in bedrock that feed a well. It is a fracture treatment, which means it only helps where the aquifer is a network of cracks in hard crystalline or well-consolidated rock. In the granitic country of the inland mountains, that describes most wells. In Point Loma, the productive geology is marine sedimentary rock and overlying alluvium near San Diego Bay — formations that store and move water through pore space and bedding rather than through an extensive open-fracture system. Without a strong fracture network to target, high-pressure injection has little to act on, and the technique simply does not deliver the gains it does inland.

There is also the practical reality of the neighborhood. Lots are tight, homes are historic, and the overwhelming majority of properties are already on reliable municipal supply. For most Point Loma owners, the question is not "how do I boost a struggling well" but "do I even need a well, or is there a simpler water problem to solve?"

Better Options for Point Loma Properties

If you are dealing with a water issue on a Point Loma property, the following approaches almost always make more sense than fracturing:

  • Municipal connection or repair. If your concern is supply reliability, connecting to or repairing your City of San Diego service is typically the cleanest, most cost-effective fix.
  • Deepening an existing well. For the rare older well that has lost yield, deepening to reach a more productive zone can outperform any fracture attempt in sedimentary rock.
  • A new, properly sited well. When a well is genuinely needed — often for landscape irrigation on the larger La Playa or Wooded Area lots — a new well designed for the local formation is more dependable than fracturing an old one.
  • Well rehabilitation. Mechanical cleaning, surging, and pump servicing restore many underperforming wells whose real problem is buildup or worn equipment, not aquifer limitation.
  • Water treatment. If the trouble is quality rather than quantity — salinity from the coastal setting, sediment, or hardness — targeted treatment solves it directly.

Common Point Loma Water Scenarios

  • Irrigation-only well on a large lot. An older La Playa or Roseville property may have a legacy well used for landscaping. If flow has dropped, rehabilitation or a pump upgrade usually restores it; fracturing the sedimentary rock rarely does.
  • Brackish or hard water. Proximity to the bay and ocean can introduce salinity and minerals. This is a treatment problem, addressed with filtration or softening, not a yield problem.
  • Low pressure on municipal supply. Many "low water" complaints on the peninsula trace back to household plumbing or pressure regulation, not the source at all.
  • Sediment after a storm. Coastal bluff settings can move fines into older wells; sediment filtration and redevelopment handle this cleanly.

What to Check Before You Call

Start by confirming your water source. Many Point Loma owners are surprised to learn they have no active well at all and are entirely on city water. If you do have a well, locate its completion report to see the depth and the formation logged. Note whether your problem is pressure (likely plumbing or pressure equipment), volume (possibly source or pump), or quality (a treatment matter). Check the pressure tank and switch, since failing pressure components are a frequent and inexpensive culprit. Gathering these details lets us point you toward the genuinely useful fix rather than an unnecessary one.

When to Call a Professional

Call a licensed contractor whenever you are unsure what is actually wrong, before spending on any major work, or when an old well needs evaluation for rehabilitation, deepening, or proper decommissioning. A professional assessment protects you from paying for a hydrofracturing treatment that the local geology will not reward. We would rather tell a Point Loma owner the truth — that fracturing is the wrong tool here — than take a job that won't help.

Why Coastal Geology Changes the Calculus

It is worth understanding why the same technique that transforms an inland granite well does so little on the Point Loma peninsula. Hard crystalline rock holds water almost exclusively in fractures, so widening and cleaning those fractures directly increases the volume of rock the well can draw from. Marine sedimentary formations like the Point Loma Formation behave differently: water moves through the spaces between grains and along bedding planes, and the rock is comparatively soft and poorly fractured. Injecting high-pressure water into that setting tends to dissipate into the formation without opening a productive, connected fracture system. On top of that, coastal aquifers carry the added concern of saltwater intrusion, where aggressive pumping or fracturing near the shore can draw seawater toward the well and degrade quality. For these reasons, the responsible recommendation for the overwhelming majority of Point Loma properties is to look first at municipal supply, equipment, rehabilitation, or treatment — and to reserve drilling or major source work for the rare cases that genuinely justify it.

Realistic Costs for Point Loma Water Work

Because hydrofracturing is rarely appropriate in Point Loma, the costs that matter most here are the alternatives. A pressure switch replacement runs $150–$350 and a pressure tank $600–$1,500 — these solve a surprising share of "water" complaints. A submersible pump replacement is $2,500–$5,500. Sediment filtration runs $300–$900, while iron/manganese treatment or a softener for hard or brackish water runs $1,500–$3,500. A constant-pressure or booster system is $2,000–$4,500. Where a new well is truly warranted, a turnkey project runs $18,000–$42,000, and properly decommissioning an abandoned well runs $1,500–$5,000. For reference, hydrofracturing itself runs $3,000–$8,000 — but in Point Loma we will usually advise against it. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward any work you choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you hydrofracture a well in Point Loma?

Technically yes, but we usually advise against it. Point Loma's marine sedimentary rock lacks the fracture network that makes hydrofracturing effective, so the treatment rarely produces meaningful gains here.

I have low water pressure — is hydrofracturing the fix?

Almost never on the peninsula. Low pressure is far more often a pressure tank, pressure switch, pump, or plumbing issue. We diagnose the real cause before recommending anything.

Do most Point Loma homes even have wells?

No. The great majority are on City of San Diego municipal water. Some larger, older lots retain legacy irrigation wells, but private domestic wells are uncommon in this part of San Diego County.

My old irrigation well slowed down — what should I do?

Start with rehabilitation and a pump evaluation. Mechanical cleaning and equipment service restore many older wells. If the well is failed, deepening or replacement is more reliable than fracturing the local rock.

My water tastes salty or hard. Can that be fixed?

Yes — that is a water-quality issue addressed with treatment such as filtration or softening, not a yield problem. We can recommend the right system for coastal water chemistry.

What if I need a brand-new well?

We design and drill wells suited to the local formation. For most Point Loma needs — typically irrigation — a properly sited new well outperforms any attempt to fracture an old one in sedimentary rock.
