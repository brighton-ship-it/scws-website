How a Private Well and Irrigation System Works

A well in this part of the coast is a full system rather than just a borehole. A submersible pump lifts water from the producing zone up to a pressure tank, and a pressure switch cycles the pump on and off so your plumbing and irrigation see steady pressure. From the tank, water feeds the house and the irrigation manifold that supplies drip lines, micro-sprayers, and spray heads. On larger landscaped lots, a storage tank lets a modest well recharge slowly overnight and then deliver strong morning flow when the controller fires every zone at once, which matters during the hottest weeks of the year.

Geologically, Carmel Valley sits on coastal terrace deposits and marine sedimentary formations, transitioning toward the fractured rock common farther inland. The defining concern for any well this close to the ocean is salinity. Coastal and low-lying wells can draw brackish or salt-influenced water, and dissolved salts are hard on ornamentals, citrus, and the occasional backyard avocado, so honest water testing matters more here than a quick sales pitch.

Common Local Scenarios We Get Called For

Because Carmel Valley is largely on municipal supply, our well calls tend to follow a few familiar patterns:

  • Irrigation wells losing pressure mid-season, usually a worn pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch that leaves sprinklers sputtering.
  • Salty or mineral-heavy water staining hardscape, burning leaf tips, and leaving scale on emitters.
  • Older domestic wells on legacy parcels that need a pump pull, a flow test, or full rehabilitation.
  • Sediment and cloudy water after a wet winter shifts coastal soils and fines into the casing.
  • Decommissioning of an abandoned well on a parcel being redeveloped, which San Diego County requires be done to code.

What You Can Check Yourself Before Calling

A few quick observations help us diagnose faster and sometimes save you a service call:

  1. Listen to the pump. Rapid on-off cycling almost always means a pressure tank that has lost its air charge or a failing switch.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Most residential systems hold 40 to 60 psi; a needle that swings wildly or never builds is a warning sign.
  3. Check the breaker. Coastal moisture and salt air corrode electrical connections, and a tripped breaker is a common, inexpensive culprit.
  4. Look at the water. Note any new color, smell, grit, or staining and when it began; that timeline narrows the diagnosis.
  5. Walk your irrigation zones. If only some emitters are weak, the problem may be salt scale or clogging rather than the well.

When to Call a Professional

Anything that involves pulling the pump, opening the wellhead, handling 240-volt wiring, or interpreting a water-quality lab report is work for a licensed contractor. If you have lost water entirely, smell sulfur, see a sudden change in salinity, or the pump runs constantly without building pressure, call us rather than guessing. A wrong move on a submersible pump can drop equipment down the casing and turn a modest repair into a major one. We offer same-day emergency service for no-water situations.

Coastal Water Quality and Treatment

Near the coast, salinity and total dissolved solids are the water-quality issues that matter most. Salt-influenced water leaves white residue on hardscape, scorches the tips of salt-sensitive plants, and plugs drip emitters with scale over time. We start with a water test for chloride, TDS, and hardness so any treatment matches what is actually in your water. Depending on the result, the right answer might be a softener, a reverse-osmosis or blending strategy for sensitive plantings, or simply a filtration and maintenance plan. Getting the chemistry right protects both your landscape investment and the life of your pump and irrigation equipment, and it keeps you from paying for treatment you do not need.

Seasonal Maintenance Keeps Costs Down

The least expensive repair is the one you prevent. We recommend an annual check of the pressure tank air charge and the pressure switch, a look at the wellhead seal and the salt-prone electrical connections, and regular filter changes for any treatment equipment. On irrigation systems, walking the zones a couple of times each season catches clogged emitters and broken heads before they make the pump work harder than it should. For properties that rely entirely on a single well, a periodic flow test confirms the well is still yielding what your landscape and household need before a dry summer puts it to the test. A modest maintenance habit almost always costs far less than an emergency replacement during peak season.

Why Local Coastal Expertise Matters

Wells near the San Diego coast behave differently from inland wells, and a contractor who understands that distinction will save you money and frustration. Salinity can rise or fall with the seasons and with how hard a well is pumped, so the right approach is often as much about how the system is operated as about the equipment installed. An out-of-area crew that treats a coastal well like a backcountry well can over-pump it, draw in worse water, or recommend treatment that does not address the real issue. Because we work across both coastal and inland San Diego County all year, we can read the symptoms accurately, size the pump and storage correctly, and recommend the treatment that actually fits your water. Frequently Asked Questions

Is Carmel Valley on well water?

The neighborhood is predominantly served by municipal water, but private and irrigation wells remain on larger lots and nearby parcels such as Del Mar Mesa and Torrey Highlands, and that is the work we focus on here.

Why does my coastal well water taste or smell salty?

Wells close to the coast can draw salt-influenced water with elevated chloride and total dissolved solids. We test the water and recommend blending, treatment, or filtration to protect your plants, plumbing, and equipment.

How deep are wells near the Carmel Valley coast?

Depths vary with location and geology, from a couple hundred feet on the terrace deposits to deeper completions inland. We evaluate each parcel individually rather than assuming a fixed depth.

Can I run my landscape on a well to lower water bills?

Often yes. An irrigation-only well with a storage tank and booster pump is a popular way to keep a large coastal landscape green without paying municipal rates for every gallon, as long as the water chemistry suits your plants.
