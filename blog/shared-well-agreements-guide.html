Shared Well Agreements Guide

Protecting Your Water Rights With Neighbors

SC

By SCWS Team

Published February 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Sharing a well with neighbors can work beautifully—or become a nightmare. The difference usually comes down to having a clear, comprehensive written agreement before problems arise. Here's what every shared well agreement should address.

Essential Agreement Elements

  • ✅ Clear ownership definition (well, pump, tank, pipes)
  • ✅ Recorded easements for access
  • ✅ Cost-sharing formula
  • ✅ Usage limits or allocation method
  • ✅ Maintenance responsibilities
  • ✅ Dispute resolution process
  • ✅ Property transfer provisions
  • ✅ Liability and insurance requirements

Why Written Agreements Matter

Handshake agreements between neighbors work fine—until they don't. Properties sell, people move, circumstances change. Without a written agreement, new owners may have different expectations. Courts have little to work with when disputes arise.

A proper shared well agreement is recorded with the county and runs with the land, binding future owners to the same terms. This protects everyone's investment and prevents future conflicts.

Key Agreement Provisions

Ownership and Easements

Clearly define who owns what: the well itself, the pump and equipment, pressure tanks, water lines to each property. Grant and record easements for physical access to the well site and for water lines crossing property boundaries.

Cost Sharing

Specify how costs are divided:

  • Equal shares: Each party pays the same regardless of usage
  • Proportional: Based on number of units, acreage, or households
  • Metered: Based on actual water consumption
  • Fixed fees: Monthly or annual fees into a reserve fund

Address both routine costs (electricity, minor maintenance) and major repairs (pump replacement, well rehabilitation). Consider requiring a reserve fund for emergencies.

Usage Limits

If the well has limited capacity, specify how water is allocated. Options include equal shares, seasonal restrictions (irrigation limits), or priority systems. Without limits, one party's excessive use can deprive others.

Maintenance and Decisions

Define who makes decisions about repairs, upgrades, and contractors. Specify voting procedures (unanimous, majority), notice requirements, and emergency authority. One party shouldn't be able to unilaterally make major changes.

Dispute Resolution

Include a dispute resolution process: informal negotiation first, then mediation, then binding arbitration. This is faster and cheaper than litigation. Specify who pays for mediation/arbitration costs.

Property Transfers

Address what happens when properties sell. Typically, the agreement transfers with the property. Consider whether other parties have right of first refusal to purchase well interests. Require sellers to disclose and assign the agreement to buyers.

Common Shared Well Problems

  • Unequal usage: One party uses far more water than others
  • Repair disputes: Disagreement about necessary repairs or costs
  • Upgrade conflicts: One party wants improvements others don't
  • Access issues: Difficulty reaching well for maintenance
  • New owner problems: Buyers unaware of or disagreeing with terms
  • Quality complaints: Different expectations for water quality

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a shared well agreement include?

Essential elements: who owns the well and equipment, easement rights for access, cost-sharing formula for repairs/maintenance, usage limits or allocations, procedures for disputes, what happens when properties sell, liability provisions, and water quality responsibilities. Have an attorney draft or review the agreement—verbal agreements cause problems.

How are shared well costs typically divided?

Common approaches: equal shares regardless of usage, proportional to number of households, metered usage with costs split accordingly, or fixed monthly fees with reserve fund. Repair costs are usually shared proportionally. The arrangement should be clear in the written agreement—ambiguity causes disputes.

What are common shared well problems?

Frequent issues: one party over-using water, disagreements about repair costs, unclear ownership when properties sell, one party wanting upgrades others don't, access disputes for maintenance, different quality expectations, and liability for contamination or failures. A clear written agreement prevents most problems.

Can I sell my property with a shared well?

Yes, but the shared well agreement should transfer with the property. The agreement should specify how this works—new owners typically inherit the same rights and obligations. Disclose the shared arrangement to buyers and provide copies of all agreements. Some lenders have specific requirements for shared well properties.
