Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Agricultural Well Service for Pauma Valley Citrus Groves

Tucked beneath Palomar Mountain between Valley Center and Pala, Pauma Valley is one of San Diego County's most fertile farming basins — citrus and avocado country for well over a century. The protected climate and rich valley soils make it ideal for oranges, lemons, and grapefruit, and the valley's productive aquifer has long made it one of the region's most favorable areas for well development. With no municipal water in rural Pauma Valley and water-hauling impractical for agriculture, the grove well is everything.

Southern California Well Service has spent more than thirty years drilling, equipping, and maintaining agricultural wells in Pauma Valley and across North County. As a licensed C-57 water well drilling contractor based nearby in Ramona, we understand the valley's geology — ancient granite bedrock overlaid with decomposed granite and alluvial deposits — and what it takes to keep citrus irrigated under Palomar's shadow.

How Much Water a Pauma Valley Grove Needs

Citrus is a thirsty, year-round crop. In the valley's warm summers a mature tree can use 50 to 75 gallons a week, and a full acre of bearing citrus commonly needs three to four acre-feet annually — roughly a million gallons per acre per year. For a 10-acre grove that means a sustained 25 to 40 GPM through the season, with larger commercial blocks needing 50 GPM or more.

Because citrus never fully goes dormant here, water can't be shut off in winter. Trees need moisture at bloom and fruit set in spring, heavy supply through summer, and protection in fall. A well sized to average demand will fall behind in the hottest weeks — so we always size to peak summer demand.

Sizing and Designing a High-Capacity Citrus Well

A sound ag well balances depth to a reliable water-bearing zone, that zone's sustainable yield, a pump and motor matched to your GPM and pressure targets, and storage that buffers the trees. Pauma Valley's average well depth runs close to the San Diego County average of about 450 feet. Shallower wells tap the alluvial aquifer near drainages, while deeper wells penetrate the granitic and metamorphic rock of the Peninsular Ranges batholith to reach more reliable water. The valley's decomposed granite drills well once you pass the weathered surface rock.

We recommend a test hole and step-drawdown pumping test before selecting a pump, matching a submersible turbine to the well's true sustainable yield without over-pumping. For grove irrigation we feed a storage tank and booster into drip lines or micro-sprinklers — efficient, filterable, and easy to automate around citrus roots.

Water Quality Matters — Citrus Is Sensitive

Citrus is salt-sensitive, so watch three factors in any Pauma Valley well:

  • Salinity (TDS): Salts accumulate in the root zone and cut yield.
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus even at moderate levels — leaf-tip burn warns you.
  • Chloride: Causes leaf scorch and lowers fruit quality.

Mineral-rich groundwater can leave scale that clogs emitters and shortens pump life. We test your water and design filtration or blending to protect both trees and hardware.

Drought, Groundwater, and Local Cooperation

Pauma Valley growers have a strong tradition of cooperative groundwater stewardship, recognizing that the valley's shared aquifer needs careful management through drought. Southern California's arid climate lowers both alluvial and bedrock water tables in dry years, leaving less for pumps to draw. We build margin into every design — setting pumps deep enough to ride out drawdown and adding storage so the well recharges between sets. Paired with efficient delivery and soil-moisture scheduling, that protects your trees and the basin everyone depends on.

Why Local Expertise Matters

Well depths, yields, and rock formations vary across the valley, and with 240 wells on record drawing from shared sources, getting the design right matters. Decades of drilling in this granite-and-alluvium country have taught us where the productive zones lie and how recharge behaves, which means faster diagnosis and a system built right the first time. We are a licensed well drilling contractor focused on long-term reliability, not a quick sale.

From Backyard Trees to Working Groves

Pauma Valley groves range from a few citrus trees on a homestead to multi-acre commercial blocks, and each scale needs its own design. A small grove may thrive on a modest pump and a simple drip schedule, while a commercial operation needs a robust high-capacity well, ample storage, and automated zones that irrigate overnight when evaporation is lowest. Efficient scheduling matters as much as raw capacity: watering deeply and less often drives roots deeper and builds heat tolerance, while shallow daily watering wastes water and invites root disease. We pair your well with controllers and soil-moisture sensors so every set delivers exactly what the trees need, protecting yield while holding pumping costs and groundwater draw in check.

Common Problems, Maintenance, and When to Call a Pro

The issues we see most in Pauma Valley are pump failures from mineral buildup, pressure-tank trouble, declining yield during drought, iron or sulfur in the water, and electrical faults from rural power swings. Routine care extends a well's life: annual checks of pump performance, pressure, and amperage; periodic water testing; and quick attention to falling flow. When yield drops from encrustation, hydrofracturing can reopen fractures and restore production for far less than a new well. Call us when flow or pressure declines, emitters spit air, power bills climb, water discolors, or the pump short-cycles — and call before planting. Frequently Asked Questions

How many GPM does a Pauma Valley citrus grove well need?

A 10-acre grove typically needs a sustained 25 to 40 GPM through the season, while larger commercial blocks often require 50 GPM or more. We size pumps to peak summer demand so the grove never falls behind during the hottest weeks.

How deep are wells in Pauma Valley?

Pauma Valley's average well depth runs close to the San Diego County average of about 450 feet. Shallower wells tap the alluvial aquifer near drainages, while deeper wells reach the granitic and metamorphic bedrock of the Peninsular Ranges. A test hole confirms the right depth for your site.

Can well water damage my citrus trees?

Yes. Citrus is salt-sensitive, and high salinity, boron, or chloride can cause leaf burn, stunting, and lost yield. We test your water and can recommend filtration or blending to protect the trees.

My grove well's output is dropping — do I need a new well?

Often not. Worn pumps, fouled screens, or mineral encrustation are common causes, and hydrofracturing can restore production for $3,000 to $8,000. A diagnostic visit finds the real cause before you invest in a new well.

A turnkey agricultural well generally runs $25,000 to $60,000 or more depending on depth, casing, and pump. Pump replacements run $3,000 to $8,000, and our $125 diagnostic fee is credited toward any work.
