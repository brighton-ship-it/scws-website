What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Thermal Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

In Thermal, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Thermal wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Thermal: An Honest Assessment

Thermal is an unincorporated farming community in the eastern Coachella Valley of Riverside County, south of Indio and La Quinta and near Mecca, Oasis, and the north shore of the Salton Sea. It sits in a deep desert basin where the land surface actually drops below sea level, and the surrounding agriculture — table grapes, dates, citrus, and row crops — runs largely on groundwater pumped from the valley's vast alluvial aquifer. Because of that geology, we want to be straight with you up front: hydrofracturing is usually a weak fit for wells in Thermal, and there are almost always better ways to solve a low-water problem here. This page explains why, and what we recommend instead.

Why the Thermal Basin Is Different

Hydrofracturing works by opening and connecting cracks in hard bedrock. It is the right tool when a well draws from fractured granite or crystalline rock, as it does in the mountain backcountry. Thermal is the opposite kind of ground. The aquifer beneath the eastern Coachella Valley is made of unconsolidated late-Pleistocene and Holocene alluvium — layered sand, gravel, silt, and clay deposited over hundreds of thousands of years. Water-bearing deposits here are estimated to exceed 700 feet thick, and most production wells are completed somewhere between roughly 490 and 900 feet, with larger agricultural and municipal wells sometimes reaching 1,000 to 1,200 feet. There is no rigid rock to fracture; high-pressure injection into loose sand and gravel simply does not create the durable new pathways that make hydrofracturing effective in bedrock.

How Hydrofracturing Works (And Why the Setting Matters)

To understand why Thermal is a weak fit, it helps to know how the process actually functions. A contractor lowers inflatable packers into a cased well to isolate a target zone, then injects clean water at 1,500 to 3,000 or more PSI. In solid rock that pressure pries open and cleans existing hairline fractures and extends them outward until they intersect water-bearing cracks. The newly opened fractures stay open because the rock holds its shape. In loose alluvium there is nothing to "crack" — pump pressure dissipates into the surrounding sand and gravel, no stable channels form, and any short-term effect collapses as the material settles back. That is the core reason the technique is reserved for bedrock wells and is not a standard tool on the Coachella Valley floor. Many landowners first hear about hydrofracturing from articles written for granite regions of New England or the Sierra foothills, where it genuinely shines; the confusion is understandable, but the eastern Coachella Valley simply is not that kind of ground.

What Actually Causes Low Yield in Thermal

When a Thermal-area well loses capacity, the cause is rarely "plugged fractures." It is far more often one of these:

  • Declining regional water levels. The Coachella Valley aquifer has experienced long-term drawdown and even land subsidence in places as pumping has outpaced natural recharge. A static water level that drops below your pump intake will starve the pump even though the aquifer is huge.
  • Screen and gravel-pack fouling. In a sand-and-gravel aquifer, the well screen and surrounding gravel pack can clog with fine sediment, iron bacteria, or mineral scale, choking flow into the casing.
  • A pump set too high. As water levels fall, a pump that was once deep enough may now sit near or above the water column.
  • Worn or undersized equipment. Older pumps lose efficiency, and a tired pump in a deep desert well can look like a failing aquifer.

Better Solutions for Thermal Wells

Because the basin is alluvial, the honest, cost-effective fixes are usually these rather than hydrofracturing:

  • Well rehabilitation and screen cleaning. Mechanical surging, brushing, and chemical treatment of the screen and gravel pack can restore flow to a fouled alluvial well at a fraction of the cost of any major work.
  • Lowering or upsizing the pump. If water levels have simply dropped, resetting the pump deeper or installing a properly sized unit ($2,500-$5,500) often solves the problem outright.
  • Deepening or drilling a new well. Where the aquifer has receded or the existing well is old and collapsing, a new turnkey well ($18,000-$42,000) drilled to a productive depth is the durable answer. In a thick aquifer like this, a properly designed and screened well can produce very well.
  • Water treatment. Coachella Valley groundwater can carry sediment, hardness, iron, or other constituents. Sediment filtration ($300-$900), an iron or manganese filter or softener ($1,500-$3,500), or a constant-pressure/booster system ($2,000-$4,500) addresses quality and pressure complaints that are sometimes mistaken for yield problems.

What to Check Before You Call

A little information helps us recommend the right fix quickly:

  • Find your well completion report. Total depth, screen interval, and pump setting tell us whether the issue is likely water level, screen fouling, or equipment.
  • Note any change in water quality. Sudden sand, cloudiness, or staining points toward screen or gravel-pack problems rather than a dry aquifer.
  • Watch the pressure tank and switch. A waterlogged tank ($600-$1,500) or failed pressure switch ($150-$350) can mimic a weak well and is cheap to correct.
  • Track when the problem occurs. Steady decline over months suggests water-level or screen issues; sudden loss suggests equipment.

Deep desert wells are not a place for guesswork. If your Thermal-area well is losing capacity, a licensed contractor should test it before money is spent. We begin with a diagnostic ($125, credited toward any work we do), check water level and pump performance, and inspect the casing and screen with a downhole camera when warranted. For Thermal wells, expect equipment and rehabilitation work to be the most common spend: a pump replacement $2,500-$5,500, a pressure switch $150-$350, a pressure tank $600-$1,500, sediment filtration $300-$900, iron or manganese filtration or a softener $1,500-$3,500, and a constant-pressure or booster system $2,000-$4,500. Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing fix my low-yield well in Thermal? Almost certainly not. Hydrofracturing only works in hard, fractured rock. Thermal sits on deep sand-and-gravel alluvium, so the technique cannot create lasting new water pathways here.

My neighbor's bedrock well was hydrofracked successfully — why not mine? Geology is local. Wells in the mountains around the valley draw from fractured granite and can benefit; wells on the valley floor draw from unconsolidated aquifer material and do not.

What should I do instead? Usually screen rehabilitation, lowering or replacing the pump, water treatment, or — where the aquifer has receded or the well is failing — deepening or drilling a new well.

How deep are wells in the Thermal area? Domestic and public-supply wells are commonly completed between roughly 490 and 900 feet, with some agricultural wells reaching 1,000-1,200 feet, because the water-bearing alluvium is very thick.

Why is my water level dropping? The Coachella Valley aquifer has seen long-term drawdown where pumping exceeds recharge, which can lower static levels and even cause localized subsidence. Resetting the pump deeper or a new well is often the fix.

Do you charge for the diagnostic? We charge a $125 diagnostic fee that is credited toward any work you authorize, so an honest assessment costs you nothing if we proceed.
