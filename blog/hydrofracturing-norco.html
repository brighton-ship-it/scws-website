What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Norco Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Norco Wells: A Straight Answer Before You Spend a Dollar

Norco wears its nickname proudly. "Horsetown USA" is built around horse trails instead of sidewalks, large-lot properties instead of tract subdivisions, and a way of life that depends on dependable water. Tucked into the Santa Ana River valley in western Riverside County, just south of Eastvale and northeast of Corona, Norco is one of the few places in Southern California where it is normal to step out your back door to a barn, an arena, and a row of fruit trees. All of that lifestyle runs on water, and for many Norco households that water comes from a private well rather than a municipal connection.

That creates a particular kind of pressure. A two-acre horse property might water several animals, keep pasture and landscaping alive through a long dry summer, fill troughs, wash down stalls, and still need to supply the house. When a well that used to keep up suddenly cannot, the temptation is to reach for the most dramatic fix. Hydrofracturing (often called hydrofracking) is one of those fixes, and in the right Norco well it can be transformative. But Norco's geology means it is the right answer in some yards and the wrong answer in others. This guide is meant to help you tell which one you have before you commit money to it.

What Hydrofracturing Actually Is

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation technique that uses nothing more exotic than clean water under high pressure. There are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no resemblance to the oil-and-gas "fracking" you may have read about. The goal is simple: reopen and clean the natural cracks in solid rock so that groundwater can travel into your well faster.

The work centers on a tool called a packer. A packer is an inflatable seal that a technician lowers into the well casing on a pipe. Once it is positioned at the target depth, it is inflated to isolate a specific zone of the borehole, sealing it off from the sections above. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated zone at roughly 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. That pressure pushes into the existing hairline fractures in the surrounding bedrock, flushing out the silt, mineral scale, and fine sediment that have been choking them, and easing the fractures open a little wider. New, cleaner pathways form between the water-bearing rock and your casing.

A typical treatment takes about four to eight hours on site. The crew may treat a single zone or step the packer through several depths, isolating and pressurizing each one in turn. When the pressure work is done, the well is surged and pumped to clear the loosened debris, and then it is flow-tested to measure the result.

The Norco Geology Question: Bedrock vs. Valley Floor

Here is the part most advertising leaves out, and the part that matters most in Norco. Hydrofracturing only works in rock that holds fractures. It does its job by opening cracks in hard formations such as granite and other bedrock. In loose material, there are no fractures to open, the pressure simply dissipates into the surrounding sediment, and the treatment does little or nothing.

Norco straddles two very different settings:

  • The Santa Ana River valley floor. Much of Norco sits on alluvial deposits laid down by the river over thousands of years: layers of sand and gravel. Wells finished in this material draw water through the spaces between grains, not through cracks in rock. These valley-floor alluvial wells are usually not good hydrofracturing candidates.
  • The surrounding slopes and higher ground. As you move toward the hills that frame the valley, wells are more likely to reach bedrock. A bedrock well may be a genuine candidate for hydrofracturing, because it produces from fractures that can be cleaned and opened.

How to Tell Which Kind of Well You Have

You do not have to guess. Two things will tell you:

  1. Your driller's log (well completion report). Every legally drilled well in California has one filed with the state. It describes the materials the drill passed through at each depth. If the log shows sand and gravel all the way down, you almost certainly have an alluvial well. If it shows granite or other hard rock, you have a bedrock well that could respond to fracturing.
  2. Location and depth. Wells on the valley floor near the river tend to be shallower and finished in alluvium. Most private wells around Norco fall in the 150 to 400 foot range. A well that had to be drilled deep into hard rock on higher ground is more likely a fracturing candidate.

An honest contractor will look at your log before recommending hydrofracturing. If anyone offers to frack your well without first asking what formation it produces from, that is a reason to pause.

Signs a Bedrock Norco Well May Need Hydrofracturing

If you have confirmed you are on bedrock, these are the symptoms that point toward a fracturing treatment:

  • Declining yield over the years. The well once kept up with the barn, the arena, and the house, but now it falls behind, especially in summer.
  • Slow recovery after pumping. You run the well hard for irrigation, it draws down, and it takes a long time to refill before it can produce again.
  • It cannot keep up with irrigation and livestock demand. On a horse property the combined draw of troughs, wash racks, pasture, and landscaping is heavy. A marginal bedrock well shows its limits fastest under that load.
  • A new well that under-produces. Sometimes a freshly drilled bedrock well disappoints from day one because it intersected only a few productive fractures. Fracturing can connect it to more.

Better-Fit Solutions for Alluvial Wells

If your driller's log shows you are on the valley floor in sand and gravel, do not despair, and do not let anyone talk you into fracturing a formation that cannot benefit. There are several proven ways to restore an alluvial well's performance:

  • Well redevelopment and cleaning. Over time, fine sediment and mineral scale clog the screen and the gravel pack around it. Mechanical surging, brushing, and jetting can reopen those pathways and recover lost capacity.
  • Screen and casing work. A corroded or encrusted screen restricts flow. Repairing, replacing, or rehabilitating the screen and casing can make a dramatic difference in an alluvial well.
  • Deepening the well. If the water table has dropped or the well was finished shallow, extending it can reach more saturated material.
  • Pump upgrade. A well producing plenty of water can still feel weak at the house if the pump is undersized, worn, or set at the wrong depth. The right submersible pump matched to the well's true capacity often solves the complaint.
  • A larger pressure or booster system. Horse properties with long irrigation runs and multiple hydrants frequently suffer from pressure problems rather than supply problems. A bigger pressure tank or a constant-pressure booster system can deliver steady flow to the far corners of the property.

Safety First: What to Check Before You Assume the Well Is the Problem

Before spending thousands on any below-ground work, rule out the simple and inexpensive causes. Many "low water" calls in Norco turn out to be equipment, not the aquifer:

  • The pump. A failing submersible pump produces less and less water until it quits. Age and cycling patterns are clues.
  • The pressure tank. A waterlogged or failed tank causes rapid pump cycling and weak, surging pressure.
  • The pressure switch. A dirty or failing switch can shut the pump down early or run it erratically.
  • Leaks. A cracked underground line, a stuck float valve on a trough, or a leaking irrigation zone can drain a perfectly healthy well.
  • A flow test. The single most useful diagnostic is a professional flow test that measures what your well and pump are actually delivering. It separates a supply problem from a hardware problem so you do not pay to fix the wrong thing.

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Well work involves high pressure, electrical components, and heavy equipment lowered hundreds of feet into the ground. It is regulated for good reason. In California, water-well drilling and rehabilitation are performed under a C-57 Water Well Drilling contractor license. Hiring a licensed C-57 contractor protects you, ensures the work is filed correctly with the county and state, and means the person assessing your well is qualified to tell you honestly whether hydrofracturing will help or whether your money is better spent elsewhere.

How Well Hydrofracturing Works in Fractured Rock

When the formation is right, the results are real. In hard, fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of treated wells, with typical gains of about two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate. The honest caveat is that it is not a guarantee; a minority of wells show little change, which is exactly why a candid assessment of the formation comes first. We document the outcome with before-and-after flow tests, generally comparing the baseline measurement to a follow-up taken 24 to 48 hours after treatment so the well has stabilized.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure to reopen natural cracks in rock. There are no chemicals and no sand proppants. It is a water-well rehabilitation method, completely different in scale and purpose from oil-and-gas extraction.

My well is on the valley floor near the river. Will fracturing help?

Probably not. Valley-floor wells in Norco are usually finished in sand and gravel alluvium, which has no fractures to open. Those wells respond far better to redevelopment, screen and casing work, a pump upgrade, or a larger pressure system. Your driller's log will confirm which type you have.

How do I find out whether my well is bedrock or alluvial?

Check your driller's log (well completion report), which describes the materials drilled through at each depth. Granite or hard rock points to a fracturing candidate; sand and gravel points to an alluvial well. A licensed contractor can review the log with you.

How long does hydrofracturing take, and how disruptive is it?

The on-site treatment typically takes four to eight hours in a single day. Equipment is set up at the wellhead, the work is done, and the well is flow-tested. Most properties are back to normal use shortly after, with results verified within 24 to 48 hours.

What does it cost compared with drilling a new well?

On a suitable bedrock well, hydrofracturing runs about $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well in this area runs roughly $18,000 to $42,000. That is why fracturing is worth investigating first when the formation supports it.

What if my problem turns out to be the pump or pressure tank?

That is common, and it is good news because those repairs are far less expensive. A flow test ($125, credited toward the work) tells us whether you have a supply problem or a hardware problem before you spend money on the wrong fix.
