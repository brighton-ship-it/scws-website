How a Well Pressure System Works

Four parts deliver your water. The pump draws groundwater from the aquifer. A pressure tank stores it under a cushion of compressed air so the pump does not run on every tap. The pressure switch starts the pump at a cut-in pressure (often 30 PSI) and stops it at cut-out (often 50 PSI). The water delivered between those points is the drawdown. On Mentone's foothill lots, where temperature swings and sloped terrain stress equipment, a healthy drawdown and a correctly sized pump keep pressure steady at the house and up the hill.

Mentone Geology and Its Pressure Challenges

Mentone sits where granite bedrock from the San Bernardino Mountains meets sedimentary deposits of sand, gravel, and silt washed down over millennia into the Santa Ana River watershed. Wells here often tap alluvial aquifers that vary widely in depth and yield depending on proximity to the mountains or the basin. The region's geology, combined with seasonal rainfall and occasional snowmelt from higher elevations, drives groundwater recharge and well performance. Near the mountains, wells may hit fractured granite that limits storage and yields inconsistently; further into the basin, sandy alluvial layers can carry sediment into wells, clogging screens. The area's proximity to fault zones, including the San Andreas nearby, can shift underground water pathways over time, and Mentone's semi-arid climate means droughts lower water tables and stress wells further.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Mentone

  • Pump damage from sediment and hard water: Alluvial sand and gravel wear pump impellers, while calcium and magnesium from mountain runoff scale components and sap performance.
  • Pressure tank malfunctions: Mineral deposits damage bladders, and foothill temperature swings disrupt air charge, causing inconsistent delivery.
  • Low yield from seasonal drought: Reduced mountain recharge in dry summers drops water tables, leaving wells unable to meet demand.
  • Clogged infrastructure: Sediment from alluvial soils clogs screens and pipes, and iron or manganese deposits narrow pipe diameters over time.
  • Electrical and aging equipment: Power inconsistencies in rural foothill areas interrupt pump cycles, and older systems lack capacity for current demand.

What to Check Before You Call

Start with a few checks. Compare fixtures: weak flow everywhere points to pump or tank; an isolated weak tap points to a clog. Watch the pump for rapid cycling (tank or switch) versus constant running without pressure (mechanical failure or low yield). Inspect the water for sand or metallic taste, common with alluvial sediment or mineral leaching. Check the tank air charge with a tire gauge, pump off and tank drained, near 28 PSI for a 30/50 switch. And note the season, since a dry-month drop usually means yield. These observations help us arrive ready to act.

Preventive Maintenance for Mentone Wells

Preventive care saves costly repairs. Schedule a yearly inspection before summer drought to catch sediment or pressure issues early. Check the pressure tank monthly with a tire gauge so the air charge matches cut-in and avoids cycling worsened by foothill temperature swings. Install a sediment filter if sandy water appears, replacing it every few months, and flush outdoor taps annually. Shield pumps and tanks with a cover or enclosure against Mentone's temperature extremes. Conserve during dry seasons to preserve aquifer levels, and add a water softener to fight the hard water that mountain runoff brings.
