What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your National City Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in National City: What Owners Should Know

Searches for hydrofracturing in National City are usually driven by a low-producing well — but the first honest thing to say is that private wells are rare here. National City is a built-out coastal community in San Diego County served by a municipal water agency, so most properties never touch groundwater directly. For the small number that do rely on a private well, the geology makes hydrofracturing a weak fit. This page explains why, and what works better.

National City Is on City Water

National City receives its water from the Sweetwater Authority, a publicly owned agency that serves the city along with Bonita and parts of Chula Vista. Its supply blends the Sweetwater River, brackish groundwater desalination, the San Diego Formation, and imported water. Practically speaking, that means the typical National City home or business is on the municipal system, not a backyard well. If that describes you, hydrofracturing simply is not part of the picture — your water service questions belong with the Authority.

How Hydrofracturing Works (and When It Applies)

For the rare private well, here is the technique in plain terms. We seal off a section of the well's borehole with an inflatable packer, then inject clean water at high pressure — commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. In fractured bedrock, that pressure opens the rock's natural fissures, clears the silt and scale plugging them, and widens the network so groundwater reaches the well more freely. It uses only water, takes four to eight hours, and can multiply a bedrock well's yield several times over. The crucial condition is the rock: without solid, fractured stone to open, the technique has nothing to act on.

Why National City Geology Is a Weak Candidate

National City sits on coastal and mesa sediments. The productive groundwater that local agency wells tap comes from the San Diego Formation, reached at roughly 800 feet — deep layers of marine and alluvial sand and gravel rather than fractured crystalline rock. High-pressure water does not create durable fractures in that kind of loose sediment. So even where a private well exists in the National City area, it is almost always finished in material that hydrofracturing cannot meaningfully improve.

Where Bedrock Wells Do Become Candidates

Hydrofracturing earns its reputation farther inland and up in elevation, where San Diego County wells reach decomposed granite and fractured crystalline bedrock — in the foothill and mountain communities east of the coastal plain. If your property is on the inland fringe and your well log notes granite or fractured rock, you may be a candidate. The flat, urban, sediment-based ground in and around National City generally is not.

Better Fixes for a Low-Yield Well Here

  • Deepen the existing well. Reaching a more productive sediment layer can restore output without a full replacement.
  • Drill a properly screened new well. A correctly designed well in good coastal sediment can be a dependable producer. A new turnkey well runs $18,000–$42,000.
  • Repair the equipment first. A worn pump ($2,500–$5,500), a waterlogged pressure tank ($600–$1,500), or a failing pressure switch ($150–$350) often explains the symptoms entirely.
  • Treat water quality. Coastal wells can carry sediment, hardness, or brackish influence; filtration ($300–$900) and softening or iron/manganese treatment ($1,500–$3,500) address those.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Confirm you are actually on a well. Many owners in this area assume a well and discover they are on the municipal system, or vice versa.
  • Find your well log. If it shows sand and gravel rather than rock, fracturing is off the table.
  • Watch the symptoms. Air at the tap and slow recovery point to the well; steady low pressure across the house points to the pump or pressure system.
  • Inspect the pressure tank. Short-cycling usually means the tank or switch — an inexpensive fix.

The San Diego Formation Beneath South Bay

The deep groundwater under National City and the wider South Bay comes from the San Diego Formation, a thick sequence of marine and alluvial sands and gravels. Sweetwater Authority taps it with engineered municipal wells reaching several hundred feet, and even treats brackier water through desalination to stretch local supply. None of this is bedrock. For the rare private well in the area, that geology is decisive: the formation is a sediment aquifer, productive for a properly designed well but unresponsive to hydrofracturing, which only widens fractures in solid rock. Knowing this up front spares an owner from paying for a treatment that physically cannot improve a sediment well.

If You Have a Private Well on the Inland Edge

A handful of properties on the inland and eastern fringes of the National City area — closer to the rising ground toward the foothills — may sit where sediment thins and fractured rock comes into play. If your well log notes granite, decomposed granite, or fractured rock near the bottom, you could be the exception that benefits from hydrofracturing. For everyone else on coastal sediment, the productive paths are deepening to a better layer, building a properly screened replacement well, or, most commonly, correcting a pump or pressure-system problem that has been masquerading as a failing well.

Water Quality Matters Near the Coast

Coastal South Bay wells can carry challenges beyond yield. Proximity to the bay and the lower Sweetwater River means brackish influence and hardness are common, and sediment intrusion can foul a screen over time. Where the issue is quality rather than quantity, the fix is treatment, not fracturing: sediment filtration runs $300 to $900, while softening or iron and manganese treatment runs $1,500 to $3,500. We assess water chemistry alongside flow during our diagnostic so the recommendation fits the real problem.
