What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your University Heights Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many University Heights wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in University Heights: What Owners Should Know

University Heights is one of San Diego's older streetcar neighborhoods, a walkable Mid-City community perched on the mesa just north of North Park and east of Hillcrest, in central San Diego County. With its 1920s bungalows, Park Boulevard businesses, and tightly platted lots, University Heights is about as urban as San Diego gets, and like the rest of the central city it is served entirely by treated municipal water from the City of San Diego. For that reason, an honest page about hydrofracturing here has to start with a plain truth: for nearly every University Heights property, hydrofracturing does not apply, because the technique only works on private water wells, and this neighborhood does not have them.

We would rather earn trust by being candid than by overselling. If you landed here because your University Heights home has weak water pressure, discolored water, or a high bill, the explanation is almost always in your home's plumbing or the city supply, not in groundwater. The sections below explain what hydrofracturing is, the rare cases where it could matter near University Heights, and the practical fixes for the water issues that actually affect homes in this part of San Diego.

How Hydrofracturing Works, and Where

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates a struggling private well by injecting clean water under high pressure, typically 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, into an isolated section of the borehole. The pressure flushes silt and mineral scale out of the rock fractures that feed the well and can open new connections to water-bearing seams, sometimes multiplying the well's yield several times over. None of that is relevant unless three things are true: there is a private well on the property, it is finished in fractured rock, and it is underperforming. University Heights, built on mesa-top sediments and connected to the municipal grid since its earliest days, almost never meets the first condition, let alone the others.

The Rare University Heights Exception

Could any University Heights parcel have a private well? In principle, an unusually deep historic lot, a converted commercial site, or an old irrigation well on a larger holding might. If you have physical evidence of a wellhead, casing, or an old pump, it is worth an inspection before assuming anything. Outside of those uncommon situations, though, the answer for a standard residential lot is that there is no well to treat, and any high-pressure service would be pointless.

What Usually Causes Water Trouble Here

  • Aging galvanized pipe. Many University Heights bungalows still have original galvanized supply lines that corrode and constrict, throttling pressure and flow. Repiping in copper or PEX is the durable fix.
  • Pressure-reducing valve issues. A worn regulator where the city line meets the house can drop pressure throughout the home.
  • Hard water. San Diego's imported water is hard, leaving scale on fixtures and water heaters. A softener or conditioner solves it.
  • Taste and odor. Chlorine or flat taste from municipal treatment is handled with point-of-use or whole-house filtration.

Simple Checks Before You Spend Money

  • Verify your water source. A City of San Diego water bill confirms you are on municipal supply with no active well.
  • Put a pressure gauge on an outside spigot. A low reading suggests a regulator problem rather than anything underground.
  • Determine whether one fixture or the whole house is affected. A single slow tap usually means a clogged aerator or valve.
  • Ask neighbors if they are seeing the same thing, which would point to the city main rather than your home.

Why the Geology Argues Against Wells in University Heights

The reliable private wells we hydrofracture are inland, in the fractured granite of the Peninsular Ranges that runs through East County and the mountains. University Heights sits on the relatively flat sedimentary mesa of central San Diego, a setting that never offered the productive bedrock aquifers found in the backcountry. As the city expanded across these mesas in the early twentieth century, neighborhoods like University Heights were plumbed for imported water from the outset. There simply was never a base of domestic rock wells here to maintain, which is the underlying reason a hydrofracturing specialist has essentially no work to do within the neighborhood, and why we tell owners so directly.

What Hydrofracturing Looks Like in Practice

So you have the full picture, here is what a real hydrofracturing job involves when there is a well to treat. A crew arrives with a high-pressure pump and a string of downhole packers. After measuring the well's baseline output, they lower a packer to seal off a target zone, then build pressure in stages, watching how the formation accepts water. The pressure scours debris from the existing fractures and propagates them outward to intersect new water-bearing seams. The well is then redeveloped to flush loosened material, and a final flow test documents the improvement. The whole operation takes a half day or so. It is a proven, chemical-free technique, but it is also irrelevant without a private well in suitable rock, which is why we keep returning to that point for University Heights readers rather than padding this page with promises that would not apply to your home.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing fix low water pressure in my University Heights home?

No, not if you are on city water, which describes essentially every home in the neighborhood. Pressure problems on a municipal supply come from regulators or plumbing and call for a plumber, not a well service.

How can I tell if I have a private well?

If the City of San Diego sends you a water bill, you are on municipal water with no active well. A visible wellhead, casing, pump, and pressure tank would indicate otherwise.

There is an old pipe in my yard that looks like a well. What now?

Have it inspected before assuming it is usable. We can determine whether it is a real well and whether the geology beneath your lot could ever support meaningful yield.

Is University Heights good well country?

No. It sits on sedimentary mesa rather than the fractured granite that makes inland wells productive, which is why the neighborhood relies on imported municipal water.

What is the right fix for hard water and chlorine taste?

A water softener for hardness and a filtration system for taste and odor. Both address the characteristics of San Diego's imported supply and are unrelated to wells.

Would you still come out to University Heights?

Yes, to evaluate any genuine well and give you an honest recommendation, even when that recommendation is that hydrofracturing is not appropriate for your property.
