What Is a Well Screen?

A well screen is the slotted or perforated section at the water-producing zone of your well. Think of it as a filter between the aquifer and your pump.

What It Does

  • Allows water in: Slots or holes let groundwater flow into the well
  • Keeps sand out: Openings are sized to block sediment
  • Supports the well: Provides structural stability in the aquifer zone
  • Protects equipment: Prevents sand from destroying your pump

Why It Matters

A failing screen leads to:

  • Sand and sediment in your water
  • Pump damage (sand is abrasive)
  • Clogged fixtures and appliances
  • Reduced water production
  • Potential well collapse

Types of Well Screens

Continuous Slot (Wire-Wound)

  • Design: V-shaped wire wound around support rods
  • Pros: Maximum open area, self-cleaning, long life
  • Cons: Higher cost
  • Best for: Fine sand formations, high-production wells
  • Cost: $$$$

Slotted PVC

  • Design: Machine-cut slots in PVC pipe
  • Pros: Corrosion-resistant, affordable
  • Cons: Less open area, slots can clog
  • Best for: Residential wells, non-corrosive water
  • Cost: $$

Louvered/Shutter Screen

  • Design: Stamped metal louvers
  • Pros: Good strength, moderate cost
  • Cons: Lower open area than wire-wound
  • Best for: Gravel formations, moderate production
  • Cost: $$$

Perforated Casing

  • Design: Holes drilled in steel or PVC casing
  • Pros: Simple, inexpensive
  • Cons: Limited open area, prone to clogging
  • Best for: Rock wells, coarse gravel
  • Cost: $

Signs of Screen Problems

Sand or Sediment in Water

  • Grit in fixtures, toilets, water heater
  • Sediment settling in glasses of water
  • Cloudy water that doesn't clear
  • Urgency: High—sand damages pump quickly

Declining Water Production

  • Gradual decrease in flow over months or years
  • Lower pressure than before
  • Flow test shows reduced GPM
  • Urgency: Moderate—investigate before it worsens

Increased Pump Cycling

  • Pump runs more often than before
  • Shorter run times
  • May indicate restricted flow through screen
  • Urgency: Moderate—stresses pump

Recurring Pump Problems

  • Pump failures every few years instead of 10-15
  • Sand in pump when pulled
  • Worn impellers and seals
  • Urgency: High—fix the source, not just the symptom

Water Quality Changes

  • Iron bacteria or mineral buildup
  • Odor changes
  • May indicate screen biofouling

What Causes Screen Failure

Mineral Encrustation

Minerals in groundwater deposit on screen openings:

  • Iron: Rust-colored buildup, very common
  • Manganese: Black deposits
  • Calcium carbite: White scale (hard water)
  • Gradually reduces open area, restricting flow

Bacterial Buildup (Biofouling)

  • Iron bacteria: Slimy orange-brown masses
  • Clogs screen openings
  • Can completely block flow over time
  • Often accompanied by rotten egg or swampy odor

Corrosion

  • Aggressive water chemistry attacks metal screens
  • Low pH (acidic water) accelerates corrosion
  • Creates holes that let sand through
  • Eventually leads to structural failure

Physical Damage

  • Pump hitting screen during installation
  • Ground movement or settling
  • Sand pumping creating voids
  • Collapse from external pressure

Age

  • Typical screen life: 15-40 years
  • Depends on water chemistry and screen material
  • Even well-maintained screens eventually degrade

Cleaning vs Replacement: Decision Guide

When Cleaning Works

  • Screen is structurally intact (no holes or collapse)
  • Buildup is mineral or bacterial (can be dissolved)
  • Well has history of good production
  • Camera shows screen slots are blocked but not damaged
  • Cost-effective to extend screen life

When Replacement Is Needed

  • Screen has holes or corrosion damage
  • Screen has collapsed or deformed
  • Sand coming through indicates structural failure
  • Multiple cleaning attempts haven't helped
  • Screen is very old (30+ years)

Cleaning Methods

Method Cost Best For
Acidizing (chemical) $1,000-$2,500 Mineral scale
Chlorination $500-$1,500 Bacterial slime
Mechanical brushing $800-$2,000 Soft deposits
Jetting (high pressure) $1,200-$3,000 Heavy buildup
Combination treatment $1,500-$4,000 Multiple issues

Expected Results from Cleaning

  • Successful cleaning: Restores 70-100% of original capacity
  • Extended life: 5-15 years if screen is in good condition
  • May need repeat: Every 5-10 years for wells prone to buildup

Screen Replacement Process

Step 1: Inspection & Diagnosis

  • Video camera inspection of well
  • Assess screen condition and damage extent
  • Determine if replacement is necessary
  • Plan approach based on well construction

Step 2: Pump Removal

  • Pull pump and drop pipe from well
  • Inspect pump for damage (may need replacement)

Step 3: Screen Removal/Installation

Method depends on well type:

  • Telescoped screen: Can pull old screen and install new
  • One-piece casing: May need to install liner screen inside existing
  • Gravel pack wells: More complex—may require redevelopment

Step 4: Well Development

  • Clean well of drilling debris
  • Develop screen to maximize flow
  • Remove fine particles from formation

Step 5: Testing & Completion

  • Reinstall pump
  • Flow test to verify production
  • Water quality test
  • Disinfect well

Timeline

  • Simple replacement: 1-2 days
  • Complex job: 2-4 days
  • No water during work

Prevention & Maintenance

Regular Chlorination

  • Annual shock chlorination controls bacteria
  • Prevents biofouling buildup
  • Cost: $200-$500 per treatment

Water Treatment

  • Iron filter reduces iron buildup in well
  • Water softener reduces mineral scaling
  • Treats symptoms but doesn't eliminate source

Flow Monitoring

  • Annual flow test tracks production
  • Catches declining flow early
  • Baseline comparison shows changes

Camera Inspections

  • Every 5-10 years for older wells
  • Catches problems before they're critical
  • Guides maintenance decisions

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does well screen replacement cost?

$2,000-$10,000+ depending on well depth. Shallow wells: $2,000-$5,000. Deep wells: $5,000-$10,000+. Screen cleaning costs $800-$3,000 and may restore function without replacement.

What is a well screen?

The slotted or perforated section at the bottom of your well that lets water in while keeping sand out. It's the filter between the aquifer and your pump.

How long do well screens last?

15-40 years typically, depending on water chemistry and screen material. Aggressive water (low pH, high iron) shortens life. Stainless steel and PVC last longer than carbon steel.

How do I know if my screen is clogged or damaged?

A camera inspection ($200-$500) shows the difference. Clogged screens have visible buildup that may be cleanable. Damaged screens have holes, corrosion, or collapse requiring replacement.

Can a clogged screen be cleaned?

Often yes, if the screen is structurally sound. Chemical treatment dissolves mineral and bacterial buildup. Cleaning costs $800-$3,000 and can extend screen life 5-15 years.

What causes sand in my well water?

Screen failure—either holes from corrosion, collapsed sections, or slots worn larger than the sand particles. This requires screen replacement, not just pump work.

Should I replace my screen during pump replacement?

Not automatically. Get a camera inspection. If the screen is in good condition, leave it. If it's showing wear, replacing during pump work saves on labor costs.
