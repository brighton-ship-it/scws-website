Sand in Your Barstow Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Barstow well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Barstow well.

Sandy and Gritty Well Water in Barstow: A Local Owner's Guide

Barstow sits in the heart of the Mojave Desert along the Mojave River, where the line between a productive well and a sand-plagued one often comes down to a few hundred feet of geology. The town draws its groundwater from the Middle Mojave River Valley basin, an aquifer system built almost entirely from river-borne sand and gravel. When you live over that kind of ground, a little grit in your water isn't a freak event — it's the land reminding you what it's made of. The trouble is that "a little grit" rarely stays little. Sand in a private well tends to get worse as pumps wear, screens age, and water levels drop.

Why Barstow Wells Produce Sand

Barstow's groundwater comes from two interconnected, unconfined aquifers. The first is a shallow floodplain aquifer made of permeable young and old river deposits — felsic alluvium up to about 200 feet thick near Barstow — but it's restricted to within roughly a mile of the Mojave River channel. Below and beyond it lies a deeper regional aquifer of unconsolidated older alluvium and fan deposits up to about 1,000 feet thick. Both are sandy by nature. Wells finished in the floodplain deposits are pulling water straight through young river sand, which is exactly why sand is such a common complaint in this part of the Mojave. The geology sets the stage, but the specific cause in your well usually comes down to one of these five things:

  • Worn pump impellers: Years of pumping abrasive Mojave River sand grinds down the impellers and bowls inside a submersible pump. Once those parts erode, the pump loses its ability to seal against sand and starts passing more of it with every cycle.
  • An oversized pump pulling sand: A pump that's too powerful for the well draws water faster than the surrounding sand can settle. The extra suction yanks fine river sand off the formation and straight up the column, even in a well that was clean for years.
  • A failing or wrong-slot well screen: The screen is the filter that keeps formation sand out. In Barstow's fine river alluvium, a screen with slots that are too wide — or one that's corroded and split — lets sand pour right in.
  • Sand in the local aquifer: Wells set in the floodplain felsic alluvium are surrounded by loose, sandy material that simply migrates toward any pumping well. Some sand production is the formation itself, not a broken part.
  • An over-pumped, drought-stressed well: When water levels fall, the pump intake ends up closer to the bottom of the well where sediment collects. Heavy seasonal demand and stressed basin levels pull the water table down and concentrate sand right where the pump is drawing.

What Sand Is Actually Doing to Your System

Sand is abrasive, and it never stops moving. Every grain that comes up the well acts like liquid sandpaper on the inside of your system — scouring pump impellers, chewing through pressure tanks, cutting grooves into valve seats, and clogging the tiny orifices in faucet aerators, toilet fill valves, and irrigation emitters. It settles in the bottom of your water heater and pressure tank, robbing you of capacity and forcing the pump to cycle harder. Over time, that constant abrasion shortens the life of a pump that should run for 15 years down to a fraction of that, and it leaves grit in your bathtub, sediment in your dishwasher, and a faint scratchiness in water that should be clean. Left alone, a minor sand issue becomes a chain of expensive failures.

How to Tell What Kind of Sand Problem You Have

Before you call anyone, a few simple checks at home can tell you a lot:

  • Run cold water into a clear glass jar straight from an outdoor spigot, let it sit, and look for sand settling at the bottom.
  • Note when the sand appears — only when the pump first kicks on, steadily all day, or worse during heavy irrigation.
  • Check whether the grit is fine and powdery (formation sand) or coarse and gritty (often screen or pump trouble).
  • Inspect faucet aerators and shower screens for trapped sediment.
  • Watch for clay-colored or cloudy water after the pump runs hard, which can point to a stressed well drawing from near the bottom.

Those clues narrow things down, but a real diagnosis takes professional tools. We measure your static and pumping water levels, check the pump's amperage and actual output against its rating, and when needed run a downhole camera to see the screen and casing condition for ourselves. That tells us whether you're dealing with a worn pump, a bad screen, or simply a sandy formation — and which fix will actually solve it. Fixing Sandy Well Water in Barstow

Sand Separators and Centrifugal Sand Filters

When the sand is coming from the formation and the well is otherwise healthy, the most cost-effective fix is to filter it out at the surface. A centrifugal sand separator spins water fast enough to fling heavy sand grains out of the stream before they reach your pressure tank and plumbing — no cartridges to change, just a periodic purge. Installed, these systems typically run $400 to $1,500 depending on flow rate and configuration. For many Barstow homes drawing from sandy river alluvium, this alone restores clean water.

Resizing or Raising the Pump

If an oversized pump is pulling sand or the pump sits too low in a well with falling water levels, the answer is to right-size the equipment and reposition the intake higher in the water column, away from settled sediment. A full pump replacement generally runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower. Matching the pump to your well's real capacity often cuts sand production dramatically while extending the life of the whole system.

Well Screen Replacement and Screened Liners

When the trouble is the screen itself — corroded, split, or slotted too wide for Barstow's fine alluvium — we can install a properly sized screened liner inside the existing casing or replace the screen section. The slot size is matched to your formation so it holds back sand while still letting water flow freely. This is the durable, long-term fix for a structural screen failure.

Well Rehabilitation

Older wells accumulate mineral scale, bacterial buildup, and sand bridges that choke flow and pull sediment loose. Rehabilitation — mechanical surging, brushing, and chemical treatment to clean the screen and surrounding gravel pack — restores the well's original performance. This work typically runs $1,500 to $5,000 and can postpone or avoid the far larger cost of drilling a new well.

When to Call a Professional

Some sand is tolerable; some is a warning. Call us right away if you notice any of the following — they usually mean the problem is accelerating and a small repair window is closing.

  • Sand suddenly appearing where there was none before.
  • A steady increase in grit over weeks or months.
  • The pump short-cycling, running constantly, or losing pressure.
  • Visible sediment building in the pressure tank, water heater, or toilet tanks.
  • Clogged aerators, sprinkler heads, or irrigation emitters.
  • Cloudy or muddy water after the pump runs hard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sandy water common in Barstow wells?

Yes. Barstow sits over sandy Mojave River alluvium — both the shallow floodplain aquifer and the deeper regional fan deposits are largely sand and gravel — so some sand is one of the most common well issues we see here.

Will a little sand hurt my well pump?

Even small amounts are abrasive and wear out pump impellers, valves, and pressure tanks over time. Catching it early almost always costs less than replacing a pump that sand has destroyed.

Can I just add a filter and ignore the rest?

A sand separator solves formation sand cleanly, but if the real cause is a worn pump or failing screen, filtering only treats the symptom. That's why we diagnose the source before recommending a fix.

Why did my Barstow well suddenly start making sand?

Sudden sand often means a screen has failed, a pump has worn through, or falling water levels have dropped the intake into sediment. A sudden change is worth a prompt inspection.
