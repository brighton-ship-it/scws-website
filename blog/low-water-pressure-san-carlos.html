The Parts of Your Well System That Create Pressure

Weak pressure almost always traces back to one of three components. Knowing what each one does helps you describe the problem accurately and spot trouble early:

  • Submersible pump — this is the workhorse, set deep in the well bore in the granitic rock, lifting water up to your home. If it is wearing out, every faucet in the house feels weak.
  • Pressure tank with an internal bladder — the bladder holds an air cushion that keeps water flowing between pump cycles. A healthy tank smooths out pressure; a failed one makes it pulse and surge.
  • Pressure switch — typically calibrated to a 40/60 psi range, it commands the pump to switch on at 40 psi and off at 60 psi. Drift, corrosion or a stuck contact throws your whole system off.

Think of it as a relay team: the pump produces the water, the tank stores and steadies it, and the switch keeps the timing right. A breakdown anywhere in that chain shows up as low or erratic pressure at the tap.

Why Low Pressure Happens in the Eastern County Foothills

Across our years working the eastern San Diego County backcountry near Santee, Lakeside and El Cajon, a handful of causes account for the vast majority of low-pressure service calls:

  • Pressure tank that has lost its air charge — the single most frequent cause. The pump short-cycles, pressure bounces, and the tank may feel heavy and full of water throughout.
  • Pressure switch worn or out of calibration — the mineral content typical of granitic-rock wells corrodes contacts and lets the cut-in and cut-out points drift.
  • Decomposed-granite sediment — the fine grit that gives the Mission Trails area its character also works into the well, clogging intake screens and filters.
  • An aging or undersized submersible pump — hard-rock wells are demanding, and pumps lose output as they age past a decade.
  • Reduced well yield in dry years — during San Diego County's recurring droughts, the water table in a fractured-rock well can fall, slowing recovery and leaving you short under heavy demand.
  • Scaled or partly closed plumbing — iron, manganese and hardness build up inside older pipes and valves, throttling flow long before the well itself is the problem.

One cause frequently masks another. A tired pump may look fine until a sediment-clogged screen pushes it past its limit, and a failing tank can quietly destroy a perfectly good pump through constant short-cycling. Pinpointing the real source is the difference between a lasting repair and a repeat visit.

Safe Checks You Can Do at Home

Before you pick up the phone, here are a few checks any homeowner can perform safely, without touching electrical components:

  1. Watch the pressure gauge. Find the gauge near your pressure tank and observe it as water runs. If it never reaches the high 50s, or if the needle jumps around quickly, suspect the tank or switch.
  2. Knock on the pressure tank. The upper portion should ring hollow and the lower portion sound solid. If it sounds solid all the way up, the bladder has likely failed.
  3. Isolate the problem. Run several fixtures. If only one is weak, you probably have a clogged aerator or fixture rather than a well issue.
  4. Inspect for leaks around the wellhead, tank fittings and visible piping. Even a slow leak can rob pressure.
  5. Watch for sediment or color in the water, which points to grit intrusion from the decomposed granite.

A serious safety note: never open the pressure switch cover or touch any wiring. Well pumps run on 230-volt power, and that switch carries enough current to cause severe injury or death. Leave anything electrical, and anything inside the well casing, to a licensed professional. The homeowner checks above are observation-only for good reason.

When It Is Time to Call a Professional

Reach out without delay if you have lost water entirely, if the pump runs nonstop yet pressure stays low, if you hear the pressure switch chattering rapidly, or if your water suddenly turns cloudy or gritty. Those symptoms usually signal a failing pump, a ruptured tank, or sediment overwhelming the system. For rural homes in the foothills toward Santee and Lakeside, a quick diagnostic visit can head off a full no-water emergency, especially in summer when demand peaks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really private wells near San Carlos?

Inside San Carlos itself most homes are on city water, but just east and north of the neighborhood, in the foothills toward Santee, Lakeside and the El Cajon backcountry, private wells are common. We serve those rural and semi-rural San Diego County properties.

What is the most common reason for low well pressure?

A pressure tank that has lost its air charge is the leading cause. When the bladder fails, the pump short-cycles and pressure surges and drops. It is usually an affordable fix compared with replacing the pump.

Why does my water sometimes contain grit?

The decomposed granite common to the Cowles Mountain and Mission Trails area can work fine sediment into a well. A sediment filtration system protects your pump, fixtures and appliances from that grit.

Is it safe to adjust the pressure switch on my own?

No. The pressure switch is wired into a 230-volt circuit and can deliver a dangerous shock. Always have a licensed technician recalibrate or replace it. We can do it safely in one visit.
