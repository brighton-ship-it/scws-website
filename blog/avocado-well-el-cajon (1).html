Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your El Cajon well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well and Grove Irrigation Service in the El Cajon Valley

El Cajon, Spanish for "the box," takes its name from the broad inland valley boxed in by hills in central San Diego County. Unlike many of the coastal neighborhoods we serve, El Cajon has a real agricultural pedigree. The valley and the slopes around it, Crest, Bostonia, Fletcher Hills, Rancho San Diego, Dehesa and the country reaching up toward El Cajon Mountain, were citrus and avocado ground, and groves and well-irrigated landscapes still hold on across the hillsides and rural edges even as the valley floor has filled in with city on municipal water.

Southern California Well Service is a licensed C-57 water well contractor with more than 30 years of experience. The El Cajon area is exactly the kind of place our expertise was built for: a mix of urban valley and well-fed hillside grove country with demanding granite geology.

How a Private Well and Grove Irrigation System Works Here

On a hillside grove or rural-residential parcel around El Cajon, a submersible pump lifts water from the well casing, often several hundred feet down in the granite hills, to the surface. From there it commonly fills a storage tank that buffers the heavy, uneven draw of grove irrigation, then a pressure tank or constant-pressure controller delivers steady flow to drip lines and micro-sprinklers. Avocados in particular want long, even soakings, which is why storage and a properly tuned booster matter so much.

The geology is decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock through most of the surrounding hills, with alluvial fill along the valley drainages. In fractured-rock wells, yield hinges on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole crosses, which is why two wells a short distance apart can perform very differently.

Common Local Scenarios and Causes

  • Low or declining yield on granite hillside wells, especially late in a dry summer.
  • Hard, mineral-rich water scaling pumps and clogging drip emitters.
  • Chloride and salinity stressing avocados, since the trees are highly salt-sensitive.
  • Pressure tank waterlogging causing pump short-cycling and premature burnout.
  • Iron and sulfur showing up as staining or a rotten-egg smell on some wells.

What to Check Before You Call

Confirm the well breaker has not tripped. Read the pressure gauge; a pinned or bouncing needle signals a pump or tank fault. Listen for rapid cycling that points to a failed pressure tank. Walk visible drip lines for breaks or capped zones. Note water color, odor and any staining, and watch your avocados for leaf-tip burn that hints at salt. Do not keep restarting a pump that will not build pressure, as dry-running can destroy a motor quickly.

When to Call a Professional

Anything below the wellhead, in the control box, or in the water chemistry is a licensed-crew job. Pulling a deep granite-well submersible takes proper rigging, and pump wiring is dangerous to improvise. Lost water, collapsed pressure, a pump that never shuts off, low yield, or salt-burned grove leaves all mean it is time to call.

Designing Irrigation for a Real Grove

Because El Cajon's hills carry genuine avocado and citrus plantings, irrigation design here is not an afterthought. Avocados want deep, even, infrequent soakings that wet the entire root zone, and on decomposed-granite slopes that means running drip and micro-sprinkler zones long and steady so water penetrates instead of sheeting off. We build systems around storage tanks and constant-pressure or booster pumps precisely so a grove can drink that way, and we zone properties by slope, sun and tree age so young plantings and mature trees each get the right amount. Where the well water carries chloride or hardness, we factor blending, leaching or treatment into the plan, because a chloride-sensitive crop like avocado will tell you fast, in scorched leaf tips, when the water is wrong.

Seasonal Grove and Well Care

A productive El Cajon grove runs on a seasonal rhythm. Spring is for servicing the system before the heat: pressure tank, pump amp draw, filters and a full walk of the irrigation lines. Summer is peak demand, when fractured-rock wells show their limits and salt can concentrate in the root zone, making storage capacity and a periodic deep leaching irrigation essential to protect the trees. Fall is for water testing after the long pumping season and for replacing worn parts before winter, and even the valley's mild winters bring occasional frost worth guarding against on young trees and exposed lines. Growers who follow that calendar protect both their crop and their equipment, turning a cheap spring fix into avoided thousands in summer emergency repairs.

Boosting a Tired Hillside Well

When a granite-backed El Cajon well starts falling short of a grove's demand, you have options short of starting over. Hydrofracturing injects water under pressure to open and connect existing fractures, often restoring meaningful yield to a hillside well. In other cases, deepening or a revised pump configuration is the better play. We evaluate the well honestly and recommend the path that gives your trees reliable water for the least long-term cost, rather than defaulting to the most expensive solution.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does El Cajon have avocado and citrus heritage?

Yes. The valley and its surrounding slopes, Crest, Bostonia, Fletcher Hills and the El Cajon Mountain country, have a genuine citrus and avocado history, and groves persist on the hillsides even as the valley floor urbanized.

How deep are wells here?

It depends. Valley-floor wells can be moderate, while granite hillside wells often run several hundred feet into fractured bedrock. A site visit is the only honest estimate.

Are avocados sensitive to the water?

Very. They are chloride- and salt-sensitive, and some inland wells carry elevated chloride or hardness. We test chloride, sodium, boron and TDS.

Which neighborhoods still use wells?

Hillside and semi-rural areas like Crest, Harbison Canyon, Dehesa and the slopes toward El Cajon Mountain.

Can you improve a low-yield hillside well?

Often yes, through hydrofracturing to open new fractures, or by evaluating deepening or a new pump setup.
