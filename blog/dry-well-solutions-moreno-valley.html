Why Wells Go Dry

  • Drought Conditions: Extended dry periods lower the water table
  • Increased Demand: More wells in the area drawing from the same aquifer
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: Water table naturally drops in summer/fall
  • Pump Position: Pump may be positioned above current water level
  • Well Age: Mineral buildup reducing water entry points
  • Aquifer Depletion: Long-term overdrawing of groundwater

Solutions for Dry Wells in Moreno Valley

1. Well Deepening

If the water table has dropped, deepening your existing well may reach water again. This is often less expensive than drilling a new well.

2. Hydrofracturing

High-pressure water treatment can open new fractures in bedrock, allowing more water to flow into your well.

3. Pump Lowering

Sometimes simply lowering your pump deeper in the well can restore water production.

4. New Well Drilling

When rehabilitation isn't viable, we can drill a new well in a better location on your property.

Dry and Low-Yield Wells in Moreno Valley: Causes and Solutions

Moreno Valley sits in western Riverside County, east of Riverside and south of the Box Springs Mountains, in a broad valley that has grown rapidly over recent decades. While most residents are served by public water agencies, a number of landowners on the valley's rural edges — toward the foothills, the Reche Canyon area, and outlying agricultural parcels — still rely on private wells. For those owners, a dry or low-yield well is a real disruption, and the causes here are tied to the valley's groundwater conditions.

Southern California Well Service has more than 30 years of experience across Riverside County, and we help Moreno Valley well owners diagnose and resolve dry-well problems. Understanding the local groundwater picture is the first step.

Why Moreno Valley Wells Go Dry

Moreno Valley overlies alluvial groundwater within the broader San Jacinto and Bunker Hill-area systems of western Riverside County. Records for the area show a wide range of well depths — an average on the order of 180 feet with many shallower wells — and a static water level often around 90 feet below the surface, though depth to water ranges roughly from 100 to 150 feet in places. Several factors lead to dry wells:

  • Groundwater decline and overdraft. Parts of western Riverside County's basins have seen groundwater levels decline due to heavy demand and, in some areas, overdraft. Falling levels leave shallower private wells short.
  • Drought. Southern California's recurring droughts reduce recharge to the valley's aquifers, dropping the water table.
  • Wells drilled too shallow. Many older private wells here are relatively shallow; as levels fall, they are the first to go dry.
  • Concentrated pumping. Heavy municipal and agricultural pumping in surrounding areas can lower regional levels that private wells share.
  • Pump set above the water. A pump hanging above the current pumping level pulls air before the well is truly dry.

Confirming the Well Is Really Dry

Our technician measures the static and pumping water levels, tests the pump's amperage, and watches the recovery rate after a controlled drawdown. We compare those readings to your driller's report, inspect the pressure tank and switch, and check for sand or air. That separates a fallen water table from an equipment or placement issue or a fouled bore. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward any repair.

Solutions for a Dry Moreno Valley Well

Lower the Pump

When the well still holds water but the pump sits above it, resetting the pump deeper in the casing is the fastest, cheapest fix — $800 to $2,500 — if there is depth and water column to spare. With relatively shallow wells and a water table that has dropped modestly, this often does the job.

Deepen the Existing Well

Where a shallow well has been outpaced by declining levels, deepening to reach a more reliable zone can restore production for $5,000 to $15,000 — far less than a new well.

Storage Tank and Booster Pump

For a well that produces slowly, a storage tank and booster pump let it fill a cistern around the clock while the booster delivers strong household pressure on demand — roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed.

Flow-Management Controls

Low-water cutoffs, timers, and flow controllers protect the pump from running dry and help a modest yield keep up with demand.

Hydrofracturing

Where a well reaches the harder rock at the valley's margins, or where the screen and gravel pack have fouled, rehabilitation and hydrofracturing techniques can restore lost capacity, at $3,000 to $8,000. We will tell you honestly whether your well is a candidate.

Drill a New, Deeper Well

For a worn-out or hopelessly shallow well, a new, deeper well is the durable answer, at $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey depending on depth. We treat it as a last resort after rehabilitation is ruled out.

When to Call a Professional

Air in the faucets, sand in the water, a short-cycling pump, or sudden whole-house pressure loss are signals to shut the pump down and call a licensed C-57 contractor. Running a pump dry destroys motors fast. We are licensed and equipped to work inside the well itself, unlike a general plumber.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are private wells in Moreno Valley?

They vary widely. Records for the area show an average depth on the order of 180 feet, with many shallower wells, and a static water level often around 90 feet. Older private wells tend to be shallow and more vulnerable as groundwater levels decline.

Why is my Moreno Valley well running dry?

Most often a combination of regional groundwater decline — including overdraft in parts of western Riverside County — and drought has lowered the water table below your pump or the bottom of a shallow well. Our diagnostic confirms exactly how far the water has fallen.

Can I just lower my pump?

Often, yes. With the relatively shallow wells common here, if the well still holds water and the casing has remaining depth, resetting the pump deeper restores production for $800 to $2,500 — the least expensive fix.

Is deepening worth it, or should I drill new?

Deepening usually costs $5,000 to $15,000 versus $18,000 to $42,000 for a turnkey new well. We evaluate lowering the pump and deepening before recommending a new well, since rehabilitation is normally far cheaper.

Will a storage tank help a slow Moreno Valley well?

Yes. A storage tank and booster pump let a low-yield well fill a cistern continuously while the booster delivers strong household pressure on demand. At roughly $3,000 to $7,000, it is a cost-effective way to live comfortably on a slow well.
