Well Rehabilitation Cost: How to Restore Your Old Well

Published February 1, 2026 | 12 min read

Is your well producing less water than it used to? Before you spend $20,000-$50,000 on a new well, consider well rehabilitation. This process can restore your existing well's performance at a fraction of the cost—typically $2,000 to $15,000. In this guide, we'll explain what well rehabilitation involves, when it works, and how to decide if it's right for your situation.

What Is Well Rehabilitation?

Well rehabilitation—also called well restoration, well redevelopment, or well rejuvenation—is the process of restoring a declining well to its original or improved capacity. Over time, wells naturally lose efficiency due to mineral scaling, bacterial buildup, sediment accumulation, and other factors. Rehabilitation addresses these issues to restore water flow.

Think of it like cleaning a clogged pipe. Your well's screen and the surrounding aquifer formation become plugged with deposits that restrict water movement. Rehabilitation techniques remove these blockages and reopen the pathways that allow water to flow into your well.

Unlike drilling a new well, rehabilitation works with your existing infrastructure. When successful, it can restore 80-100% of your well's original capacity—and sometimes even improve upon it if the original development was incomplete.

Signs Your Well Needs Rehabilitation

How do you know if your well is a candidate for rehabilitation? Watch for these warning signs:

  • Gradually declining water yield: Your well produces less water than it did years ago
  • Increased pumping time: The pump runs longer to fill your pressure tank
  • Sand or sediment in water: Particles appearing where water was once clear
  • Dropping water levels: Static water level is lower than historical records show
  • Reduced water pressure: Despite a working pump and pressure tank
  • Discolored water: Brown, orange, or black tinting indicating iron bacteria or mineral issues
  • Sulfur smell: Rotten egg odor from bacterial activity in the well

If you're experiencing low water pressure or noticing your well seems to be running dry, rehabilitation might restore your water supply without the expense of drilling new.

When Does Rehabilitation Work vs. When You Need a New Well

Well rehabilitation isn't a miracle cure for every well problem. Understanding when it works—and when it doesn't—saves you money and frustration.

Rehabilitation Usually Works When:

  • Biofouling is the problem: Iron bacteria, sulfur bacteria, and slime-forming organisms can be treated effectively
  • Mineral scaling has built up: Calcium carbonate, iron oxide, and manganese deposits respond well to chemical treatment
  • Sediment has accumulated: Sand, silt, and fine particles can be removed through surging and jetting
  • The well casing is structurally sound: No cracks, holes, or severe corrosion
  • The aquifer still contains water: Regional groundwater levels haven't permanently dropped
  • Decline has been gradual: Slowly decreasing yield over years typically indicates treatable causes

A New Well Is Usually Better When:

  • The casing has failed: Holes, cracks, or collapsed sections can't be reliably repaired
  • The aquifer is depleted: Regional water tables have permanently dropped below your well depth
  • Contamination can't be eliminated: Nearby pollution sources make the water unsafe
  • Previous rehabilitation attempts failed: If two proper attempts haven't worked, structural issues likely exist
  • The well is extremely old: Wells over 50-60 years old often have multiple failing components
  • Major construction damage occurred: Earthquake damage, vehicle impact, or similar trauma

A thorough well inspection before rehabilitation helps identify whether your well is a good candidate. We use downhole cameras to assess casing condition and determine the likely cause of reduced production.

Quick Decision Guide

Compare rehabilitation vs. new well drilling:

  • New Well Cost: $15,000 - $50,000+

  • New Well Timeline: 1-2 weeks

Well Rehabilitation Methods and Costs

Several techniques are used to rehabilitate wells, often in combination. The right approach depends on what's causing your well's decline.

1. Chemical Treatment ($2,000 - $5,000)

Chemical treatment dissolves mineral deposits and kills bacteria that clog your well screen and surrounding formation. It's the most common and cost-effective rehabilitation method.

How it works:

  • Acidic solutions (typically food-grade phosphoric acid or sulfamic acid) dissolve calcium, iron, and manganese deposits
  • Chlorine or specialty biocides kill iron bacteria and slime-forming organisms
  • Chemicals are circulated through the well and into the formation
  • After treatment, the well is thoroughly flushed until water quality returns to normal

Best for: Biofouling, mineral scaling, and gradual capacity loss. Chemical treatment works well for most residential wells with moderate decline.

Cost breakdown:

  • Basic treatment: $2,000 - $3,000
  • Heavy encrustation: $3,500 - $5,000
  • Treatment time: 4-12 hours plus flush time

2. Mechanical Surging and Jetting ($2,500 - $6,000)

Mechanical methods physically agitate the well to loosen and remove debris. These techniques are often combined with chemical treatment for maximum effectiveness.

Surging:

  • A surge block is moved up and down in the casing, creating pressure waves
  • This action loosens sediment and scale from the well screen
  • Debris is then pumped or bailed out of the well

High-pressure jetting:

  • Water jets at 1,000-3,000 PSI blast through screen openings
  • Removes stubborn deposits that chemical treatment alone can't dissolve
  • Particularly effective for sand-locked screens

Best for: Sediment accumulation, sand production, and wells where chemical treatment alone hasn't been sufficient.

Cost breakdown:

  • Surging only: $2,500 - $4,000
  • High-pressure jetting: $3,500 - $6,000
  • Combined with chemical treatment: Add $1,500 - $2,500

3. Hydrofracturing ($5,000 - $15,000)

Hydrofracturing (or hydrofracking) uses high-pressure water to create new fractures in the rock formation surrounding your well. This opens additional pathways for water to reach your wellbore.

How it works:

  • Specialized equipment isolates a section of the well using inflatable packers
  • Water is pumped at extremely high pressure (500-3,000 PSI)
  • The pressure opens existing fractures and creates new ones in bedrock
  • These new fractures connect more water-bearing zones to your well

Best for: Wells in fractured rock formations (common in San Diego's granite and metamorphic geology) where the original drilling didn't intersect enough water-bearing fractures.

Cost breakdown:

  • Standard hydrofracturing: $5,000 - $8,000
  • Deep wells (400+ ft): $8,000 - $12,000
  • Multiple zones treated: $10,000 - $15,000
  • Treatment time: 4-8 hours

Hydrofracturing has an excellent success rate in our region's granite formations. It's often the best option for wells that have adequate casing but simply don't intersect enough water-bearing fractures.

4. Airlift Development ($1,500 - $3,500)

Airlift development uses compressed air to rapidly pump water and debris from the well. It's a finishing technique that removes loosened material after other treatments.

Best for: Final cleaning after chemical or mechanical treatment, and for wells with fine sediment issues.

Success Rates: What to Realistically Expect

Well rehabilitation has a strong track record, but success isn't guaranteed. Here's what the data shows:

  • Overall success rate: 70-90% when wells are properly evaluated first
  • Chemical treatment for biofouling: 80-95% success rate
  • Hydrofracturing in granite: 75-85% achieve significant improvement
  • Mechanical treatment for sediment: 85-95% success rate

"Success" typically means restoring at least 70% of the well's original capacity. Many rehabilitations achieve 90-100% restoration, and some wells actually perform better afterward than when they were new (if original development was incomplete).

The key to high success rates is proper diagnosis. A well inspection with camera survey helps identify the actual cause of decline before treatment begins. Rehabilitating a well with undiagnosed casing failure, for example, will likely fail regardless of technique.

How Long Does Well Rehabilitation Last?

A successful well rehabilitation isn't permanent—the same processes that originally caused decline will eventually recur. However, proper rehabilitation provides years of restored service:

  • Mineral scaling issues: 5-10 years before retreatment typically needed
  • Biofouling (iron bacteria): 5-15 years depending on water chemistry
  • Hydrofracturing results: Often permanent improvement since you've created new fractures
  • Sediment problems: 7-12 years with proper pump depth adjustment

You can extend rehabilitation longevity with proper well maintenance. Periodic shock chlorination, keeping your well cap sealed, and monitoring water quality all help prevent premature decline.

Can You Rehabilitate an Abandoned Well?

Many San Diego properties have old, unused wells that owners wonder about rehabilitating. This is possible in some cases, but requires careful evaluation:

Potentially Rehabilitable:

  • Wells abandoned less than 10-15 years ago
  • Wells that were properly capped (not open to contamination)
  • Wells with steel casing in good condition
  • Wells in areas where groundwater levels haven't significantly dropped

Usually Not Worth Rehabilitating:

  • Wells abandoned 30+ years ago (casing likely deteriorated)
  • Wells that were never properly sealed (contamination risk)
  • Dug wells or large-diameter wells (different construction, often unsafe)
  • Wells with unknown depth or construction details

Before investing in rehabilitating an old well, get a professional inspection. A downhole camera survey reveals casing condition, and water testing identifies any contamination issues. Sometimes the inspection reveals the old well isn't worth saving—but when it is, rehabilitation can give you a working well at much lower cost than drilling new.

The Rehabilitation Process: What to Expect

Here's what typically happens during a professional well rehabilitation:

1. Initial Assessment (Day 1)

  • Review well records and history
  • Test current yield and water level
  • Perform downhole camera inspection
  • Collect water sample for analysis
  • Determine likely cause of decline
  • Recommend treatment approach

2. Pump Removal

  • Remove existing submersible pump and drop pipe
  • Inspect pump condition (may need replacement regardless of rehabilitation)

3. Treatment Application

  • Apply selected treatment method(s)
  • Chemical treatments typically soak for 12-24 hours
  • Mechanical treatments take 4-8 hours
  • Hydrofracturing typically completes in one day

4. Development and Cleanup

  • Pump or airlift to remove loosened debris
  • Continue until water runs clear
  • Neutralize any remaining chemicals

5. Testing and Pump Reinstallation

  • Test new yield and compare to pre-treatment
  • Reinstall pump (or install new pump if needed)
  • Verify system operation
  • Test water quality

Most rehabilitations complete in 1-3 days. You'll be without water during treatment, so plan accordingly or arrange temporary supply.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does well rehabilitation cost?

Well rehabilitation typically costs between $2,000 and $15,000 depending on the method used and severity of the problem. Basic chemical treatment and surging costs $2,000-$5,000, while hydrofracturing ranges from $5,000-$15,000. Most residential well rehabilitations fall in the $3,000-$8,000 range.

How long does well rehabilitation last?

A successful well rehabilitation typically lasts 5-15 years depending on local water chemistry and geological conditions. Wells with mineral scaling may need retreatment sooner (5-7 years), while rehabilitations addressing bacterial buildup often last longer (10-15 years) with proper maintenance.

What is the success rate of well rehabilitation?

Well rehabilitation has a 70-90% success rate when the underlying cause is treatable (biofouling, mineral scaling, or sediment accumulation). Success rates are lower for wells with structural damage or aquifer depletion. A thorough well inspection before rehabilitation helps predict success likelihood.

When should I rehabilitate vs drill a new well?

Consider rehabilitation when your well has gradually lost capacity, has biofouling or mineral buildup, and the casing is structurally sound. Drill a new well when the casing is damaged beyond repair, the aquifer is depleted, contamination sources can't be eliminated, or rehabilitation has failed twice.

Can an old abandoned well be rehabilitated?

Sometimes. Abandoned wells can potentially be rehabilitated if the casing is intact, there's no contamination, and the aquifer still produces water. However, wells abandoned for many years often have deteriorated casings or sanitary seal issues that make rehabilitation impractical. A professional inspection is essential before attempting to rejuvenate an old well.
