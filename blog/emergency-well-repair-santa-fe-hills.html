The Most Common Well Emergencies We See in Santa Fe Hills

Most "no water" calls from the San Marcos area trace back to a short list of failures. Knowing which one you are facing helps you describe the symptoms accurately when you call, and it helps us arrive with the right parts on the truck.

  • Sudden total loss of water. You turn on a tap and nothing comes out. This usually points to a failed submersible pump, an electrical fault, or a well that has drawn down faster than it can recover during a dry stretch.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout. Submersible pumps in this region commonly last 8 to 15 years. Heat, sediment, voltage spikes, and rapid on-off cycling all shorten that life. A burned-out motor produces no water at all and often trips the breaker.
  • Pressure tank failure. When the bladder inside a pressure tank fails, the pump cycles on and off every few seconds. That waterlogging not only kills your water pressure but quickly destroys the pump motor if it is not corrected.
  • Well went dry or low. Granite-and-decomposed-granite geology around San Marcos means many wells are fractured-rock wells with modest storage. A drought year or heavy irrigation can pull the water level below the pump intake, producing sputtering, air in the lines, and muddy water.
  • Electrical or control box failure. Tripped breakers, failed pressure switches, burned control-box components, and lightning or surge damage are among the most frequent culprits and often the fastest to fix.
  • Burst pipe or major leak. A cracked drop pipe, a failed pitless adapter, or a broken line between the wellhead and the house can drain pressure and flood an area before you notice.
  • No water after a power outage. SDG&E outages and Public Safety Power Shutoffs are a fact of life in North County. After power is restored, pressure switches and control boxes sometimes do not reset cleanly, leaving you dry even though the grid is back.

What to Check First Before You Call

A few safe checks can occasionally restore water in minutes, and even when they do not, they give our technician a head start. Never open the well casing yourself or touch wiring while standing in water.

  1. Check the breaker. Find the breaker labeled for the well or pump in your main panel. If it has tripped, switch it fully off and back on once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us; that usually signals a motor or wiring fault.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge. A reading of zero suggests a pump or supply problem; a normal reading with no water at the tap suggests a valve, switch, or plumbing issue.
  3. Listen at the pressure tank. Tap the tank. A hollow ring up high and a solid sound low down is normal. A tank that sounds full of water all the way up is likely waterlogged.
  4. Check the pressure switch. If you can safely see the switch near the tank, look for obvious burned or corroded contacts. Do not attempt to repair it yourself.
  5. Confirm it is not the whole grid. If the power is out across Santa Fe Hills, your pump simply has no electricity; once power returns, water should follow unless a control component failed to reset.

What to Do While You Wait for Us

Once you have called and we are on the way, protect your household and conserve what water you have. Turn off the well pump breaker if it keeps tripping so you do not risk further damage to the motor. Avoid running appliances that need water. Fill a few containers from any remaining pressure for drinking and hand-washing, and set aside water for pets, livestock, or chickens, which are common on the larger Santa Fe Hills and Twin Oaks parcels. If you suspect a burst pipe or leak, shut the main valve to stop water loss and flooding. In hot weather, move pets and animals into shade and keep everyone hydrated with bottled water until service is restored.
