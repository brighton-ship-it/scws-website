How a Private Well Pressure System Actually Works

Before you can fix low pressure, it helps to understand the parts that create it. A typical Clairemont well system has four pieces working together. First, a pump (usually a submersible unit hanging deep in the casing, or a jet pump at the surface on shallower wells) lifts water out of the ground. Second, a pressure tank stores water under a cushion of compressed air so the pump does not have to run every time you open a faucet. Third, a pressure switch turns the pump on when household pressure drops to the "cut-in" setting (commonly 40 psi) and off again at the "cut-out" setting (commonly 60 psi). Fourth, the plumbing, screens, and any sediment filtration carry that water to your fixtures. When any one of these falls out of adjustment or wears out, you feel it as weak pressure at the showerhead. Most residential systems are designed to hold a steady 40 to 60 psi, so when we arrive, that band is the first thing we measure.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Clairemont Wells

San Diego's coastal sedimentary geology, the age of Clairemont's housing stock, and the region's hard, mineral-rich groundwater combine to produce a handful of recurring culprits:

  • Waterlogged pressure tank: The most common cause we find. When the air bladder fails, the tank fills with water, the pump short-cycles, and pressure swings wildly between weak and strong.
  • Failing or out-of-adjustment pressure switch: Pitted contacts or a switch set too low leave you running on the bottom of the pressure band. Often a quick, inexpensive fix.
  • Worn pump or clogged intake: Decades-old pumps in legacy Clairemont wells lose lift capacity. Hard-water scale and fine sediment from the coastal aquifer wear impellers and restrict the intake screen.
  • Mineral scale in galvanized pipe: Many mid-century homes still have galvanized steel supply lines that corrode and narrow internally, choking flow long before the well is to blame.
  • Sediment-clogged filters and screens: A neglected whole-house or inline filter can silently strangle pressure throughout the house.
  • Dropping water table: Urban development limits aquifer recharge, and during dry years the shallow coastal water table beneath Clairemont can fall, reducing the water available to the pump.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks can tell you a lot and sometimes save a service visit:

  1. Is it one fixture or the whole house? If only one faucet or shower is weak, the problem is usually a clogged aerator or cartridge, not the well. Unscrew and clean the aerator first.
  2. Read the gauge. Find the pressure gauge near your tank and watch it during use. If it never climbs past 30 to 35 psi, or swings rapidly, note that for us.
  3. Tap the tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow on top and solid (full of water) on the bottom. A tank that sounds full all the way up is likely waterlogged.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off (short-cycling) points to the tank or switch. A pump that runs constantly without building pressure points to the pump, a leak, or a dropping water level.
  5. Check for leaks. Wet spots, a spinning meter with everything off, or a sudden jump in the water bill all suggest a hidden leak bleeding off pressure.

Please leave the wiring, the well cap, and any pressurized components to a professional. The 240-volt circuits and stored energy in these systems are genuinely dangerous.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Clairemont well water pressure drop in the late afternoon?

Peak household and irrigation use, sometimes combined with a slightly lower water table during San Diego's dry season, can pull pressure down at the busiest times of day. A waterlogged tank or undersized pump makes the dip worse. We measure pressure under load to find the true cause.

Could Clairemont's hard water be causing my low pressure?

Yes. San Diego County groundwater is mineral-rich, and scale builds up inside pumps, pipes, and fixtures over time, narrowing the flow path. Descaling fixtures and adding treatment such as a softener often restores noticeably stronger flow.

Is a waterlogged pressure tank expensive to fix?

Often it is one of the more affordable repairs. Sometimes the tank only needs its air charge restored; if the bladder has failed, replacement runs $600 to $1,500. Either way it is far cheaper than letting a short-cycling tank burn out your pump.

Do I need a permit to replace a well pump in Clairemont?

Pump and tank swaps usually do not require a permit, but new wells, deepening, and certain electrical work in San Diego County do. As a licensed C-57 contractor we handle the paperwork whenever it applies.

Can low pressure mean my well is running out of water?

Sometimes. A pump that runs without building pressure can signal a falling water level or a low-yield well. We perform a yield test to confirm, then recommend the right fix, whether that is adjusting the pump setting, hydrofracturing, or other options.
