What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Normal Heights Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Normal Heights

In Normal Heights, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Normal Heights wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing and Normal Heights, San Diego: A Straight Answer

Normal Heights is a leafy, walkable neighborhood in the mid-city core of San Diego, perched on the mesa just north of Adams Avenue and south of the Mission Valley rim. Named for the old State Normal School that once trained teachers nearby, it is a dense grid of 1920s bungalows and Craftsman homes between North Park, Kensington, and University Heights. It is also, like the rest of urban San Diego, entirely on municipal water — served by the City of San Diego Public Utilities Department, not by private wells. So if you came here looking to hydrofracture a well in Normal Heights, the honest answer is that there is almost certainly no well to treat.

We would rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise. Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation technique for wells drilled into bedrock, and it has real value — just not on a mesa-top city lot connected to a water main. Below we explain why, what hydrofracturing actually is, and where it does make sense for the well owners we serve elsewhere in San Diego County.

Why Normal Heights Is Not a Hydrofracturing Candidate

Two things rule it out. First, there are no domestic wells in this neighborhood; every home draws from the city distribution system that blends imported Colorado River and State Water Project water with treated local supply. Second, the ground itself is wrong for fracturing. Normal Heights sits on the uplifted coastal mesa — sedimentary terrace deposits and sandstone formations, not the hard crystalline bedrock that hydrofracturing depends on. Even in the rare event a property had some legacy borehole, this is not the fractured granite where high-pressure injection opens productive seams.

In short, hydrofracturing solves a problem that Normal Heights does not have. If your concern is water pressure, taste, or hardness coming out of the tap, that is a plumbing-and-treatment question, and we are glad to help with it. But it is not a downhole well problem.

What Hydrofracturing Actually Is

For context, here is how the technique works where it belongs. A crew lowers an inflatable packer into a bedrock well and seals off a section of the borehole. Clean water — no chemicals — is then injected into that isolated zone at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes out the silt and mineral scale plugging the rock's natural fractures and widens those seams so groundwater can reach the well more freely. On a good fractured-rock well, yield can climb two to five times. It is a precise, well-established method — but it requires a rock well, which urban San Diego neighborhoods do not have.

Where Hydrofracturing Helps in San Diego County

The wells we treat sit out in the county's backcountry, where homes run on private groundwater and the geology is granite. Communities like Ramona, Julian, Santa Ysabel, Lakeside, Alpine, Jamul, and Descanso are built on the San Diego batholith — the same fractured crystalline rock that forms the Cuyamaca and Laguna ranges. Those wells live or die by the fractures they intersect, and when yield fades, hydrofracturing is frequently the most cost-effective rescue. If you own one of those properties and landed here, you are in the right place — you are just in the wrong neighborhood name.

Common Causes of Low Yield on Backcountry Wells

  • Fracture fouling — silt, clay, and mineral scale slowly seal the cracks that feed a granite well.
  • Drought drawdown — after dry winters, the regional water table drops and shallow seams go dry.
  • A marginal original borehole — some wells never intercepted a generous fracture network and underperformed from the start.
  • Decomposed granite infill — loose “DG” sloughs into the hole and chokes the lower screen or open section.

What to Check (If You Do Have a Well)

  • Confirm you are actually on a private well — in Normal Heights itself, you are almost certainly on city water.
  • If you own a rural parcel, pull the well completion report: depth, static level, and reported yield tell us if it is a hydrofrac candidate.
  • Check the pressure switch and the pressure tank's air charge — a $150–$350 switch or a waterlogged tank often masquerades as a weak well.
  • Note whether the decline was gradual (rehabilitation territory) or sudden (more likely a pump or controls failure).

The Mesa Geology Under Normal Heights

Normal Heights occupies one of San Diego's classic coastal mesas, the flat-topped uplands carved by canyons like the one that drops toward Mission Valley along the neighborhood's northern edge. These mesas are built from layered marine and terrace sediments — sandstones, siltstones, and conglomerates — deposited over millions of years and later lifted and dissected by erosion. It is a geology that makes for stable, well-drained home sites and the gentle hillside views Normal Heights is known for, but it is the opposite of what hydrofracturing needs. There is no shallow crystalline bedrock here laced with the water-bearing fractures that the technique reopens. Even setting aside the fact that the neighborhood has no wells, the formation itself would not respond to high-pressure injection the way mountain granite does.

This matters because reputable well work always starts with geology. A contractor who would offer to hydrofracture a Normal Heights lot is either confused about the location or not being straight with you. The honest map of San Diego County puts the fracturable rock inland and uphill, in the batholith country, while the coastal mesas like this one run on imported municipal water.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there water wells in Normal Heights?

No. Normal Heights is a built-out urban neighborhood served entirely by City of San Diego municipal water. There are effectively no private domestic wells here to hydrofracture.

So why does this page exist?

Many people researching hydrofracturing in San Diego actually own a well elsewhere in the county. This page explains why the technique does not apply on an urban mesa lot and where it does help.

My Normal Heights water pressure is low — can hydrofracturing fix it?

No. On city water, low pressure is a plumbing or regulator issue, not a well issue. Hydrofracturing only affects bedrock wells. We can point you to the right plumbing or treatment fix instead.

Where would hydrofracturing help me?

On a fractured-rock well in the county backcountry — places like Ramona, Julian, or Alpine. There, hydrofracturing can multiply a fading well's yield for a fraction of the cost of a new well.

How much does hydrofracturing cost?

Typically $3,000 to $8,000 for a qualifying bedrock well, versus $18,000 to $42,000 for a new well. It only applies where there is a rock well to treat.

Is hydrofracturing safe?

Yes — it uses only clean water under pressure, no chemicals, and when performed by a licensed C-57 contractor it does not harm the casing. It is simply not applicable to an urban, municipally served address like Normal Heights.
