How an Avocado Irrigation Well System Works

A productive avocado well system is more than a pump in a hole. For a Mountain View grove, the components have to work together to deliver steady flow during the long warm season:

  • The well and pump: A submersible pump set at the right depth in the casing lifts groundwater to the surface. Most foothill San Diego County wells run between 200 and 600 feet deep, and avocado groves typically need a pump sized for sustained gallons-per-minute rather than short household bursts.
  • Pressure tank and switch: The pressure tank cushions the system and the pressure switch tells the pump when to cycle. On a grove these wear faster than on a house because the pump runs longer and more often.
  • Storage and booster: Many Mountain View-area growers store water in a tank and re-pressurize it with a booster or constant-pressure system so the drip lines see even pressure across the whole block.
  • Filtration and treatment: Sediment filters protect emitters from sand, and where iron, manganese, or chloride are high, additional treatment keeps the trees and the hardware healthy.
  • Drip and micro-sprinkler distribution: The water finally reaches the root zone through emitters tuned to each tree's age and the slope of the ground.

When any one of these links fails, the trees feel it first. Catching the weak link early is the difference between a service call and a crop loss.

Common Well Problems We See Around Mountain View

Since 2020 in San Diego County, the issues that bring Mountain View-area growers to the phone follow a familiar pattern, shaped by the local geology and climate:

  • Hard-water scale and mineral buildup: The granite-derived groundwater here carries dissolved minerals that crust on pump components, pressure tanks, and emitters, slowly choking flow.
  • Chloride creep: As wells age and water tables drop in dry years, chloride concentrations can climb. Avocados are among the most chloride-sensitive crops grown in California, and leaf-tip burn is an early warning.
  • Declining yield in drought: Southern California's multi-year dry cycles lower the water table; a well that was strong a decade ago may now struggle to keep up with peak summer demand.
  • Pressure-tank and switch failure: Worn switches cause the pump to short-cycle, which burns out motors and starves the drip system.
  • Sediment and turbidity: Fine decomposed granite migrates into the casing and plugs filters and emitters faster than growers expect.

What a Mountain View Grower Can Check Before Calling

A few quick observations help you describe the problem and sometimes save a trip:

  1. Listen and look at the pressure tank. Rapid clicking of the pressure switch or a pump that cycles every few seconds points to a tank or switch issue.
  2. Check the breaker. Rural and semi-rural circuits trip during power fluctuations; a reset sometimes restores water, but a breaker that trips again signals an electrical or motor fault.
  3. Inspect a few emitters. If output is uneven across the block, the problem may be filtration or pressure regulation rather than the well itself.
  4. Note any change in water color or smell. Rusty water, a sulfur odor, or new grit are clues a technician can act on immediately.
  5. Watch the trees. Tip burn, wilting at midday, or sudden fruit drop tell you the root zone is not getting what it needs.

Write down what you see and roughly when it started. The more detail you give us, the faster we diagnose.

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Some grove tasks are safe for an owner; most well repairs are not. Pulling a submersible pump from a 400-foot San Diego County well requires a rig, and wiring a three-phase motor or a constant-pressure controller is licensed electrical work. Call a C-57 contractor when you have no water, when the pump runs but no water reaches the surface, when you smell burning at the control box, or when water quality changes suddenly. Trying to muscle a pump out by hand or guessing at wiring risks both your safety and an expensive casing repair.

Building a Maintenance Habit That Prevents Emergencies

The most reliable groves we serve in San Diego County are the ones whose owners treat the well as a system to maintain rather than a black box to ignore until it fails. We encourage Mountain View-area growers to schedule a check before the warm season, measuring pump amperage, pressure-switch operation, and tank pre-charge, and to run an irrigation-water test for chloride and hardness so any treatment is in place before the trees are stressed. A short annual log of pump run times turns a vague worry into a precise diagnosis, because a pump that slowly runs longer to deliver the same water is signaling wear or a dropping table well before it quits.

Spreading bigger projects across the calendar protects both the crop and the budget. Hydrofracturing a weak well, adding storage, or installing a constant-pressure system is best done after harvest, when a few days of downtime costs nothing. This kind of planning is far cheaper than an emergency in July, and it is exactly where a local crew that already knows your grove earns its keep.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are wells near Mountain View in San Diego County?

Most wells in the San Diego County foothills around Mountain View run between 200 and 600 feet deep, drilled through decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock. Valley-bottom wells in alluvial sand can be shallower. The right depth depends on where the productive fractures or aquifer layers sit on your specific parcel, which is why a local well log matters.

Why are avocado trees so sensitive to my well water quality?

Avocados are one of the most chloride-sensitive crops in California. Even moderate chloride in irrigation water accumulates in the leaves and causes tip burn, reduced photosynthesis, and lower yield. We test Mountain View well water for chloride and other avocado-critical parameters and recommend blending, leaching, or treatment when levels are too high.

My grove lost water pressure during a heat wave. What is wrong?

During peak San Diego County summer demand, a sudden pressure drop usually means a failing pressure switch or tank, a pump that can no longer keep up with the lowered water table, or a clogged filter. Check the breaker and pressure switch first, then call for a diagnostic. We offer same-day emergency service because avocados can drop fruit within days of water stress.
