Sand in Your Colton Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Colton well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Colton well.

Sand and Sediment in Colton Well Water: What San Bernardino County Owners Need to Know

If you own a private well in or around Colton and your water has turned gritty, cloudy, or you are finding sand settled in the bottom of the toilet tank, you are dealing with a problem that will not fix itself. Colton sits in the western part of San Bernardino County, in the Rialto-Colton groundwater basin along the Santa Ana River and Warm Creek corridor. The alluvial deposits here—coarse sand and gravel laid down by the river channel—make for productive wells, but they also make sand intrusion a genuine and recurring risk. Because sand is abrasive, running a well that is producing it steadily wears out pumps, clogs tanks, and damages appliances. Here is how sandy water happens in the Colton area, how we find the cause, and what the repairs realistically cost.

Why Colton Wells Produce Sand

The Rialto-Colton Basin is fed by underflow and recharge off the San Gabriel Mountains and the Badlands, with seepage from the Santa Ana River and Warm Creek. The upper water-bearing units are made largely of river-channel deposits—coarse-grained, loosely consolidated material that a well screen has to hold back. Many local wells are screened across these sandy zones, and some deeper Colton wells are screened well below 400 feet. When the balance between the screen, the pump, and the formation shifts, sand starts coming up the column. The usual culprits are:

  • A worn or failed well screen. The screen's slots are sized to the surrounding sand. Corrosion and years of pumping enlarge those openings, and the coarse river-channel sand that the screen used to exclude now flows straight through.
  • A pump set too low in the borehole. Sediment settles at the bottom of every well. A pump set down in that layer—or one that has slipped—pulls sand with every cycle.
  • An oversized or over-pumped well. Pulling water faster than the aquifer can cleanly yield increases the velocity of water entering the screen, and higher velocity mobilizes sand. This is especially common where a pump was upsized for irrigation without checking the well's safe yield.
  • A falling water table. Heavy basin pumping and dry years lower static levels. As the water column above the pump shrinks, the pump effectively sits closer to the sediment and draws harder, kicking up fines.
  • A corroded or breached casing. Steel casing in older Colton wells corrodes over decades. A single hole lets the surrounding formation pour in.

Local Scenarios We See Around Colton

A very common Colton call is the agricultural or large-lot property whose water turns sandy mid-summer, when irrigation demand pushes the pump hard and the water table is at its seasonal low—a classic over-pumping and drawdown combination. Another is the older established well, clean for years, that abruptly starts producing coarse sand: usually a screen that has finally corroded or a casing breach. Because the basin's river-channel sand is relatively coarse, Colton owners often notice actual grit they can feel between their fingers rather than fine silt, which tends to make the screen or casing cause more likely than a purely fine-sediment pump-setting issue.

We also see problems tied to the basin's barriers and faults. The Rialto-Colton Fault and other partial barriers within the basin mean water levels and formation character can change noticeably over short distances, so two neighbors a quarter-mile apart can have very different wells. A pump and screen configuration that worked fine for one property may over-draw the formation on another, which is one more reason a local, on-site diagnosis beats a generic phone estimate. Owners who irrigate pasture or orchard acreage are especially prone to seasonal sand, because their pumps run for long, continuous stretches that steadily lower the water column and increase entrance velocity at the screen.

What to Check Before You Call

Gather a few facts first. Draw water into a clear jar from an outdoor tap and let it settle—note whether it is coarse sand, fine silt, or rusty sediment, and how much collects. Note whether the grit is constant or only appears on start-up or after long irrigation runs. Check your pressure tank, spin-down filter, and toilet tanks for buildup. Listen for a pump that cycles on and off too frequently or a drop in pressure. And recall whether anything changed recently—a new or larger pump, a dry season, or a jump in water use. All of this narrows the diagnosis.

Resist the urge to keep running the system and hope it clears. In coarse-sand basins like Rialto-Colton, continued pumping of sandy water is the quickest way to destroy a pump and drive grit into every valve and appliance downstream.

How We Fix Sandy Well Water in Colton

There is no single “sand cure”—the correct repair depends on the cause, so we diagnose before we prescribe:

  1. Diagnosis. We measure the sand load, check pump amperage and cycling, and typically pull the pump or run a downhole camera to inspect the screen and casing. The diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward any repair.
  2. Right-size and reset the pump. If the pump is oversized for the well's safe yield or set too deep in the sediment zone, matching it to the aquifer and raising the setting depth frequently ends the sand on its own.
  3. Sediment separation. A centrifugal sand separator or spin-down filter at the wellhead removes grit before it reaches your plumbing. Installed, sediment filtration runs $300–$900 and is well worth it in a coarse-sand basin.
  4. Screen replacement and well rehabilitation. When the screen has failed, we install a new stainless steel screen and redevelop the well to re-stabilize the surrounding river-channel sand.
  5. Casing repair. A breached casing is sealed or relined to shut off the sand pathway.

Where sand has already done downstream damage, expect a pressure switch at $150–$350, a pressure tank at $600–$1,500, a control box or capacitor at $400–$900, and a full submersible pump replacement at $2,500–$5,500. If a booster or constant-pressure system is involved, that runs $2,000–$4,500. Only when a well is truly beyond rehabilitation does a new turnkey well ($18,000–$42,000) come into play—another reason to address sand early.

When to Call a Professional

Reach out immediately if the sand is heavy or constant, if pressure is dropping, if the pump is short-cycling, or if you have already found grit in the pressure tank or water heater. Those are signs of an active problem that worsens with every cycle. Pulling a submersible pump, camera-inspecting casing hundreds of feet down, and redeveloping a well require a licensed contractor and specialized equipment—this is not a job for a general handyman or a homeowner. A same-day service call now costs a fraction of an emergency pump replacement later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the sand in my Colton well water so coarse?

Colton sits in the Rialto-Colton Basin, where the aquifer is largely coarse river-channel sand and gravel from the Santa Ana River. Coarse grit usually points to a failing screen or a casing breach rather than a fine-sediment pump-setting issue—a downhole inspection confirms which.

Can I just keep using the water until it clears?

No. Running a pump in sandy water abrades the impellers and seals and can ruin the pump in weeks. It also drives grit into your pressure switch, tank, water heater, and appliances. Stop hard use and have it diagnosed.

Does a filter solve the problem?

A spin-down filter or centrifugal separator ($300–$900) protects your household plumbing and appliances, but it treats the symptom, not the cause. It works best paired with a mechanical repair of the screen, pump, or casing.

My water turned sandy during summer irrigation—why?

Heavy summer pumping lowers the water table and pulls water fast, which increases the velocity at the screen and lifts sand. Right-sizing the pump to the well's safe yield and adjusting the setting depth usually resolves seasonal sand.
