Well Inspection Before Buying a House

You've found your dream property in San Diego's backcountry—but it has a private well. Before you sign on the dotted line, a professional well inspection is absolutely essential. Unlike municipal water where the city guarantees supply and quality, with a private well, you're buying complete responsibility for your water system. Here's everything you need to know to protect yourself.

✅ Pre-Purchase Well Inspection Must-Haves

  • ☐ Flow rate test (GPM)
  • ☐ Recovery/yield test
  • ☐ Water quality testing (bacteria, nitrates, minerals)
  • ☐ Pump and equipment evaluation
  • ☐ Wellhead and casing inspection
  • ☐ Review of well records/permits
  • ☐ Pressure tank and system check

Why You MUST Get a Well Inspection

A general home inspection typically includes only a cursory look at the well system—checking that water flows when you turn on the tap. That's nowhere near enough. Here's what could go wrong without a proper well inspection:

💰 $15,000-$50,000+

Cost to drill a new well if the existing one fails or has insufficient yield

💰 $3,000-$8,000

Cost to replace a failed pump system

⚠️ Health Risk

Contaminated water can cause serious illness, especially for children and elderly

😰 No Water

Low-yield wells may not support your planned water use (irrigation, pool, livestock)

A $400-$600 inspection is cheap insurance against these scenarios. Many San Diego real estate transactions in areas like Ramona, Alpine, Fallbrook, and Valley Center have fallen through or been renegotiated significantly based on well inspection findings.

What a Comprehensive Well Inspection Includes

1. Wellhead and Casing Inspection

The inspector examines the visible components of your well:

  • Well cap/seal: Should be secure and sanitary (no insects, debris entry)
  • Casing condition: No visible cracks, corrosion, or damage
  • Proper elevation: Casing should extend above ground level to prevent surface water entry
  • Grout seal: Space around casing should be properly sealed
  • Setback distances: Adequate distance from septic system, fuel tanks, etc.

2. Flow Rate Test

This measures how many gallons per minute (GPM) your well can deliver. The inspector runs water at full capacity and measures output over time.

Flow Rate Assessment Suitable For
Under 1 GPM Poor Major concern—may need new well or storage system
1-3 GPM Marginal Minimal household use only, storage tank recommended
3-5 GPM Adequate Basic residential needs for small family
5-10 GPM Good Normal residential use, some irrigation
10+ GPM Excellent Full residential plus irrigation, livestock, pool

3. Recovery/Yield Test

Even more important than flow rate is recovery rate—how quickly does the well replenish after pumping? The inspector may run an extended test (often 4 hours) to evaluate:

  • Static water level (level before pumping)
  • Pumping water level (level during sustained pumping)
  • Drawdown (difference between the two)
  • Recovery time (how long to return to static level)

⚠️ San Diego Seasonal Consideration

Well yields in San Diego County can vary significantly by season. A well tested in March after winter rains may produce much more than the same well in September after a dry summer. Ask when the test was performed and consider retesting if done during high water table season.

4. Water Quality Testing

A comprehensive water test should check for:

Essential Tests (Minimum)

  • Coliform bacteria: Indicates contamination (must be absent)
  • E. coli: Dangerous pathogen (must be absent)
  • Nitrates: Common from septic or agricultural runoff
  • pH: Affects corrosivity and treatment needs
  • Total dissolved solids (TDS): Overall water quality indicator

Recommended Additional Tests for San Diego County

  • Arsenic: Naturally occurring in some areas
  • Iron and manganese: Common in East County wells
  • Hardness: Very high throughout the region
  • Sulfur/hydrogen sulfide: Causes odor issues
  • Uranium: Found in some granite aquifers

5. Pump and Equipment Inspection

The inspector evaluates all mechanical components:

  • Pump type and age: Submersible or jet pump, when installed
  • Pump capacity: Is it appropriately sized for the well?
  • Pressure tank: Condition, proper pre-charge, signs of waterlogging
  • Pressure switch: Functioning properly, appropriate cut-in/cut-out
  • Control box: Condition of electrical components
  • Electrical connections: Safe, up to code

6. Well Records Review

A good inspector will review available documentation:

  • Well driller's log: Original well construction details
  • Well permit: Legal authorization for the well
  • Previous water tests: History of water quality
  • Repair records: Past pump replacements, well rehabilitation
  • Depth and casing information: Critical for future service

🚨 Missing Records Red Flag

If the seller can't provide well records and the county has no permit on file, the well may have been drilled illegally or be very old. This can create problems for insurance, financing, and future permits. In San Diego County, you can check well records through the County DEH or search the state DWR well completion report database.

Red Flags to Watch For

Certain findings should make you pause—or walk away:

🚨 Bacteria Contamination

Coliform or E. coli present. While sometimes treatable with shock chlorination, recurring contamination indicates a serious problem with the well construction or a nearby contamination source.

🚨 Very Low Yield (Under 2 GPM)

May require expensive storage tank system, severe water restrictions, or eventually a new well. Calculate if this meets your water needs.

🚨 Failing Recovery

If the well doesn't recover to static level within 24 hours of pumping, it may be depleting or the aquifer is stressed.

⚠️ Old Pump (15+ years)

Not necessarily a deal-breaker, but budget $2,000-$5,000 for replacement soon.

⚠️ High Nitrates

Over 10 mg/L requires treatment or alternative water for drinking. Indicates contamination from septic or agriculture.

⚠️ Damaged Wellhead

Cracked casing, improper seal, or cap issues allow contamination entry. Repairable but negotiate the cost.

Using Inspection Results in Negotiations

Well inspection findings are powerful negotiating tools. Here's how to use them:

Option 1: Request Repairs Before Closing

Ask the seller to fix issues before the sale completes. This works well for:

  • Wellhead repairs and cap replacement
  • Shock chlorination for bacteria
  • Pressure tank replacement
  • Electrical/safety issues

Option 2: Negotiate Price Reduction

Get quotes for needed repairs and ask for a price reduction to cover them. This is often preferred because:

  • You control the quality of work
  • You choose your own contractor
  • You're not relying on seller's repairs

💡 Pro Tip

Get written repair estimates from a licensed well contractor (like Southern California Well Service) to support your negotiation. A professional estimate carries more weight than a guess.

Option 3: Closing Credits

Ask for a credit at closing rather than price reduction. This can have advantages for financing and keeps more cash in your pocket for immediate repairs.

Option 4: Walk Away

For serious issues—persistent contamination, extremely low yield, or a well that may need replacement—walking away is sometimes the best option. A $500 inspection fee is much better than a $40,000 well drilling bill after you've closed.

Questions to Ask Before Buying

Beyond the inspection, ask the seller (and verify through records):

  1. When was the well drilled?
  2. How deep is the well?
  3. When was the pump last replaced?
  4. Has the well ever run dry or low?
  5. Have there been any water quality issues?
  6. Is there a well permit on file?
  7. Are there any shared well agreements?
  8. What treatment systems are installed?
  9. How much does water treatment cost monthly?
  10. Has the well ever been rehabilitated or deepened?

Special Considerations for San Diego County Properties

Areas with Known Water Quality Issues

  • Julian/Pine Valley: High iron and manganese common
  • Ramona: Variable yields, some arsenic concerns
  • Valley Center/Pauma Valley: Hard water, agricultural nitrate risk
  • Borrego Springs: Very hard water, deep wells required
  • Alpine/Descanso: Iron staining issues common

Drought and Water Rights

San Diego County has experienced severe droughts, and water availability is a real concern. Ask about:

  • Historical performance during drought years
  • Whether neighbors have had well problems
  • Any water district expansion plans (future municipal connection?)
  • Groundwater basin status (adjudicated or not)

FHA and VA Loan Requirements

If you're using government-backed financing, be aware of specific requirements:

FHA Well Requirements

  • Well must be at least 50 feet from septic tank
  • At least 100 feet from septic drain field
  • Water quality must meet EPA standards
  • Flow rate typically must be at least 3-5 GPM
  • Well must be sole water source (or shared agreement recorded)

VA Loan Requirements

  • Similar distance requirements as FHA
  • Water must be potable (safe to drink)
  • Adequate quantity for household needs
  • Individual water supply must be clearly noted

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a well inspection before buying a house?

Absolutely yes. A well inspection is essential when buying a property with a private well. Unlike municipal water, you're buying responsibility for your entire water system. A thorough inspection reveals the well's condition, water quality, production capacity, and potential expensive repairs. Most lenders require it, but even if not required, skipping the inspection could cost you thousands in unexpected repairs.

What does a well inspection include?

A comprehensive well inspection should include: visual inspection of the wellhead, casing, and cap; pump and pressure system evaluation; flow rate test (gallons per minute); water quality testing for bacteria, nitrates, and basic parameters; review of well records and permits; inspection of pressure tank and controls; and a recovery test to verify adequate yield.

How much does a well inspection cost?

In San Diego County, expect to pay $300-$600 for a basic well inspection including water quality testing. More comprehensive inspections with extended pump tests, video inspection of the well, or additional water quality panels can run $500-$1,000+. This is a small price compared to the cost of major well repairs ($3,000-$15,000+) or drilling a new well ($15,000-$50,000+).

What are red flags in a well inspection?

Major red flags include: bacteria contamination (coliform or E. coli); low flow rate (under 3 GPM for residential); rapid pressure drop during flow test; damaged or deteriorating wellhead/casing; pump that's 15+ years old; missing well permit or records; nearby contamination sources (septic, agriculture); high nitrates, arsenic, or other contaminants; and seller reluctance to allow testing.

Can I negotiate based on well inspection results?

Yes, well inspection results are commonly used for negotiation. If issues are found, you can: request the seller make repairs before closing; negotiate a price reduction to cover repair costs; ask the seller to credit you at closing for repairs; or walk away if problems are severe (within your contingency period). Get repair estimates from a well contractor to support your negotiation position.
