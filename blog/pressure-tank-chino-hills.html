How a Pressure Tank Actually Works

Your pressure tank does one deceptively simple job: it stores water under pressure so your well pump doesn't have to start every time you open a tap. Inside a modern bladder tank, a flexible rubber diaphragm separates the water from a cushion of compressed air. As water fills the tank, it squeezes that air; as you draw water, the compressed air pushes it back out to your fixtures. That air cushion — called the pre-charge — is the heart of the system.

The pump runs between two set points controlled by the pressure switch, commonly 30/50 or 40/60 PSI. The pump kicks on at the lower number and shuts off at the higher one. The volume of water you actually get between those two points is the drawdown, and it is always far less than the tank's physical size. A 44-gallon tank might only deliver 12 to 16 gallons of usable drawdown. When the pre-charge is correct — typically set 2 PSI below the pump cut-in pressure — the tank delivers its full drawdown and the pump enjoys long, healthy run cycles.

What Goes Wrong: Waterlogging and Ruptured Bladders

The most common failure we see in Chino Hills is a waterlogged tank. Over years of cycling, the air pre-charge slowly bleeds off, or the bladder develops a leak or tears completely. Once the air cushion is gone, water fills nearly the entire tank and there is almost no drawdown left. The result is short cycling — the pump snapping on and off every few seconds every time someone runs a faucet or flushes a toilet. Short cycling is not just annoying; it burns out pump motors, pressure switches, and control components, turning a modest tank repair into an expensive pump replacement if it's ignored.

Signs Your Chino Hills Pressure Tank Is Failing

  • Rapid on/off pump cycling — the classic waterlogged-tank symptom
  • Pressure that surges then drops during a single shower
  • Water spitting from the Schrader (air) valve on top of the tank — a sure sign the bladder has ruptured
  • A tank that feels heavy and full when you rock it, with no hollow air space at the top
  • Visible rust, weeping, or corrosion at the seams or base of the tank
  • Water hammer — banging pipes when fixtures shut off

Diagnosing the Problem the Right Way

Before we ever quote a replacement, we diagnose. We shut off the pump, open a faucet to bleed the system to zero pressure, and check the air pre-charge at the Schrader valve with an accurate gauge. If the tank holds no air — or spits water — the bladder is finished. If the pre-charge is simply low but the bladder is intact, a straightforward re-charge with a compressor may restore normal operation. We also verify the pressure switch cut-in and cut-out settings and inspect the switch contacts, because a pitted switch and a waterlogged tank often fail together. Our diagnostic fee is $125, and we credit it toward any repair we perform, so an honest inspection never costs you extra.

What to Check Before You Call

If you're comfortable and safe doing so, a few quick observations help us arrive prepared. Watch the pressure gauge during a single fixture use — does the pump cycle more than once? Tap the side of the tank low and high; a healthy tank sounds solid at the bottom and hollow near the top. Never try to add air to a tank with the pump running or the system pressurized. When in doubt, leave it to us — misreading a waterlogged tank as a pump problem is one of the most common (and costly) DIY mistakes.

Sizing and Replacement

When a tank needs to be replaced, sizing it correctly is what protects your pump. We size based on your pump's actual flow rate in gallons per minute and your household's peak demand — not just the number of bathrooms. The goal is a run cycle of at least one to two minutes so the motor isn't hammered by constant restarts. A small 1-2 bathroom Chino Hills home may do fine on a 20-32 gallon tank, while larger homes with irrigation, a pool, or livestock often need 44-86 gallons or a paired-tank setup. We install quality bladder tanks matched to your system and set the pre-charge precisely before we leave.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my pressure tank is waterlogged?

The tell-tale sign is a pump that short cycles — turning on and off every few seconds when you use water. Press the Schrader air valve on top: if water sprays out, the bladder has ruptured. A waterlogged tank also feels uniformly heavy with no hollow air space at the top.

Can a pressure tank be repaired, or does it need replacing?

If the bladder is intact and only the air pre-charge is low, we can often re-charge it. But once the internal bladder tears — which is what usually causes waterlogging — the tank cannot be repaired and needs replacement. We always diagnose before recommending either.

What PSI should my pressure tank air charge be set to?

The air pre-charge should be set about 2 PSI below your pump's cut-in pressure, measured with the tank empty of water. For a 30/50 switch that means roughly 28 PSI; for a 40/60 switch, about 38 PSI. Setting it incorrectly kills drawdown and shortens pump life.

Why does my well pump turn on and off so fast?

Rapid cycling almost always points to a waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air cushion. Less commonly it can be a leaking check valve or a pressure switch problem. Because short cycling destroys pumps quickly, it's worth diagnosing right away.

How long do pressure tanks last in San Bernardino County?

A quality bladder tank typically lasts 10 to 15 years. Mineral-rich water, common on Chino Hills wells, can shorten that lifespan, while a correctly set pre-charge and periodic checks help you reach the upper end of the range.
