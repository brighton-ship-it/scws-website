The Honest Truth About Avocados in Barstow

Let us start with candor, because it serves you better than marketing copy. Barstow is a high desert city in the Mojave, sitting in San Bernardino County at the junction of Interstate 15 and Interstate 40, along the usually dry channel of the Mojave River. The climate here is hot, arid, and extreme — blistering triple-digit summers, hard winter freezes, very low annual rainfall, and big day-to-night temperature swings. Avocados, by contrast, are subtropical trees that demand mild, frost-free, low-salinity conditions. They simply do not thrive in this environment, and there is no meaningful commercial avocado industry in Barstow or the surrounding Mojave. The genuine Southern California avocado belt lies far to the south and west, in coastal-influenced San Diego County communities such as Fallbrook, Bonsall, Valley Center, and Pauma Valley.

So why does this page exist? Because the real water challenges that face desert property owners around Barstow — declining water tables, salty groundwater, hard-water scale, iron and arsenic, and the constant battle against evaporation — are every bit as demanding as those of a coastal grove, and arguably tougher. If you own a home, a small ranch, or an irrigated parcel out here, your well is your lifeline. This guide explains how desert wells and irrigation actually work in the Mojave, what commonly goes wrong, and when it makes sense to call a licensed professional.

How a Desert Well and Irrigation System Works Here

Barstow has essentially no surface water to rely on. The Mojave River runs mostly underground, and groundwater is the primary water source for the region. The Mojave River groundwater basin is structured in two broad layers. There is a shallow floodplain aquifer, generally found from roughly 50 to 200 feet down near the old river channel, and a deeper regional aquifer that can extend up to about 1,000 feet thick away from the river. Water tables in the basin have declined over the decades from pumping, and the basin is adjudicated and actively managed, which means how much water can be drawn is regulated.

What this means in practice is that wells in and around Barstow often need to reach deep, and pumping from depth requires correctly sized equipment. A typical domestic system out here includes a submersible pump set in the casing, a pressure tank and pressure switch that maintain household pressure, and often a storage tank that lets a modest well refill slowly while still delivering water on demand during peak hours. For irrigation, the smart approach in this climate is high-efficiency drip rather than overhead spray. Evaporation rates in the Mojave are punishing, and drip places water directly at the root zone where the plant can use it before the desert air steals it. Storage plus drip is how desert growers and rural homeowners stretch a limited, regulated water supply.

Desert Agriculture Realities Nearby

While avocados are off the table, agriculture does happen in pockets of the high desert. Newberry Springs, east of Barstow, supports some alfalfa and pistachio operations — crops far better suited to heat, freezes, and the saltier groundwater common here. These desert-adapted plantings still depend on deep wells, large storage, and disciplined irrigation scheduling. The lesson for any landowner is the same: match your crop and your water system to the realities of the Mojave rather than to a coastal ideal.

Common Local Well Scenarios in the Mojave

Over years of work across the high desert and the wider region, the same handful of problems come up again and again. Knowing them helps you spot trouble early.

Declining Water Table and Deep Pumping

As regional water levels drop, a pump that was once comfortably below the water level can end up too high, leading to air in the lines, short cycling, or a pump that runs but produces little. Sometimes the fix is lowering the pump; sometimes it is deepening or rehabilitating the well. In some cases hydrofracturing — injecting pressurized water to open up fractures around the borehole — can restore yield in a tired well at a fraction of the cost of drilling new.

Salinity and High TDS

Desert groundwater frequently carries high total dissolved solids and salinity. Salty water stresses plants and, over time, builds up salts in the soil. The standard agronomic answer is leaching — applying extra water periodically to flush accumulated salts below the root zone — paired with regular soil and water testing so you know exactly what you are dealing with. For household use, high TDS may call for treatment.

Hard Water and Scale

Mojave groundwater is typically very hard and mineral-rich. Hard water leaves scale inside pipes, water heaters, fixtures, and irrigation emitters. Scale narrows lines, clogs drip emitters, and shortens the life of pumps and appliances. A water softener or anti-scale treatment protects both the plumbing and the irrigation system.

Iron, Arsenic, Fluoride, and Nitrate

Iron is a common nuisance here, leaving rusty stains and a metallic taste, and it can foul equipment. More seriously, some desert wells show elevated arsenic, and occasionally fluoride or nitrate, which are health concerns at higher concentrations. These are not problems to guess about. Laboratory testing tells you what is present, and targeted treatment — from iron and manganese filtration to specialized arsenic removal — brings water within safe limits.

Sediment and Frost

Sand and silt in well water wear out pumps, clog filters, and damage valves and emitters. Sediment filtration protects the whole system. And although it is not a water-quality issue, frost is a genuine hazard in Barstow: exposed wellheads, pressure tanks, and above-ground lines can freeze on cold desert nights, so insulation and proper installation matter.

What to Check Before You Call

A few simple observations can save you time and money and help your technician arrive prepared.

  • Pressure behavior: Note whether pressure is steadily low, fluctuating, or dropping over time, and whether the pump cycles on and off rapidly.
  • Sounds and stains: Listen for the pump short cycling or running constantly, and look for rust staining, cloudiness, sediment, or any change in smell or taste.
  • The pressure tank and switch: A waterlogged tank or a worn pressure switch is a frequent, inexpensive culprit behind erratic pressure.
  • Electrical basics: Confirm the breaker has not tripped, since rural power fluctuations can knock a pump offline.
  • Recent changes: Did the problem start suddenly or build slowly? Sudden loss often points to equipment failure; gradual decline can point to a falling water table or scaling.

When to Call a Professional

Anything involving the pump itself, the wellhead, electrical wiring, or the water column belongs to a licensed contractor. Pulling a submersible pump from a deep desert well takes specialized equipment and experience, and guesswork is expensive. Call a professional when you have lost water entirely, when pressure problems persist after checking the tank and breaker, when testing reveals arsenic or other contaminants, when you suspect declining yield, or when you are planning a new well or a major irrigation upgrade. A licensed C-57 water well contractor is the right partner — not a general plumber — because well work is a regulated specialty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I actually grow avocados in Barstow?

Realistically, no — not at any commercial scale. The desert heat, hard winter freezes, aridity, and salty groundwater are all hostile to avocado trees, which need mild, frost-free, low-salinity conditions. The real avocado belt is in coastal-influenced San Diego County. In the high desert, alfalfa and pistachios near Newberry Springs are far more realistic crops.

How deep are wells around Barstow?

It depends on location relative to the Mojave River. Shallower wells near the old river channel may tap the floodplain aquifer at roughly 50 to 200 feet, while wells away from the river often reach into the deeper regional aquifer, which can extend toward 1,000 feet. Declining water tables mean depth requirements have generally increased over time.

Why does my desert well water taste salty or leave stains?

High total dissolved solids and salinity are common in Mojave groundwater and produce a salty or mineral taste. Rusty staining usually means iron, and a metallic edge can accompany hard, mineral-rich water. Laboratory testing identifies exactly what is present so the right treatment can be chosen.

Should I worry about arsenic in my well?

It is worth checking. Some desert wells in the region show elevated arsenic, and occasionally fluoride or nitrate, all of which are health concerns above certain levels. The only way to know is a water test. If contaminants are found, targeted treatment systems can bring your water back within safe limits.

My pump runs but I get very little water. What is happening?

Several things can cause this: a dropping water table that has left the pump too high, a failing pump, severe scale buildup, or a clogged sediment screen. A technician can measure water levels and test the equipment to pinpoint the cause. Sometimes hydrofracturing or lowering the pump restores yield without drilling a new well.

How can I irrigate efficiently with so much evaporation?

Drip irrigation is the single most effective tool in the desert because it delivers water straight to the root zone with minimal loss to the air. Pairing drip with on-site storage lets a modest, regulated well keep up with demand, and periodic leaching keeps salts from accumulating in the soil.
