What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your University City Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

In University City, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many University City wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in University City: An Honest Assessment

University City is a dense, built-out community in the city of San Diego, sitting between La Jolla, Clairemont, and the UC San Diego campus in central San Diego County. Bisected by Interstate 5 and the 805, it is one of the most thoroughly urbanized parts of the county, and virtually every home and business here is connected to treated municipal water supplied through the City of San Diego system. That reality shapes the honest answer to the question this page exists to address: for the overwhelming majority of University City properties, hydrofracturing is not the right service, simply because there is no private well to treat.

We believe in giving straight answers rather than selling a service that does not fit. If you are a University City homeowner experiencing low water pressure or water-quality concerns, the cause almost certainly lies in your plumbing, a pressure regulator, or the municipal supply, not in a backyard well. Below we explain what hydrofracturing actually is, when it genuinely applies, and what the real solutions are for the water problems people in University City tend to have.

What Hydrofracturing Actually Does

Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation technique for private water wells drilled into hard or fractured rock. A crew isolates a section of the well borehole with inflatable packers and injects clean water at high pressure, commonly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, to clear and widen the natural fractures that carry groundwater into the well. In the right setting, this can multiply a low-yield well's output. The key phrase is the right setting: the property must have a private well, and that well must draw from fractured bedrock rather than loose sediment. University City, built on coastal terraces and largely served by city water, fits neither condition for the typical lot.

When Hydrofracturing Would Apply Near University City

There are narrow exceptions. A few larger or older parcels on the edges of the area, an institutional or landscape-irrigation well, or a property inherited with a long-disused well could conceivably have a private well in rock. If that describes your situation, hydrofracturing may be worth evaluating. But these cases are rare in University City, and we would confirm the well's construction and geology before recommending any high-pressure work.

Why Coastal San Diego Is Not Well Country

It helps to understand why hydrofracturing thrives in the backcountry but not in University City. The productive private wells our crews treat in places like Ramona, Valley Center, and the mountain communities are drilled into the granitic and metamorphic bedrock of the Peninsular Ranges, where groundwater moves through a network of cracks that high pressure can clean and extend. University City, by contrast, sits on flat-lying coastal terrace sediments above marine bedrock, a setting that historically did not support the kind of reliable, high-yield domestic wells found inland. When San Diego grew westward, this part of the county was developed around imported municipal water from the start. There was never a meaningful inventory of private rock wells here to rehabilitate, which is precisely why a hydrofracturing crew has little to do in the neighborhood today. Recognizing that up front saves homeowners from paying for an assessment of a well that, in most cases, does not exist.

More Likely Causes of Water Problems in University City

For homes on the municipal system, here is what usually lies behind the symptoms people call us about:

  • Low pressure at the fixtures. Often a failing or misadjusted pressure-reducing valve where the city line enters the house, or corroded galvanized supply piping in older homes.
  • Sudden flow drops. Frequently a partially closed shutoff, a clogged aerator or fixture screen, or sediment from a water main repair.
  • Hard water and spotting. San Diego's imported water is naturally hard. A water softener or conditioning system is the right fix, not anything well-related.
  • Taste, odor, or chlorine concerns. A point-of-use or whole-house filtration system addresses these for municipal supplies.

What to Check Before You Call Anyone

  • Confirm whether you are actually on a well or on city water. Your water bill from the City of San Diego is the quickest answer; if you receive one, you are on municipal supply.
  • Test pressure at an outdoor hose bib with an inexpensive gauge. Municipal pressure in the area should generally read in a normal range; a very low reading points to your regulator.
  • Check whether the problem affects the whole house or one fixture. One slow faucet is almost always a local clog, not a supply issue.
  • Look for recent city main work in the neighborhood, which can stir sediment temporarily.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hydrofracturing help my University City home's low water pressure?

Almost certainly not, if you are on city water, which nearly all University City homes are. Low pressure on a municipal supply is a plumbing or pressure-regulator issue. Hydrofracturing only applies to private wells in rock.

How do I know whether I have a well?

If you receive a water bill from the City of San Diego, you are on municipal supply and do not have an active private well. A wellhead, pressure tank, and pump in a utility area would indicate a well.

I have an old well on my property. Is it worth reviving?

Possibly, for irrigation, but it depends on its construction and the geology beneath your lot. We can inspect it and tell you honestly whether rehabilitation or hydrofracturing is realistic.

Is University City good geology for wells?

The area sits on coastal terrace deposits rather than the fractured granite that makes inland wells productive, so it is not strong well country. That is another reason municipal water dominates here.

What should I do about hard water?

Install a water softener or conditioning system sized to your home. This addresses San Diego's naturally hard imported water and has nothing to do with wells.

Do you still serve University City for other needs?

Yes. We are happy to evaluate any genuine well on your property and provide honest guidance, even if the answer is that hydrofracturing is not the right tool for your situation.
