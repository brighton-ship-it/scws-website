How a Pressure Tank Keeps Your Well System Healthy

Inside every pressure tank is a flexible bladder or diaphragm separating stored water from a pocket of compressed air. As the pump delivers water, it compresses that air; when you open a faucet or a valve, the compressed air pushes the water back out. That stored energy means the pump can rest between cycles instead of restarting for every small draw. The air side carries a pre-charge, an air pressure set while the tank is empty, and that figure must be tuned to the pump's controls.

A pressure switch turns the pump on at its cut-in pressure and off at its cut-out, usually 30/50 or 40/60 PSI. The amount of water you can use between those two points is the drawdown, and it is considerably less than the tank's gallon rating. Drawdown is what shields your pump: every start-stop cycle generates heat and wear, so a tank that supplies a generous reserve lets the pump run in long, smooth cycles. On agricultural parcels where demand spikes hard, that protection matters even more, because a failing tank can destroy an expensive pump in a single hot season.

Why Tanks Fail Faster in Thermal

The extreme heat and hard water of the below-sea-level Coachella Valley drive several recurring failures. The ones we encounter most around Thermal include:

  • Waterlogging: When the bladder leaks or loses its charge, water occupies the whole vessel and the air cushion vanishes, leaving the pump with no reserve.
  • Short-cycling: A waterlogged or undersized tank forces the pump to snap on and off rapidly, the single most damaging condition for a pump motor and a top priority to correct.
  • Ruptured bladder: Heat and mineral content fatigue the rubber diaphragm until it tears, after which the tank cannot hold a separate air charge.
  • Lost air charge: Air slowly bleeds out of even a sound tank, and a low pre-charge makes a big tank behave like a tiny one.
  • Fouled air valve: Mineral scale and corrosion clog the Schrader valve, blocking any attempt to check or top off the pre-charge.
  • Corrosion: Searing ambient temperatures and mineral-rich water rust tank shells from both sides, frequently appearing first along the base.

Simple Checks You Can Do First

A few minutes of inspection can tell you a lot. Rap the tank with a wrench from top to bottom: the upper section should ring hollow where the air sits, and the lower section should sound solid where the water is. A solid sound from top to bottom means the tank has waterlogged.

To verify the air pre-charge, shut off power to the pump, open a faucet, and drain the tank fully so the air side is dry. Then press a tire gauge onto the Schrader valve on top. You want a reading about 2 PSI under the cut-in, around 28 PSI on a 30/50 setup or 38 PSI on a 40/60 setup. A reading far below that, or water hissing out of the valve, means the bladder is shot. Take a look at the pressure switch contacts as well, and watch a running faucet: if the flow throbs while the pump clicks on and off in quick succession, you have confirmed short-cycling.

Sizing and Pre-Charge for High-Demand Wells

Correct sizing keeps the pump running a minimum of one to two minutes per cycle. Bigger tanks store more drawdown and cut cycling sharply, which is exactly why agricultural and high-demand properties around Thermal benefit from larger vessels. A modest residence might do fine on a 20 to 32-gallon tank, but a ranch house with irrigation, livestock water, or simultaneous heavy draws is far better served by an 86 or 119-gallon tank, or in some cases multiple tanks plumbed together.

No matter the capacity, the pre-charge has to be set roughly 2 PSI below the cut-in pressure. That match between pre-charge and switch settings is what unlocks the tank's full drawdown. Set it incorrectly and even a brand-new tank will cycle itself, and the pump, to an early grave. We always set and confirm the pre-charge during installation.

When It Is Time to Call a Pro

Reach out to us if your pump runs nonstop, if you have lost water altogether, if the tank is rusted or weeping at a seam, or if you have re-charged the air only to have the short-cycling come right back. That last symptom usually points to a torn bladder or a pump problem rather than a simple air loss. We also strongly recommend professional sizing whenever you replace a tank, because copying the old tank's size can repeat a mistake that was undersizing your system all along, an easy trap on busy ag wells.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do agricultural wells need a different pressure tank than a house?

Often, yes. Ag and high-demand wells around Thermal typically draw more water at once, so they need larger tanks, or multiple tanks, to provide adequate drawdown and protect the pump from constant cycling.

Why does my tank seem to fail every few years?

The intense heat and hard, mineral-rich water of the below-sea-level Coachella Valley are tough on bladders and tank shells. Repeated early failures usually mean the tank was undersized or low-grade, and we can recommend a better-matched replacement.

Is short-cycling really that serious?

It is the most pump-destructive condition there is. A pump that clicks on and off every few seconds overheats and wears out quickly, so short-cycling should be diagnosed right away.

How do I know if my bladder has ruptured?

Drain the tank with the pump off and press the air valve on top. If water comes out instead of air, the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacing.

Can heat alone damage a pressure tank?

Heat accelerates rubber fatigue and condensation-driven corrosion, so yes, the extreme Thermal climate shortens tank life even apart from water chemistry.

Yes. As a full-service, C-57 licensed well company we handle residential and agricultural well systems alike, from the pressure tank to the pump itself.
