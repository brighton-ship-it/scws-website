Sand in Your Montclair Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Montclair well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Montclair well.

Sand and Sediment in Montclair Well Water: A Local Guide

Montclair sits in the southwestern corner of San Bernardino County, tucked into the Pomona Valley right against the Los Angeles County line beside Claremont and Pomona. The city spreads across the gently sloping alluvial plain that fans out below the San Gabriel Mountains, and that location is the single most important clue to understanding why so many local wells eventually start producing sand. The ground beneath Montclair is not solid bedrock but a deep accumulation of loose sand, gravel, and finer sediment washed down over thousands of years by San Antonio Creek and the mountain canyons to the north.

For homeowners and property owners across Montclair and the surrounding Inland Empire communities of Upland, Ontario, Chino, Claremont, Pomona, and Rancho Cucamonga, finding grit at the bottom of a glass, hearing a sputtering faucet, or watching sediment settle in a toilet tank is a common and frustrating experience. The good news is that sandy water is almost always fixable. The key is understanding the local geology, diagnosing the true source, and matching the repair to the problem rather than guessing. This guide walks through exactly how we approach sand and sediment problems in Montclair wells.

Why Montclair Wells Produce Sand

Montclair draws its groundwater from the Chino Basin, one of the largest groundwater basins in Southern California, along with the adjacent Pomona and Six Basins area to the northwest. The Chino Basin is a deep alluvial basin: layer upon layer of sand and gravel deposited as San Antonio Creek and the streams draining the San Gabriel Mountains lost their energy and dropped their sediment load across the valley floor. Up in the foothills to the north the material is coarser, with bouldery fan deposits and fractured rock, but out across the Montclair plain the aquifer is dominated by sand and gravel with zones of finer, easily mobilized sand. Because the water-bearing formation is itself made of sand, a well here is essentially drinking through a straw stuck into a giant sandbox, and anything that disturbs the balance between the screen, the pump, and the surrounding material can let sand into the water.

That alluvial setting makes sand and sediment intrusion a genuine local risk rather than a rare fluke. Several specific causes show up again and again in Montclair wells:

  • A worn or failing pump: An aging submersible pump with worn impellers and bearings loses efficiency and can begin pulling fine sand up with the water, and the abrasive sand then accelerates its own decline.
  • An oversized pump that over-draws the well: A pump rated for more gallons per minute than the aquifer can comfortably give pulls water through the sand too aggressively, dragging fine particles into the well.
  • A failed or corroded well screen: The screen is the filter that keeps formation sand out. After years in mineral-rich groundwater, screens corrode, crack, or develop slots that have widened, and sand pours in.
  • The wrong screen slot size for fine sand: Montclair's alluvium includes very fine sand. A screen with slots sized for coarser gravel will pass that fine material straight through.
  • Excessive drawdown: When the water level inside the well is pulled down too far during pumping, the increased velocity near the screen scours sand loose from the formation.
  • A new or under-developed well: A recently drilled well that was not fully developed will produce sand until the fine material around the screen has been flushed out and stabilized.
  • The naturally sandy aquifer itself: In some parts of the basin the formation is simply so fine and unconsolidated that careful pump sizing and filtration are required from day one.

Identifying which of these is at work is the whole job. A pump set too deep in the sediment column calls for a very different fix than a corroded screen or an oversized motor, and only a proper diagnosis tells them apart.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand in well water is far more than a cosmetic nuisance. Sand is abrasive, and once it is moving through your system it grinds away at everything it touches. The submersible pump suffers first: sand chews through impellers, seals, and bearings, and a pump that might have lasted fifteen years can fail in a fraction of that time. Pressure tanks fill with settled sediment, losing their working volume and short-cycling the pump. Sand scores the seats of valves and faucets so they no longer shut off cleanly, and it collects in the bottom of water heaters where it bakes into a hard crust that wastes energy and shortens the tank's life. Dishwashers, washing machines, irrigation valves, and any appliance with fine internal passages are all vulnerable to clogging and premature wear. Left unaddressed, a sand problem quietly destroys thousands of dollars of equipment, which is why catching it early in a Montclair home pays for itself many times over.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Montclair

Guessing is expensive, so we diagnose before we recommend anything. Our process is methodical:

  • Jar test and sand concentration: We collect samples straight from the wellhead and let the sediment settle so we can see how much sand is present and how fine it is. A trace of fine silt points in a very different direction than a steady stream of coarse grit.
  • Amp draw and pump depth check: We measure the pump's electrical draw and confirm how deep it is set. Abnormal amperage and a pump sitting too low in the sediment column are both common Montclair culprits.
  • Pull and inspect the pump and screen: When the evidence warrants it, we pull the pump and inspect it along with the visible portions of the casing and screen for wear, corrosion, and damage.
  • Downhole camera inspection: A camera lowered into the well lets us actually see cracked screens, casing breaches, and sediment buildup rather than inferring them from the surface.
  • Flow and drawdown testing: By measuring yield against how far the water level drops, we can tell whether the well is being over-pumped and whether excessive drawdown is scouring sand loose.

We charge a $125 diagnostic fee for this evaluation, and we credit that amount toward any repair you choose to have us perform. You get a clear picture of what is actually happening in your well, and you are never paying twice for the same visit.

How We Fix It

Once we know the cause, the repair follows logically. Depending on what the diagnosis reveals, the right solution for a Montclair well is usually one or a combination of the following:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters: These use centrifugal force to spin sand out of the water before it reaches your pressure tank and plumbing, ideal when the well produces a manageable, steady amount of sand.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration: Spin-down filters and staged sediment cartridges polish the water and capture finer particles that a separator alone may miss, protecting appliances and fixtures.
  • Pump sizing and placement: If an oversized or poorly positioned pump is the root cause, we resize it to match the aquifer's true capacity and reset it at the correct depth above the sediment, which alone resolves many sand complaints.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment: For wells that are silting in or were never fully developed, we clean and redevelop the well to flush out fine material and restore stable production.
  • Screen repair or relining: When the screen itself has failed, we repair, reline, or replace it so the formation sand is once again held back where it belongs.

The goal is always to stop sand at its source first and then add filtration as a safeguard, rather than relying on a filter to mask a mechanical problem that will keep getting worse.

Preventing Sand Problems

Because Montclair's aquifer is sandy by nature, prevention matters as much as repair. The most effective habit is to avoid over-pumping: running a pump harder or longer than the well can sustain pulls sand loose, so a correctly sized pump operated within the well's safe yield is your best protection. Scheduling a periodic well inspection, including an occasional downhole camera look, catches screen corrosion and early sediment buildup before they become emergencies. Keeping a sediment filter in good condition and changing cartridges on schedule protects your appliances even when the well is behaving. If you have a newer well, making sure it was properly developed at the start prevents years of nuisance sand. And paying attention to changes, such as a faucet that suddenly spits grit or a pressure tank that needs draining more often, lets you call for help while the fix is still small and inexpensive.

When to Call a Professional

Some sand situations call for a licensed well contractor right away rather than a wait-and-see approach. Call promptly if you see a sudden increase in sand, if your water goes from clear to gritty over a short period, or if sand appears alongside a drop in water pressure or yield, which together suggest a screen or drawdown problem. A pump that is short-cycling, drawing abnormal amperage, or making new noises should be inspected before it fails completely. Visible sediment collecting in toilet tanks, water heaters, and filter housings faster than usual is another clear signal. Pulling a pump and inspecting a screen safely requires the right equipment and training, and a C-57 licensed well drilling contractor can do it correctly without damaging the casing. When in doubt, a diagnostic visit is far cheaper than a destroyed pump.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Montclair well water normal?

A brand-new well may produce some sand for a few weeks until it is fully developed, and that can be normal. For an established well, a sudden or steady appearance of sand is not normal and signals a screen, pump, or drawdown issue that should be diagnosed before it damages equipment.

Why does Montclair groundwater carry sand in the first place?

Montclair sits over the Chino Basin, a deep alluvial basin of sand and gravel deposited by San Antonio Creek and the San Gabriel Mountain canyons. Because the aquifer itself is made of sand, any disturbance to the screen, pump, or pumping rate can let that fine material into the water.

Can a sediment filter alone solve my sand problem?

A filter protects your plumbing and appliances, but it treats the symptom rather than the cause. If sand is entering because of a failing screen, an oversized pump, or excessive drawdown, that underlying problem will keep worsening. We recommend fixing the source first and using filtration as a safeguard.

Will sand ruin my well pump?

Yes, over time. Sand is highly abrasive and grinds down impellers, seals, and bearings, often cutting a pump's lifespan dramatically. Running a pump that is pulling sand is one of the most common ways Montclair homeowners end up with a premature and expensive pump failure.

How do you tell what is causing the sand?

We run a jar test to measure sand concentration, check the pump's amp draw and set depth, perform flow and drawdown testing, and when needed pull the pump and run a downhole camera to inspect the screen and casing. That evidence tells us whether the cause is the pump, the screen, the formation, or the pumping rate.
