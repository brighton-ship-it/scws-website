Well Pump Won't Start in Victorville?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Victorville homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Common Reasons a Well Pump Won't Start in the High Desert

A pump that refuses to turn on is almost always being stopped by one of these problems:

  • Tripped breaker or blown fuse — no power reaching the pump. The single most common cause. Desert summer load on the grid, A/C surges, and aging panels all trip breakers.
  • Failed pressure switch or clogged sensing port. The pressure switch is the brain that tells the pump when to start. Mojave groundwater carries minerals and fine sediment that can plug the small sensing port, leaving the pump electrically alive but never commanded to run.
  • Burned-out capacitor or failed control box. Submersible pumps deep in Victor Valley wells rely on a control box at the surface with a start capacitor and relay. Heat inside an unshaded box on a 105-degree day shortens capacitor life dramatically.
  • Failed or seized motor or pump end. Sand-laden water and years of cycling eventually wear bearings and windings until the motor will not turn.
  • Wiring and corrosion faults. Drop wire splices hundreds of feet down, sun-baked surface conduit, and rodent damage in rural junction boxes all interrupt the circuit.
  • Stuck check valve. A check valve frozen shut can stall a pump or trip overload protection.
  • Waterlogged or air-bound pressure tank. A tank that has lost its air charge causes rapid short-cycling that can trip safety controls and leave the pump apparently "dead."
  • Rural power problems. On Victorville's outskirts and toward the desert fringe, voltage sags, brownouts, and lightning-season surges are real. Low or unstable voltage prevents motors from starting and can damage controls.

Safety-First Steps to Check Before You Call

Warning: well pumps run on 240 volts. That voltage can kill. Never open a control box, pull wires, or touch terminals with the power live. Work through these steps in order:

  1. Turn the pump breaker OFF at the main electrical panel first. This is the single most important safety step before you inspect anything.
  2. With power off, look at the breaker. If it was tripped (sitting between ON and OFF), you may reset it ONCE. Flip it fully off, then on.
  3. If the breaker immediately trips again, stop. Leave it off and call us — repeated tripping points to a short or failing motor and is a fire risk.
  4. Check your pressure tank gauge. A reading stuck at zero, or far above your normal cut-out, tells a story worth reporting to the technician.
  5. Listen near the pressure switch and tank for a faint click when water demand should start the pump. No click often means a switch or control problem.
  6. Look over visible surface wiring and the conduit for obvious burn marks, melted insulation, chew damage, or a scorched smell — all reasons to keep the power off and call.
  7. Confirm other circuits in the home work, which helps separate a whole-property power outage from a pump-specific fault.

Electrical Causes Worth Understanding

Because Victor Valley submersibles sit so deep, the electrical path is long and unforgiving. A two-wire pump has its capacitor and overload built into the motor downhole; a three-wire pump puts those components in the surface control box, which is far easier — and cheaper — to service. When a three-wire pump won't start, a failed start capacitor or relay in that box is one of the most frequent and least expensive fixes. We also see voltage problems specific to outlying Victorville properties: long service drops from the road, undersized wire, and utility brownouts during peak desert demand. A motor that "hums but won't spin" is often a capacitor or low-voltage symptom, not a dead motor. Diagnosing this correctly with a meter — measuring incoming voltage, insulation resistance of the motor windings, and amperage draw — is what separates a $300 repair from an unnecessary $4,000 pump pull. This is precisely the testing we perform on every no-start diagnostic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't my well pump start even though I have power to the house?

House power and pump power are usually on separate breakers. A tripped pump breaker, a failed pressure switch, or a bad capacitor can leave the rest of your Victorville home fully powered while the pump stays silent. Check the dedicated pump breaker first.

Is it safe to reset my pump breaker myself?

You may reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us. Repeated tripping signals a short, a grounded motor, or another fault that can start an electrical fire if forced.

How deep are wells in the Victorville area, and does depth affect repair cost?

Victor Valley wells average around 240 feet but commonly run 400–600 feet on the benches and mesas. Greater depth raises the labor cost of pulling a pump, which is why surface-level repairs like switches and capacitors are far cheaper than a full replacement.

Could desert heat or rural power cause my pump to stop?

Yes. High Desert heat shortens the life of start capacitors in unshaded control boxes, and rural voltage sags or brownouts on Victorville's outskirts can prevent a motor from starting and even damage controls. We test incoming voltage on every no-start call.
