Rusty Water from Your Colton Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Colton well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

Colton sits squarely in the heart of San Bernardino County, where the Rialto-Colton groundwater basin and the larger Bunker Hill basin feed more than a thousand wells through the older alluvial deposits left behind by the Santa Ana River. That alluvium is rich in sand, gravel, and the dissolved minerals that come with mountain runoff from the San Bernardino and San Gabriel ranges. Iron is one of those minerals, and it is the number-one reason Colton well owners call us about rust-colored water.

When iron-bearing groundwater is pumped to the surface and meets oxygen, the dissolved (ferrous) iron oxidizes into the visible (ferric) iron particles that stain everything orange. But dissolved iron is only one of several culprits we diagnose in Colton:

  • Dissolved iron from the aquifer: Water moving through the Santa Ana River alluvium often carries naturally occurring iron, especially in the deeper, confined water-bearing zones of the Bunker Hill basin.
  • Iron bacteria: These harmless-but-ugly organisms feed on iron and produce a slimy, reddish-brown sludge that clogs screens, pressure tanks, and toilet flush valves. Colton's warm summer temperatures speed their growth.
  • Corroding steel casing: Many older Colton-area wells were completed decades ago with steel casing. As that steel rusts from the inside out, flakes of iron oxide travel up the column with your water.
  • Galvanized household plumbing: Homes built in older Colton neighborhoods sometimes still have galvanized supply lines that shed rust independent of the well itself.
  • A failing water heater: If only your hot water is rusty, a corroding anode rod or tank is usually to blame, not the well.

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron is classified as a secondary (aesthetic) contaminant, which means it is not considered a direct health hazard at the concentrations typical of San Bernardino County groundwater. Most people stop drinking it long before that point, though, because rusty water tastes metallic and bitter. The bigger problems iron causes in Colton homes are practical and expensive:

  • Permanent orange staining on sinks, tubs, toilets, and white laundry
  • Reduced flow as iron and iron-bacteria slime narrow your pipes and aerators
  • Premature failure of water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines
  • A telltale rotten-egg odor when iron bacteria and sulfur bacteria grow together

One important caution for Colton: the Rialto-Colton basin has documented contamination plumes from past industrial activity in some areas. If your water suddenly changes color, taste, or smell, do not assume it is "just iron." A comprehensive test rules out the things you cannot see.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

There is no single rusty-water fix, because there is no single cause. After testing, we match the treatment to your specific Colton water chemistry and well construction.

Oxidizing Iron Filters

For moderate dissolved-iron levels, an oxidizing media filter (such as a manganese-greensand or air-injection system) converts ferrous iron to a filterable solid and traps it. These are the workhorses of iron removal for Colton-area wells and require periodic backwashing.

Water Softeners

Colton groundwater tends to be hard thanks to the calcium and magnesium in the alluvium. A softener can remove small amounts of iron along with the hardness, and is often paired with a dedicated iron filter when iron levels are high.

Shock Chlorination and Well Rehabilitation

When iron bacteria are the problem, no filter alone will keep up. We disinfect the well with shock chlorination, scrub the casing and screen, and re-establish clean flow. Recurring cases sometimes call for ongoing chlorine injection.

Casing Repair or Replacement

If a corroded steel casing is shedding rust into your water, we can evaluate whether the casing should be lined, sleeved, or replaced. In some older Colton wells, this is the only permanent cure.

Plumbing Upgrades

When the rust originates inside the house, swapping out galvanized pipe for PEX or copper eliminates a source the best whole-house filter can never fully fix.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Is it all fixtures or just hot water? Hot-only points to the water heater.
  • Is the rust constant or only after the pump kicks on? Intermittent rust often means casing or sediment in the tank.
  • Is there slimy buildup in your toilet tank? That is the signature of iron bacteria.
  • Did it appear suddenly? Sudden changes warrant a full test before any treatment.

How Iron Gets Into Your Colton Water in the First Place

It helps to understand the journey. Rainfall and snowmelt off the San Bernardino Mountains percolate down through the Santa Ana River alluvium that underlies Colton. As that water moves slowly through sand, gravel, and the older Pleistocene deposits of the Bunker Hill basin, it dissolves minerals from the surrounding rock, including iron, which is one of the most abundant elements in the earth's crust. Underground, with little oxygen present, the iron stays dissolved and invisible. Your pump lifts that clear water to the surface, and the moment it contacts air, oxidation begins. Within minutes the iron converts to rust particles, which is why a glass that looks fine at the tap can turn cloudy and orange as it sits. This is also why iron problems are so often underestimated: the water can look acceptable for a few seconds before the staining potential becomes visible.

When to Call a Professional

Some rusty-water situations are urgent and some can wait, but a few clearly warrant a call right away. Reach out to a licensed well contractor if your water suddenly changes color, taste, or smell with no obvious cause; if rust is accompanied by a drop in pressure or sputtering at the tap, which can signal a failing pump or a casing issue; if you see slimy reddish buildup that returns no matter how often you clean; or if staining is so heavy that filters and softeners cannot keep up. In a basin like Rialto-Colton, where contamination plumes exist in places, an unexplained change is always worth a professional test rather than a guess. Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Colton well water suddenly orange?

A sudden change usually means one of three things: a slug of iron-rich water from a deeper zone of the Bunker Hill basin, rust flaking off corroded steel casing, or sediment stirred up in your pressure tank. Because the Rialto-Colton basin has known contamination in places, we recommend a water test before assuming it is harmless iron.

Is iron in my Colton well water dangerous?

Iron is an aesthetic contaminant, not a recognized health hazard at typical San Bernardino County levels. It ruins taste, stains fixtures and laundry, and shortens the life of appliances, which is why most homeowners treat it even though it is not toxic.

How much does iron removal cost in Colton?

An iron or manganese filter or a softener typically runs $1,500 to $3,500 installed, while simpler sediment filtration runs $300 to $900. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward whatever solution you choose.

What is the slimy reddish gunk in my toilet tank?

That is the classic sign of iron bacteria. They are not a health threat, but they clog plumbing and cause odors. Shock chlorination and well cleaning usually clear them, with occasional chlorine injection for stubborn cases.

Can you fix rusty water that comes from old pipes instead of the well?

Yes. If testing shows the rust originates in galvanized household plumbing rather than the aquifer, we can recommend replacing those lines with PEX or copper, which eliminates a source no whole-house filter can fully cure.
