Sputtering Water From Well?

That burping, spitting, sputtering water from your faucets isn't just annoying—it's your well system telling you something's wrong. Air in your water lines can range from a minor nuisance to a sign of serious problems. This guide helps you understand what's causing the sputtering and whether you need to take immediate action.

🔍 When Does Sputtering Occur?

  • • Only first thing in morning? → Dissolved gases (usually harmless)
  • • After heavy water use? → Well water level dropping
  • • Constantly/getting worse? → System leak or pump problem (urgent)
  • • Started suddenly? → New problem—check well cap, pipes, pump

Understanding Air in Well Water Systems

Well water systems are designed to be completely sealed from the pump to your faucets. When air enters, it travels with the water and exits explosively at the point of use—causing that familiar sputtering, spitting, and uneven flow.

How Air Can Enter Your System

🔴 At the Well

  • Water level drops below pump intake
  • Pump sucking air along with water
  • Well cap not properly sealed
  • Pitless adapter leaking

🟠 In the Pipes

  • Cracked or damaged drop pipe
  • Failing check valve
  • Loose fittings allowing air entry
  • Leak in supply line from well

🟡 From the Water Itself

  • Dissolved gases coming out of solution
  • Methane in certain aquifers
  • Hydrogen sulfide gas
  • Natural groundwater gases

🔵 Pump Related

  • Worn pump drawing air
  • Pump cycling issues
  • Jet pump losing prime
  • Impeller cavitation

Common Causes & Solutions

1. Low Water Level in the Well

The most common cause of sputtering. When your well's water level drops near or below the pump intake, the pump draws air along with water.

Signs It's a Low Water Level

  • Sputtering occurs after heavy water use (showers, laundry, irrigation)
  • Problem is worse during dry seasons or drought
  • Water runs clear after you let the well rest
  • Pressure drops before sputtering starts

✅ Solutions for Low Water Level

  • • Immediate: Reduce water usage; stagger heavy use activities
  • • Short-term: Lower pump deeper in the well (if depth allows)
  • • Long-term: Add storage tank, deepen well, or drill new well

See our complete guide on wells that run out of water daily for detailed solutions.

2. Leaking Well Cap or Seal

Air can be drawn into the well through a damaged or poorly sealed well cap. This creates a continuous air leak.

Signs of Cap/Seal Leak

  • Constant low-level sputtering
  • Visible damage to well cap
  • Insects or debris found in well
  • Water contamination issues

The Fix

  • Inspect well cap and gasket
  • Replace damaged cap ($50-$150)
  • Check conduit/wire entry seals
  • Inspect pitless adapter if present

3. Failing Check Valve

Check valves prevent water from draining back into the well when the pump stops. A failing check valve can allow air to enter the system.

How Check Valve Failure Causes Sputtering

  • Water drains back when pump stops, creating air pockets
  • Pump must re-prime on each cycle, pushing air through system
  • Sputtering often occurs at the start of pump cycles

Solution: Replace check valve ($150-$400 installed)

4. Cracked or Damaged Drop Pipe

The drop pipe connects your pump to the surface. Cracks or holes can let air enter at the water line or allow air to be drawn in during pump operation.

⚠️ Signs of Drop Pipe Problems

  • • Sputtering that's getting progressively worse
  • • Pump runs but produces less water than before
  • • Visible water at well head (above-ground leak)
  • • Well uses old galvanized or steel pipe (corrosion prone)

Fix: Requires pulling the pump to inspect/replace pipe ($800-$2,000+)

5. Failing Pump

A worn or failing pump may not seal properly internally, drawing air through worn seals or cavitating.

Signs Your Pump Is Failing

  • Sputtering combined with declining water pressure
  • Pump runs longer than usual to build pressure
  • Strange noises from the well or pump
  • Pump is 10+ years old (submersible) or 8+ years (jet)
  • Electrical issues (breaker tripping, high amp draw)

Learn about pump replacement costs and other pump failure signs.

6. Jet Pump Losing Prime

Jet pumps (above-ground pumps for shallow wells) require a continuous column of water to operate. If they lose prime, they pump air.

Causes of Lost Prime

  • Air leak in suction line
  • Foot valve not holding (at bottom of suction pipe)
  • Cracked pump housing
  • Water table dropped below suction limit

Solution: Check for air leaks, replace foot valve, or convert to submersible pump

7. Dissolved Gases in Groundwater

Some aquifers contain dissolved gases that come out of solution when pressure changes—like opening a soda bottle.

💨 Common Dissolved Gases

  • • Air: Most common, harmless sputtering especially in morning
  • • Hydrogen sulfide: Rotten egg smell, indicates sulfur bacteria
  • • Methane: Flammable, requires testing and venting (rare but serious)
  • • Carbon dioxide: Can make water slightly acidic

If gas-related sputtering is your issue, an air charger tank or aeration system can remove dissolved gases before water enters your home.

Diagnosing Your Sputtering Problem

Work through this diagnostic process:

Step 1: Note the Pattern

  • Time of day: Morning only, or anytime?
  • After water use: Following heavy demand, or random?
  • Duration: Clears quickly, or continues?
  • Progression: Getting worse, or consistent?
  • All faucets: Whole house, or specific locations?

Step 2: Check the Pressure Tank

  • Is the pump short cycling (rapid on-off-on-off)?
  • Check tank air pressure at the air valve
  • Tap the tank—does it sound hollow at top, solid at bottom?
  • Waterlogged tanks contribute to cycling issues that can cause sputtering

Step 3: Inspect the Well Cap

  • Is the cap securely in place?
  • Look for cracks, damage, or missing gaskets
  • Check conduit entries for proper sealing
  • Inspect for signs of water entry (staining, debris)

Step 4: Monitor the Well

  • If possible, check static water level
  • Note if sputtering correlates with pump run time
  • Does the well recover after a rest period?

How to Purge Air From Your System

If you have air trapped in the lines, here's how to purge it:

🔧 Air Purging Procedure

  1. Turn off the well pump at the breaker
  2. Open the faucet nearest to the pressure tank (usually a basement or utility area)
  3. Let it drain until flow stops
  4. Starting at the lowest floor, open all cold water faucets
  5. Flush toilets to empty tanks
  6. Turn pump back on
  7. Wait for steady water flow at all faucets (air will sputter out)
  8. Close faucets one by one starting from furthest from tank

Note: If air immediately returns, there's an ongoing leak or source—purging won't fix the root cause.

When to Call a Professional

🚨 Call Now If:

  • Sputtering is constant and worsening
  • You smell gas or rotten eggs
  • Pump is making unusual noises
  • Water pressure is declining
  • You see water around the well head

📅 Schedule Service If:

  • Occasional sputtering after heavy use
  • Problem has persisted more than a few days
  • DIY inspection didn't reveal cause
  • You want to prevent future issues

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well water sputtering and spitting air?

Sputtering well water indicates air is entering your water system. Common causes include: water level dropped below the pump intake, failing pump that's sucking air, crack or leak in the drop pipe, air leak at the well cap or pitless adapter, pressure tank problems, or dissolved gases being released. The cause determines the urgency and solution needed.

Is sputtering water from my well an emergency?

It depends on the cause. If sputtering occurs occasionally after heavy water use, it may just be a low-yield well temporarily depleting. If it's constant or getting worse, it could indicate a failing pump or dropping water table—issues that need prompt attention. Continuous air intake can damage pumps and should be addressed quickly.

Can a bad pressure tank cause sputtering water?

Yes, a waterlogged pressure tank can contribute to sputtering. Without the proper air cushion, the pump cycles rapidly (short cycling), which can cause inconsistent water flow and introduce air into the system. However, pressure tank issues alone don't typically cause significant air in the water—look for other causes too.

Why does my well water only sputter in the morning?

Morning sputtering that clears quickly is often caused by dissolved gases in the water that accumulate overnight when the system is static. These gases come out of solution when pressure changes at the faucet. This is usually harmless. However, if it's getting worse or accompanied by other symptoms, have the well checked.

How do I get air out of my well water lines?

To purge air from your system: Turn off the pump, open the faucet closest to the pressure tank and let it drain, then open faucets throughout the house starting from the lowest point. Once water flows steadily everywhere, close faucets and turn the pump back on. If air returns, there's an ongoing leak or other issue causing air entry.

How much does it cost to fix sputtering well water?

Costs vary greatly depending on the cause: Sealing a well cap air leak costs $50-$200. Replacing a failed check valve is $150-$400. Lowering a pump in a low-water well costs $500-$1,500. Replacing a failing submersible pump runs $1,500-$3,500. A professional diagnosis ($75-$150 service call) identifies the most cost-effective fix.
