Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Liberty Station well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Liberty Station well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Well Rehabilitation

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Water at Liberty Station: What It Means and How to Fix It

Liberty Station is the vibrant, master-planned community built on the grounds of the former Naval Training Center in Point Loma, in the City of San Diego. With its restored barracks turned shops and galleries, the public market, the waterfront promenade along the boat channel, and a mix of newer townhomes and condos, it is one of San Diego's most distinctive neighborhoods. Every home and business here is served by City of San Diego municipal water — there are no private wells. So when a tap suddenly runs orange at Liberty Station, the cause is almost never a well. It is far more often corroding plumbing, a failing water heater, or a routine water-main flush.

San Diego County does still have private wells, but they are out in the rural East County — places like Lakeside, Alpine, and Jamul, where homes sit on decomposed-granite terrain off the municipal grid. This guide therefore covers both: the galvanized-pipe and water-heater corrosion plus municipal-main events that explain virtually all rusty-water complaints at Liberty Station and the surrounding Point Loma peninsula, and the iron, manganese, and well-casing issues that affect private wells inland.

Why Your Liberty Station Water Turns Rusty

Discolored water has two basic origins: corrosion inside your own plumbing after the water enters the building, or — not applicable here on city water — minerals and bacteria rising from a private well. For Liberty Station and Point Loma homes, the cause is in the pipes, the heater, or the city main.

Water-Heater Corrosion

Every tank water heater contains a sacrificial anode rod that corrodes in place of the steel tank. Once the rod is spent — typically after six to ten years — the tank itself begins to rust, and your hot water turns brown. This is one of the most common rusty-water sources in newer construction, because the building's supply piping is still in good shape while the water heater quietly ages. The giveaway: if only the hot water is discolored and the cold runs clear, the heater is your culprit.

Galvanized-Pipe Corrosion (Older Point Loma Homes)

The Liberty Station buildings themselves are relatively new, but the broader Point Loma peninsula has many mid-century homes still carrying galvanized-steel supply lines. The protective zinc coating wears away over decades and the steel rusts from the inside out, producing brown water at the first morning draw that clears after running a minute or two, often alongside slowly declining water pressure. Salt air near the channel and the bay can nudge that corrosion along on exposed fittings.

Municipal Main Flushing and Repairs

The City of San Diego periodically flushes its distribution mains and occasionally repairs breaks. Either event stirs up iron oxide and sediment that had settled in the pipes, and a whole block can get temporarily rusty water that clears within hours. If your neighbors report the same thing at the same time, the city main is the cause — not your building's plumbing.

Iron, Manganese, and Casing Issues (Inland Wells)

For households on private wells in rural East County, the picture is different. Groundwater moving through fractured, decomposed granite picks up dissolved iron and manganese that stay invisible underground but oxidize into rusty particles the moment they meet air. Aging steel casings corrode and shed rust directly into the water, and iron bacteria can colonize idle wells, leaving a reddish slime. These are problems a licensed C-57 well contractor diagnoses and resolves.

Is Rusty Water Safe to Use?

Iron and manganese are classified by the EPA as secondary, aesthetic contaminants — not acute health hazards. The secondary standards of 0.3 mg/L for iron and 0.05 mg/L for manganese exist to limit taste, color, and staining, so a little rust will not make a healthy adult sick. It is still far from harmless to your home:

  • It stains sinks, tubs, toilets, and white laundry a stubborn orange
  • It tastes metallic and ruins coffee, tea, and ice
  • Rust flakes clog aerators, fixtures, and appliance inlet valves
  • Persistent discoloration can signal a water heater or pipes worth replacing

Common Liberty Station Scenarios

A few patterns turn up again and again on the peninsula:

  • The hot-water-only complaint: Cold is clear, hot is rusty. The water heater anode rod is spent and the tank is corroding — the leading cause in newer Liberty Station homes.
  • The morning rust that clears: Brown at the first draw, then clear. Usually galvanized-pipe corrosion in an older Point Loma house nearby.
  • The whole-block event: You and neighbors all get cloudy or rusty water at once — a city main flush or nearby repair.
  • The inland well: A rural East County property sees gradually worsening orange staining — rising dissolved iron or a corroding casing, calling for a water test.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of investigation reveals a great deal about where the rust is coming from.

  1. Cold vs. hot: Run the cold tap by itself. If cold is clear and only hot is rusty, the water heater is the source — not your supply line.
  2. First draw vs. sustained flow: Rust that appears immediately and clears as the water runs points to plumbing settling overnight; rust that persists or worsens points upstream.
  3. One fixture or whole unit: A single rusty faucet is a local issue at that fixture; every tap means the source is the main line or water heater.
  4. Ask your neighbors: If the whole block is affected, it is the city main and usually clears on its own within a few hours.
  5. Catch a sample: Fill a clear glass and let it stand. Particles that settle are oxidized iron or sediment; a faint clear-orange that does not settle is dissolved iron; reddish slime indicates iron bacteria in a well.

How the Problem Gets Fixed

The right fix depends entirely on the source, which is why we diagnose before we recommend.

Water-Heater Service or Replacement

Draining and flushing the tank plus installing a fresh anode rod can add years of life; a tank already rusting through needs replacement. Keeping a new anode rod in any replacement heater prevents a repeat — the single most common fix at Liberty Station.

Repiping and Plumbing Repairs

When corroded galvanized lines in an older peninsula home are the cause, replacing them with PEX or copper is the permanent solution and often restores lost water pressure at the same time. Replacing just the worst runs can be a cost-effective interim step.

Sediment and Whole-House Filtration

A whole-house or point-of-entry sediment filter catches rust flakes from aging pipes or post-flush municipal sediment before they reach your fixtures — an inexpensive, high-value upgrade.

Iron and Manganese Treatment (Wells)

For inland private wells carrying dissolved iron or manganese, an oxidizing iron filter — sometimes paired with a softener — removes the metals at the point of entry. Iron bacteria call for shock chlorination and a system flush.

Casing Repair (Wells)

A badly corroded steel casing can be lined or, in severe cases, the well replaced — always after a downhole evaluation to confirm it is necessary.

When to Call a Professional

You can flush a fixture, drain a water heater, or wait out a municipal main flush on your own. Call a licensed contractor when rusty water appears at every tap and will not clear, when it arrives with falling water pressure, when you are on a private well and notice worsening staining, sulfur odor, or bacterial slime, or when discolored hot water flags a failing heater. Repiping and well work both demand the right tools and proper licensing.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Liberty Station home is newer and on city water — why is it rusty?

In newer construction, the most common rusty-water source is the water heater. Its sacrificial anode rod eventually dissolves and the steel tank begins to corrode, discoloring your hot water even while the building's supply pipes are still in good shape. A city main flush can also cause brief, building-wide discoloration.

Why is only my hot water rusty?

When the cold runs clear and only the hot is discolored, the source is your water heater. The sacrificial anode rod has dissolved and the steel tank is corroding. Draining and flushing the tank and replacing the anode rod — or the heater if it is old — resolves it.

The whole block has rusty water today. What happened?

That is the signature of a City of San Diego main flush or a nearby water-main repair. Sediment that had settled in the pipes gets stirred up and clears on its own, usually within a few hours. Run a cold tap for a few minutes to help flush your service line.

I have an older Point Loma home with brown morning water — what is it?

Brown water at the first draw that clears after a minute is the classic sign of corroding galvanized-steel pipes settling overnight. The permanent fix is replacing those lines with PEX or copper, which usually improves water pressure as well.

I have a private well in East County — what causes the rust?

Wells in the region's decomposed granite often carry dissolved iron and manganese that turn rusty when they meet air, and aging steel casings can shed rust directly into the water. Idle wells may also grow iron bacteria. A water test identifies which, and treatment ranges from an iron filter to shock chlorination.

How much does it cost to fix rusty water?

It depends entirely on the source, which is why we start with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the work. A water-heater anode rod or flush is the least expensive fix, sediment filtration runs a few hundred dollars, and a full repipe or well treatment costs more — but you only pay for what the problem actually requires.
