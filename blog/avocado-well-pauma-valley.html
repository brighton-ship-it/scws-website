Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Pauma Valley well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance

Pauma Valley: Citrus and Avocado Under Palomar Mountain

Pauma Valley spreads across the floor of the San Luis Rey River drainage beneath the south face of Palomar Mountain, in the heart of San Diego County's inland agriculture. The valley and its neighbors — Pala, Rincon, and Valley Center over the ridge — have grown citrus and avocados for generations, taking advantage of deep valley soils, a warm growing season, and water drawn largely from private and agricultural wells. When you farm here, your well is your lifeline.

The hydrogeology of Pauma Valley is a tale of two settings. The valley floor holds alluvial deposits laid down by the San Luis Rey and its tributaries — sand and gravel that can yield strong agricultural wells — while the surrounding foothills are decomposed granite and fractured bedrock with more variable output. That contrast shapes everything about how a grove well is designed and maintained, and it's the first thing we evaluate when we arrive.

How Avocado and Citrus Irrigation Wells Work

Tree crops in Pauma Valley are season-long, high-volume water users. A mature avocado can use 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and citrus has its own steady demand; an irrigated acre commonly needs four to six acre-feet a year. The well and pump must deliver sustained output for long irrigation sets through the dry months.

Tapping the Valley Aquifer

On the valley floor, a well screened into productive alluvium can support large irrigation sets directly, which is why some of the strongest grove wells in north San Diego County are found in valleys like this. We size the submersible to the well's sustained yield and design irrigation blocks that the formation can serve without excessive drawdown.

Storage and Constant Pressure

For foothill groves on granite wells, or to buffer peak demand on the valley floor, storage tanks paired with constant-pressure boosters keep irrigation uniform while protecting the well. The well fills storage at its natural rate; the booster delivers full pressure when blocks call for water.

Common Pauma Valley Grove Water Problems

Nitrate and Salinity in Ag Areas

Long-farmed valleys can see nitrate and salt accumulation in shallower groundwater, and avocados in particular are highly chloride-sensitive. We test well water for chloride, TDS, nitrate and related parameters, then recommend blending, leaching, or treatment so water quality supports the trees rather than stressing them.

Seasonal Water Table Decline

Valley aquifers rise and fall with rainfall and pumping. After dry winters, pumping levels drop, and a pump set too shallow can lose prime or draw air. Measuring water levels lets us set pumps correctly and warn growers before a marginal season becomes an emergency.

Sediment and Emitter Clogging

Both alluvial sand and granite mineral content clog drip and micro-sprinkler systems. Wellhead sediment filtration plus periodic flushing maintains irrigation uniformity across every block.

Irrigation Scheduling Through the Pauma Valley Season

A well is only half of a healthy grove; how you run it through the year matters just as much. Pauma Valley's warm, dry summers push avocado and citrus water demand to its peak from roughly June through September, exactly when the valley water table is at its lowest. Spreading that demand wisely keeps both the trees and the well in good shape. We help growers match irrigation sets to the well's sustainable output rather than its instantaneous maximum — running longer, well-spaced sets that let the formation recover between cycles instead of pulling it down hard every day.

Soil moisture monitoring is one of the highest-return practices we recommend. A few inexpensive sensors in representative blocks tell you when the root zone actually needs water, which often reveals that a grove is being over-irrigated in spring and under-irrigated during the late-summer peak. Correcting that not only protects the crop but reduces unnecessary draw on the aquifer and the energy cost of pumping.

We also encourage growers to plan maintenance around the calendar. The ideal time to pull and inspect a pump, service a pressure tank, or rehabilitate a fouled well is late winter or early spring, before peak demand — not during a July heat wave when downtime threatens the crop. A short off-season inspection frequently catches a wearing pump or a declining water level in time to plan a fix rather than scramble through an emergency.

What to Check Before You Call

Verify the pump breaker and disconnect, check the pressure tank's air charge, listen for short-cycling, and inspect the lines and valves for leaks. If pressure only drops during extended irrigation sets, suspect well yield or pump wear rather than the controls.

Water Testing: What We Actually Measure

For an avocado or citrus grower in Pauma Valley, a standard drinking-water test misses the parameters that matter most to the trees. We test grove well water for the things that actually drive crop health: chloride, which avocados tolerate in only small amounts; total dissolved solids and overall salinity, which affect water uptake; sodium and the sodium adsorption ratio, which influence soil structure over time; nitrate, which can be elevated in long-farmed valley groundwater; and hardness and iron, which scale hardware and clog emitters. Knowing these numbers turns guesswork into a management plan.

Once we know what is in the water, the path forward is usually clear. Elevated chloride or salts may call for blending with a lower-salt source, scheduling periodic leaching irrigations to push salts below the root zone, or selecting salt-tolerant rootstock on replants. High iron or hardness points toward filtration and conditioning to protect both the irrigation system and the pump. Regular retesting then tracks whether the situation is stable or trending, so a Pauma Valley grower can act before the trees show stress rather than after.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Pauma Valley wells better on the valley floor or the hillsides?

Valley-floor wells screened into the San Luis Rey alluvium often produce strong, steady yields suitable for large irrigation sets, while foothill wells in decomposed granite are more variable. The right design depends on which formation your well taps, which we determine on site.

Should I test for nitrate as well as salt?

Yes. Long-farmed valleys like Pauma can carry elevated nitrate in shallower groundwater, and avocados are also very chloride-sensitive. We test for nitrate, chloride, and TDS together so you know exactly what your irrigation water is delivering to the trees.

Why did my well lose prime this summer?

After a dry winter, valley water tables decline, and a pump set too shallow can draw air and lose prime as the pumping level drops below it. We measure your water levels and reset or lower the pump appropriately so it stays submerged through the season.

Can a foothill well above Pauma Valley be improved?

Often, yes. Foothill granite wells can be hydrofractured to open existing fractures and raise yield, typically for $3,000 to $8,000, or rehabilitated to clear fouled perforations. We evaluate which approach fits your specific well before recommending it.

What does a replacement pump cost for a valley grove well?

A replacement submersible for a Pauma Valley grove well generally runs $2,500 to $5,500, depending on depth and horsepower. Because valley wells can be deep, we confirm the failure with a diagnostic first so you aren't replacing a pump when a cheaper component is the culprit.
