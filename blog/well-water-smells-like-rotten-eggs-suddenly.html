Understanding & Eliminating Sulfur Smell

💧
👃

SC By SCWS Team | February 4, 2026 | 12 min read

Well Water Smells Like Rotten Eggs Suddenly?

Few things are more alarming than turning on your faucet and smelling rotten eggs. If your well water has suddenly developed this sulfur smell, you're dealing with hydrogen sulfide gas—and you want it gone. This guide explains why it happens, whether it's dangerous, and the most effective ways to eliminate it permanently.

🔍 Quick Smell Test: Where Is It Coming From?

  • • Hot water only? → Water heater issue (most common)
  • • Cold water only at first, then goes away? → Sulfur bacteria in pipes
  • • All water, always? → Well or aquifer source
  • • Only certain faucets? → Localized bacteria growth in drains or pipes

What Causes the Rotten Egg Smell?

The rotten egg odor is hydrogen sulfide gas (H₂S). Even tiny amounts—as low as 0.01 parts per million (ppm)—produce that distinctive sulfur smell. There are three main sources in well water systems:

1. Sulfur-Reducing Bacteria (SRB)

These naturally occurring bacteria feed on sulfur and produce hydrogen sulfide as a byproduct. They thrive in oxygen-poor environments like:

  • Deep wells with low oxygen
  • Water heater tanks
  • Stagnant sections of plumbing
  • Pressure tanks and holding tanks

Why sudden onset? Bacteria populations can explode after well work, during warm weather, after extended non-use, or when something changes in the well that provides nutrients (decaying organic matter, iron bacteria die-off).

2. Water Heater Chemical Reaction

Most water heaters contain a magnesium anode rod that protects the tank from corrosion. When water contains sulfates, the magnesium can react to create hydrogen sulfide:

The Chemistry: Magnesium + Sulfates + Heat → Hydrogen Sulfide

This explains why many people notice the smell only in hot water.

3. Naturally Occurring Hydrogen Sulfide

Some aquifers naturally contain hydrogen sulfide from:

  • Decaying organic matter in underground formations
  • Sulfur mineral deposits
  • Oil and gas deposits (even small, unmined ones)
  • Sulfate-rich groundwater meeting reducing conditions

If your aquifer is the source, the smell will have always been present (though may vary seasonally), not appear suddenly.

Is Sulfur-Smelling Water Dangerous?

Let's address the safety concerns:

✅ Low Levels (Under 1 ppm)

Generally not a health concern. Unpleasant but safe for bathing, cleaning, and most uses. Drinking is technically safe but unpleasant.

⚠️ Moderate Levels (1-5 ppm)

May cause nausea, headaches, or irritation. Not recommended for drinking. Can corrode pipes and fixtures. Treatment advised.

🚨 High Levels (Over 5 ppm)

Can cause serious health effects. Rare in domestic wells but possible. Requires immediate treatment. Don't use until resolved.

🔬 Get It Tested

Without testing, you can't know the concentration. Professional water testing costs $50-$200 and tells you exactly what you're dealing with.

Other Concerns with Sulfur Water

  • Corrosion: H₂S corrodes copper, steel, and brass—damaging pipes, fixtures, and appliances
  • Tarnishing: Silver jewelry and silverware will tarnish rapidly
  • Staining: Can cause yellow or black stains on fixtures and laundry
  • Associated bacteria: Where sulfur bacteria live, other bacteria may too—test for coliform

Diagnosing the Source

Before you can fix the problem, you need to identify where the hydrogen sulfide is coming from:

The "Hot vs. Cold" Test

  1. Run COLD water only for 2-3 minutes at a faucet near the pressure tank
    • Smell present → Problem is in well, pressure tank, or cold water pipes
    • No smell → Cold water system is likely fine
  2. Run HOT water for 2-3 minutes
    • Smell only in hot water → Water heater is the source
    • Smell in both → Source is before the water heater (well or incoming lines)

The "First Draw" Test

  1. Don't use water overnight
  2. In the morning, smell the first water from the faucet
  3. Let it run 5 minutes and smell again

Results interpretation:

  • Strong smell at first, then fades → Bacteria in plumbing/pipes
  • Consistent smell throughout → Source is the well or aquifer

Check Multiple Locations

Test at different faucets throughout your home:

  • Only one faucet: Bacteria in that drain or pipe section
  • Only bathrooms: Could be drain bacteria, not water
  • All faucets: System-wide issue (well, treatment, or water heater)
  • Outdoor hose bib: Tests raw well water before entering house

Treatment Solutions by Source

If the Problem Is Your Water Heater

Hot water-only sulfur smell is usually the easiest to fix:

Option 1: Replace the Anode Rod

Swap the magnesium anode for aluminum or zinc/aluminum combination rod.

  • Cost: $150-$300 (including labor)
  • Effectiveness: Excellent for most cases
  • Note: Reducing corrosion protection—monitor tank condition

Option 2: Sanitize the Water Heater

Flush and disinfect with hydrogen peroxide or chlorine bleach.

  • Cost: $100-$200 (DIY or professional)
  • Effectiveness: Good, but may need repeating
  • Process: Drain, fill with sanitizer solution, heat, flush

Option 3: Increase Water Temperature

Setting temperature to 140°F kills most sulfur bacteria.

  • Cost: Free
  • Caution: Scald risk—install mixing valve at taps
  • Energy cost: Higher bills from hotter water

If the Problem Is Your Well

Bacteria in the well require more aggressive treatment:

Shock Chlorination

Flooding the well with high-concentration chlorine kills bacteria colonies.

  • Cost: $150-$400 (professional)
  • Effectiveness: Very good for bacteria-caused odor
  • Downtime: 24-48 hours (no water use during treatment)
  • May need repeating if bacteria return

Learn more about how shock chlorination works.

Well Rehabilitation

For persistent bacteria, well cleaning and rehabilitation may be needed.

  • Cost: $1,000-$3,000
  • Includes: Mechanical cleaning, chemical treatment, development
  • Best for: Older wells with accumulated buildup

If the Problem Is Your Aquifer

When hydrogen sulfide comes from the source water itself, you need ongoing treatment:

Treatment System How It Works Best For Cost Range
Aeration System Exposes water to air, releasing H₂S gas Low-moderate H₂S levels $1,500-$4,000
Chlorine Injection Oxidizes H₂S, kills bacteria Bacteria + H₂S combined $1,200-$2,500
Hydrogen Peroxide Injection Oxidizes H₂S without chlorine taste Those who dislike chlorine $1,500-$3,000
Catalytic Carbon Filter Special carbon media oxidizes and captures H₂S Low levels, chemical-free $800-$2,000
Manganese Greensand Oxidizing filter media removes H₂S + iron Combined sulfur + iron issues $1,500-$3,000
Air-Over-Media (AIO) Combination aeration + filtration Moderate H₂S + iron $1,800-$3,500

Choosing the Right Treatment

The best treatment depends on your specific situation:

💡 Decision Factors

  • • H₂S concentration: Higher levels need more aggressive treatment
  • • Other water issues: Iron, hardness, or bacteria may require combined solutions
  • • Water usage: Larger households need appropriately sized systems
  • • Maintenance preference: Chemical injection requires regular chemical refills
  • • Budget: Balance upfront cost vs. ongoing operational costs

Why Did This Happen Suddenly?

If your water never smelled before, something triggered the change:

🔧 Recent Well Work

Pump repairs, deepening, or any work can introduce bacteria or disturb existing colonies

🌡️ Seasonal Changes

Warmer groundwater temperatures accelerate bacterial growth; summer odors are common

🏚️ Extended Non-Use

Vacation homes or periods of low use let stagnant water breed bacteria

💧 Water Table Changes

Drought or heavy rainfall can change which aquifer zones supply your well

🏗️ Nearby Activity

Construction, drilling, or agricultural activity can affect groundwater chemistry

🔌 New Water Heater

New heater with fresh magnesium anode + existing sulfates = sudden smell

DIY vs. Professional Treatment

✅ DIY-Friendly

  • • Replacing water heater anode rod
  • • Flushing and sanitizing water heater
  • • Installing small point-of-use carbon filters
  • • Basic shock chlorination (if experienced)

❌ Call a Professional

  • • Well shock chlorination (proper dosing critical)
  • • Whole-house treatment system selection & installation
  • • Chemical injection system setup
  • • Persistent odor despite DIY attempts
  • • Combined issues (iron, bacteria, hardness + sulfur)

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my well water suddenly smell like rotten eggs?

A sudden rotten egg smell in well water is usually caused by hydrogen sulfide gas (H₂S) from sulfur bacteria that have colonized your well, or from naturally occurring sulfur compounds in the aquifer. Sudden onset often indicates bacteria growth in the well, water heater, or plumbing—especially if the smell developed after the well sat unused, after a repair, or during warm weather.

Is water that smells like rotten eggs safe to drink?

At low concentrations typical of most wells (under 1 ppm), hydrogen sulfide is not considered a health hazard, though it's extremely unpleasant. However, high concentrations (rare in wells) can cause nausea and, at very high levels, be dangerous. More importantly, the sulfur smell may indicate bacteria growth that could include harmful bacteria—testing is recommended before drinking.

How do I get rid of rotten egg smell in my well water?

Treatment depends on the source: If caused by bacteria in the well, shock chlorination often eliminates it. If it's in your water heater only, replacing the anode rod or treating the heater helps. For ongoing sulfur from the aquifer, treatment options include aeration systems, chlorine injection, hydrogen peroxide injection, or activated carbon filters. A proper diagnosis determines the best solution.

Why does only my hot water smell like rotten eggs?

If only hot water smells, your water heater is the culprit—not your well. Sulfur bacteria thrive in warm environments and can colonize the water heater tank. Additionally, the magnesium anode rod in water heaters can react with sulfates in the water to create hydrogen sulfide. The fix is usually replacing the anode rod with an aluminum or zinc model and/or sanitizing the heater.

Will a water softener remove rotten egg smell?

Standard water softeners do not remove hydrogen sulfide smell. In fact, the resin bed can become contaminated with sulfur bacteria, making the smell worse. Hydrogen sulfide requires oxidation (chlorine, peroxide, aeration) or special media (manganese greensand, catalytic carbon) for removal. Some softener systems include these features, but basic softeners won't help.

How much does it cost to fix rotten egg smell in well water?

Costs vary by treatment: Shock chlorination costs $150-$400. Water heater anode rod replacement is $150-$300. Whole-house treatment systems range from $1,500-$4,000 for aeration systems, $1,200-$2,500 for chlorine/peroxide injection systems, and $800-$2,000 for catalytic carbon filters. Professional water testing costs $50-$200 and is recommended to determine the best solution.
