What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your French Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Wells in French Valley: What Works and What Doesn't

French Valley sits in southwestern Riverside County, spread across the broad basin between Murrieta, Temecula, and Winchester, with the French Valley Airport near its center. Over the past two decades the area has shifted quickly from ranchland and citrus to dense suburban subdivisions, but a meaningful number of properties along the valley margins and on the larger rural lots still draw their water from private wells. If your French Valley well has been producing less water each year, hydrofracturing may help—but whether it helps depends almost entirely on what your well is actually finished in, and French Valley is one of the trickier places in the county to answer that question honestly.

The reason is the local geology. The floor of French Valley is an alluvial basin: layers of sand, gravel, silt, and clay washed down over time from the surrounding highlands. Around the edges of the valley and up into the granitic uplands toward the south and east, the picture changes to hard, fractured crystalline rock. Hydrofracturing is a fracture-rock technique. It works beautifully in bedrock and decomposed granite, and it does essentially nothing for a well screened in loose valley alluvium. So before you spend a dollar, the first job is figuring out which French Valley you live in.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing (often spelled hydrofracking, though it has nothing to do with oil and gas drilling) is a well-rehabilitation method that uses nothing but clean water. We lower a set of inflatable rubber packers into the well casing and inflate them to seal off a target zone. Then we pump clean water into that isolated section at high pressure—typically somewhere in the 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI range. That pressure pushes into the existing hairline fractures in the surrounding rock, flushes out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have been choking them, and nudges those fractures open wider so groundwater can move into the borehole more freely. A typical job runs four to eight hours. No chemicals, no additives—just water and pressure.

The key word is rock. The whole technique depends on having a rock formation that holds and transmits water through fractures. When a borehole is sitting in unconsolidated sand and gravel, there are no fractures to open; high pressure just pushes water out into the loose material and comes back to zero. That is exactly why French Valley deserves a careful look rather than a sales pitch.

Bedrock Well or Alluvial Well? How to Tell

Here is the honest framing every French Valley homeowner should hear. Hydrofracturing is a strong candidate for wells finished in the fractured granitic bedrock around the valley edges and in the uplands. It is generally not a good candidate for a classic loose-alluvium well down on the valley floor. The good news is you can usually find out which one you have without guessing:

  • Pull the driller's log (well completion report). California well drillers file a report with the state, and many homeowners have a copy in their purchase paperwork. It describes what the drill passed through. Words like "granite," "decomposed granite," "fractured rock," or "bedrock" point toward a good hydrofracturing candidate. Words like "sand," "gravel," "clay," and "boulders in clay" with a continuous screen point toward an alluvial well.
  • Ask how the well is cased. A bedrock well is typically cased through the soil overburden and then left as open hole in solid rock below. An alluvial well usually has perforated casing or a screen across the water-bearing sand-and-gravel zones. Open hole in rock = good candidate; screened in sand = poor candidate.
  • Note the depth and how the water behaves. Shallower wells (often under a couple hundred feet) on the valley floor near the airport are more likely to be in alluvium. Deeper wells along the granitic margins that recover slowly and produce a few gallons per minute are the classic fractured-rock wells that respond to treatment.
  • When in doubt, let us run a video camera. A downhole camera survey shows the casing, the open-hole section, and visible fracture zones directly. It removes the guesswork before anyone commits to a treatment.

If the evidence says alluvium, we will tell you so and steer you toward options that actually fit—pump and screen rehabilitation, a deeper or relocated well, or storage and treatment changes. We would rather lose the job than charge you for a treatment your geology won't reward.

French Valley Scenarios We See

A few patterns come up again and again in this part of Riverside County. A family on a half-acre lot up against the hills toward the southeast notices the well that used to keep the landscaping green now sputters by late afternoon in July—their well is in fractured granite, and that is a textbook hydrofracturing case. A property closer to the valley floor and the airport has slowly declining output, but the driller's log shows sand and gravel with a long screen—pressure won't help there, and we say so. And a newer well drilled into rock that simply came in weak from day one—disappointing yield, but not because anything failed—often responds well to a development frac because the natural fractures were never fully opened during drilling.

What to Check and What to Expect

Before treatment we measure your current yield as a baseline and, when useful, run a camera. During treatment we isolate target zones with packers and bring the pressure up in stages, watching how the formation accepts water. Afterward we re-test the flow, usually confirming the result within 24 to 48 hours, and give you a documented before/after comparison so the improvement is something you can see, not just take on faith. In good fractured-rock wells around here, a 2x to 5x improvement is a realistic expectation, though every well is different and no honest contractor will promise a specific number.

French Valley Hydrofracturing FAQ

Will hydrofracturing work on any well in French Valley?

No. It works on wells finished in fractured bedrock or decomposed granite, which are common around the valley margins and uplands. It generally does not work on wells screened in the loose sand and gravel of the valley floor. We help you confirm which type you have before recommending treatment.

How do I find out if my well is in rock or in alluvium?

The fastest way is your driller's log (well completion report), which lists what the drill passed through. You can also ask whether the well is open-hole in rock or screened in sand and gravel. If no record exists, a downhole camera survey settles it.

How much does hydrofracturing cost compared with a new well?

Hydrofracturing a suitable well typically runs $3,000-$8,000, versus roughly $18,000-$42,000 for a new turnkey well. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward the work if you proceed.

Is hydrofracturing safe for my drinking water?

Yes. It uses only clean water and pressure—no chemicals of any kind. It is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking despite the similar name.

How long does the process take and when will I see results?

The treatment itself usually takes four to eight hours. We re-test flow afterward and typically confirm the improvement within 24 to 48 hours, with a documented before/after comparison.

What if you decide my well isn't a good candidate?

We tell you plainly and point you toward options that fit your geology, such as pump or screen rehabilitation, a deeper or relocated well, or storage and treatment improvements. We won't sell you a treatment your well can't benefit from.
