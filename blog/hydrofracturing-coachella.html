What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Coachella Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Coachella Valley Wells

Coachella sits at the agricultural heart of the eastern Coachella Valley, where private and irrigation wells have supported date palms, citrus, table grapes, and rural homesteads for generations. Unlike the granite-bound foothill communities to the west, Coachella draws its groundwater from a deep alluvial basin—layers of sand, gravel, and clay washed down over thousands of years from the surrounding San Bernardino, San Jacinto, and Little San Bernardino mountains. When a well here loses yield, the cause is rarely the same as it is up in the rock country, and the right rehabilitation approach has to match the local geology. Hydrofracturing (often spelled hydrofracking) is one of the most cost-effective tools available for restoring a struggling well in and around Coachella, particularly where a well is screened in tighter, finer-grained zones or where mineral scale has choked off the original water-bearing layers.

Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years working on wells across Riverside County, and we approach every Coachella job by first understanding what the local aquifer is actually doing beneath your property. Whether you own a few acres off Avenue 50, a grove near Thermal, or a rural home toward the Salton Sea, declining water in this valley deserves a careful diagnosis before anyone talks about drilling something new.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation process that uses nothing but clean, high-pressure water to reopen and clear the water-bearing pathways feeding your well. It shares a name with oil-and-gas fracking, but the two have almost nothing in common: there are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no additives—just water pressure applied in a controlled, targeted way.

The process works like this. Our crew lowers a set of inflatable rubber packers into your well casing. When inflated, these packers seal off a specific section of the borehole, isolating the zone we want to treat so that all of the energy is directed exactly where it is needed. We then pump clean water into that isolated interval at pressures typically ranging from 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. That pressure pushes water into the existing fractures, bedding planes, and water-bearing seams surrounding the borehole, flushing out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have plugged them and widening the natural passages so groundwater can move freely again.

In a layered alluvial setting like the Coachella Valley, hydrofracturing is especially useful for breaking through the fine sediment and carbonate scale that accumulates against a well screen and in the gravel pack over years of pumping. By isolating and treating one zone at a time, we can work methodically from one productive interval to the next, then run a flow test to measure exactly how much the yield has improved.

Why Coachella Wells Lose Yield

Several patterns show up again and again on wells around Coachella, Thermal, Mecca, and the surrounding farmland:

  • Mineral scale and incrustation. Groundwater in the eastern valley often carries high levels of dissolved calcium, carbonates, and iron. Over time these minerals precipitate out and coat the well screen and gravel pack, slowly strangling flow. This is one of the most common—and most treatable—causes of decline here.
  • Fine sediment plugging. Wells screened in sandy alluvium can draw in silt and fine sand that packs into the screen openings and surrounding gravel, choking the well's intake.
  • Declining regional water table. Heavy agricultural and municipal demand, plus long dry stretches, have lowered groundwater levels in parts of the valley. A well that was once comfortably below the water table may now sit near the bottom of available water during the hot summer pumping season.
  • Bacterial and biofouling buildup. Iron-related bacteria can form slime that clogs screens, a frequent issue in warm desert groundwater.
  • Aging well infrastructure. Many Coachella-area wells were drilled decades ago and have simply never been rehabilitated.

Hydrofracturing directly addresses scale, sediment plugging, and biofouling by mechanically reopening the formation around the borehole. Where the underlying problem is a dropping regional water table, we will tell you honestly that fracturing alone may not be the full answer—and we will walk you through the alternatives.

Signs Your Coachella Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Watch for these warning signs, especially as you move into the peak-demand summer months when desert irrigation and household use both spike:

  • Your well produces noticeably less water than it did a few years ago.
  • The well "draws down" quickly and the pump cycles on and off or runs dry during heavy use.
  • Water pressure at the house or in irrigation lines has steadily weakened.
  • Recovery is slow—after a long pumping session, the well takes much longer than it used to refill.
  • You see more sediment, cloudiness, or sand in the water than before.
  • A newly drilled well came in with disappointing yield from the start.

If two or more of these sound familiar, it is worth having the well evaluated before the next heat wave puts your supply to the test.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick observations help us diagnose your well faster. Note roughly when the decline started and whether it tracks with the seasons. Check whether your pump is short-cycling. If you have any records of the well's original depth and yield, or the well completion report, have them ready. And note your typical demand—a few household fixtures versus several acres of date palms or citrus call for very different solutions.

When to Call a Professional

Frequently Asked Questions

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean, pressurized water. There are no chemicals, sand proppants, or additives involved. It is a long-established, environmentally safe method for rehabilitating drinking-water and irrigation wells.

Will hydrofracturing work on a well in the Coachella Valley's sandy soil?

It depends on how your well is built and screened. Hydrofracturing is most effective at clearing scale, fine sediment, and biofouling that have plugged the screen and gravel pack—problems that are common here. We always run a flow test and assess your well's construction first to confirm it is a good candidate before recommending the treatment.

How much can I expect my well's yield to improve?

Results vary with each well, but many wells see a meaningful increase in flow once the plugged pathways are reopened. We document your yield before and after with flow tests so you can see the actual improvement, not just a promise.

How long does the process take?

A typical hydrofracturing job takes about four to eight hours on site. We usually verify the improved yield with a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours.

Is hydrofracturing cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing typically costs $3,000–$8,000, while a new turnkey well runs $18,000–$42,000. For a well that is a reasonable candidate, fracturing is the smart first step before considering replacement.

My summer water shortage is seasonal—can you help?

Often, yes. If your well draws down hard only during peak summer demand, reopening the formation can improve both yield and recovery rate. If the issue is a dropping regional water table, we will tell you honestly and discuss deepening or other options.
