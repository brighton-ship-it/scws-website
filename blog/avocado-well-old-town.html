Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Old Town well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Avocado Water Systems for the Old Town San Diego Area

Old Town is the historic heart of San Diego — the birthplace of California, tucked at the foot of Presidio Hill where the San Diego River meets Mission Valley and Interstate 5 and Interstate 8 cross. It is a dense, historic, city-serviced district, so it sits squarely on municipal water rather than wells, and it is not a commercial avocado region. But San Diego County is genuine avocado country, and the trees that define the inland north county are the same Hass and Bacon and Fuerte that backyard growers nurture on lots all around the metro area, including the older homes and canyon edges near Old Town. Whether you keep a few cherished trees in the city or run irrigation for a planting out in the county's avocado belt, Southern California Well Service brings 30+ years of local water expertise to keeping avocados healthy.

Why the Avocado Is a Water-Hungry, Salt-Shy Tree

Two facts shape every avocado watering decision. First, the tree is thirsty: a mature canopy can transpire 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and a producing acre may need four to six acre-feet a year, peaking through flowering and summer fruit-sizing. Second, the tree is salt-shy: avocados are among the most chloride-sensitive of all tree crops, so water quality is every bit as important as quantity. Get either one wrong and the tree responds the same way — dropped fruit and scorched leaves. A water system that respects both is the goal.

The Components of a Reliable Grove Water Supply

  • A pump sized for the planting, not just a house — peak grove demand is what matters.
  • Storage — a tank that fills during off hours and discharges a strong irrigation set on demand, the cheapest way to make a modest well perform like a large one.
  • Constant-pressure or booster equipment ($2,000–$4,500) for uniform pressure across uneven ground.
  • Sediment filtration ($300–$900) to keep fine emitters from clogging.
  • Treatment — softening or iron and manganese removal ($1,500–$3,500) when chemistry demands it.

Managing Salinity So Your Trees Thrive

Because Old Town and the surrounding lower-elevation, coastal-adjacent parts of San Diego County can see groundwater with elevated chloride and salinity, salt management is front and center. We start with a water analysis that measures the parameters that actually matter to an avocado: chloride, sodium, boron, bicarbonate, and total dissolved solids. From the numbers we build a plan: blend with a cleaner source when one is available, design a leaching fraction into each irrigation to flush salts past the roots, recommend salt-tolerant rootstock for new plantings, and set a soil and leaf-tissue testing rhythm so creeping salinity is caught early. The point is to manage salt deliberately rather than discover it in burned foliage.

Homeowner Checks Before You Call

  • Run a full irrigation cycle and watch pressure. A drop as zones open means the pump or booster is undersized or tired.
  • Inspect emitters and lines. Uneven flow points to sediment or scale.
  • Read the leaves. Tip and margin burn is salt; broad yellowing is nutrition or overwatering.
  • Time the recovery on a well. Slow rebuild suggests a yield issue solvable with storage or, in rock wells, hydrofracturing.
  • Find the well log if you have a well — depth, casing, and tested yield speed our diagnosis.

Where to Stop and Call a Pro

Swapping a filter, resetting a pressure switch, or fine-tuning emitter run-times is reasonable owner work. Pump service, wellhead wiring, casing work, and water-chemistry interpretation belong with a licensed contractor. A submersible pump is heavy and dangerous to pull, and an undersized replacement wastes energy for years. As a licensed C-57 water-well contractor running our own equipment, we look at the entire system and tell you the truth — including when storage or treatment beats a bigger pump.

A Neighborhood Built Around Water

It is fitting that an article about irrigation should center on Old Town, because this district exists where it does precisely because of water. The Kumeyaay, then the Spanish at the Presidio, settled the rise above the San Diego River for access to its flow, and Mission Valley below has been farmed and watered for centuries. That long history is a useful reminder for any avocado grower: in a Mediterranean climate this dry, the reliability of your water source is the single factor that decides whether trees survive a bad summer. Modern Old Town runs on imported municipal water, but only a short drive inland the county returns to private wells drawing from fractured granite and alluvial basins, and the old lesson still holds — secure your water first, then plant.

Scheduling Irrigation Through a Dry-Summer Climate

San Diego hands avocados a long rainless stretch from late spring through fall, and a sensible schedule works with that rhythm. Spring flowering and fruit-set is the critical window: water stress here translates directly into dropped fruit, so the system must be ready before the first heat event. Summer is peak transpiration, when frequent, measured sets keep the shallow roots both moist and oxygenated. Fall maturation eases the demand gradually, and winter is the time to lean on rainfall and pull back to avoid waterlogging roots that have slowed for the season. We help growers translate that calendar into emitter run-times matched to their soil, and we size pump and storage so the system never falls behind on the handful of brutal Santa Ana days that cause the most damage.

The Real San Diego County Avocado Belt

Old Town itself is urban, but it sits at the doorstep of one of the country's most important avocado regions. The inland north of San Diego County — the hills and valleys around Fallbrook, Pauma Valley, Valley Center, Bonsall, and the Ramona area — produces a large share of California's avocados, almost all of it irrigated, much of it from private wells and agricultural water districts. Those groves face exactly the challenges this page describes: high summer water demand, salt-sensitive trees, sloping ground that complicates pressure, and the ever-present pressure of water cost. When we serve a backyard grower near Old Town, we are drawing on decades of work in that belt, and the same principles that keep a commercial Pauma grove producing apply to a handful of trees on a city lot.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm near Old Town on city water. Can you help with my avocado trees?

Yes. We optimize irrigation pressure, filtration, and water quality on municipal supply, and we serve the county's avocado belt where trees run on wells. If a well would benefit your property, we will tell you honestly.

Why are my avocado leaves burning at the tips?

Tip and margin burn is the classic sign of chloride or salt buildup. A water and soil test confirms it, and the fix is usually leaching and blending rather than more fertilizer.

How much water does an avocado really need?

A mature tree can use 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day; a producing acre needs roughly four to six acre-feet a year, concentrated in the warm months. Peak capacity, not the annual average, is what protects the crop.

Can a small well support irrigation?

Often yes, by pairing the well with a storage tank that fills slowly overnight and discharges quickly during your set — far cheaper than drilling deeper.

Do you respond to emergencies?

Yes, same-day. A loss of water during fruit set or a heat wave can drop a crop, so we prioritize those calls.
