Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

What Citrus Trees Demand for Water in Homeland

Mature citrus drinks year-round, and the volumes are significant. In the hot inland climate around Homeland, a producing grove typically uses on the order of 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the heaviest demand in the long, hot stretch from late spring into early fall. One acre-foot is roughly 326,000 gallons, so even a few acres of mature trees pull a remarkable amount of water across a season.

Turning Annual Use Into Pumping Capacity

The planning challenge is converting that yearly volume into the flow your well must sustain at peak. Many citrus growers aim for a well and pump that can hold roughly 15 to 25 GPM per acre of steady output, with the exact figure shaped by your soils, your irrigation method, and how much storage you carry. Drip and micro-sprinkler systems use water far more efficiently than flood irrigation, which both lowers the live flow you must produce and helps you use a regulated groundwater supply wisely.

Why Frost Protection Rewrites the Numbers

Citrus is sensitive to cold, and the inland valleys around Homeland see real freezes. Growers who guard their trees with overhead sprinklers face a brief but enormous demand: frost protection can require 50 or more GPM per acre, sustained for hours during the coldest part of the night. That is many times the routine irrigation rate, and a well sized only for summer watering cannot deliver it on its own. This is the main reason frost-protected groves invest in substantial storage.

Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage for Citrus

A reliable citrus water system is really three coordinated pieces: the well, the pump, and the storage that ties them together.

The Well

An agricultural well for citrus is built for both volume and durability. Casing, screen placement, and gravel pack are tailored to the local formation so the well yields consistently and stands up to sand and collapse over many years of heavy seasonal pumping.

The Pump

High-capacity citrus wells generally rely on a submersible pump set deep in the borehole or a vertical lineshaft turbine driven from the surface. Each can move large volumes against significant lift. Getting the size right is critical: an oversized pump can overdraw and damage the well, while an undersized one leaves trees stressed at the height of summer. We match the pump to the well's tested yield rather than to a generic spec.

Storage Ponds and Tanks

Storage is the piece that ties everything together. By pumping into a lined pond or a set of steel or poly tanks during off-peak hours and irrigating from that reserve, a modestly sized well can serve a much larger grove, weather brief outages, and supply the huge frost-protection surge without an oversized well. On rural Homeland parcels where power can be intermittent, that stored buffer is real protection for the trees.

Water Quality for Citrus: Salinity, Chloride, and Boron

Citrus is among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so what is in the water matters as much as how much of it you have. Before planting or rehabilitating a grove, the well water should be tested.

  • Salinity (EC): Citrus tolerates only limited salinity. Rising total dissolved solids stress the roots, slow growth, and erode yield over time.
  • Chloride: Citrus is distinctly chloride-sensitive. Chloride accumulates in the leaves and produces the classic tip and margin leaf burn. Rootstock choice moves the threshold, but high-chloride water stays a long-term liability.
  • Boron: A micronutrient with a very narrow safe band. Citrus can show toxicity once boron climbs much above roughly 0.5 to 1 ppm, so even small amounts deserve testing.

If a test turns up a problem, the options run from blending sources and leaching salts with careful irrigation to treatment and rootstock selection. We can test your Homeland well water and translate the readings into clear choices for your grove.

Drought, Groundwater, and SGMA Near Homeland

Homeland overlies the San Jacinto Groundwater Basin, the alluvial basin that underlies the San Jacinto, Perris, Moreno, and Menifee Valleys in western Riverside County. This basin falls under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA). It is classified as a high-priority basin, a Groundwater Sustainability Plan for the area has been adopted and is being implemented, and Eastern Municipal Water District acts as the groundwater sustainability agency over much of it. Portions of the broader basin near the Hemet and San Jacinto area are also adjudicated under the Hemet-San Jacinto Watermaster.

For a grower, the practical message is that this groundwater is a shared, regulated resource. Efficient irrigation, accurate metering, and well-maintained equipment make good economic sense and keep you in step with long-term basin management. During drought years, static water levels can decline, and a pump set too high in the casing may begin to break suction or draw air, so periodic review of how the well is performing is worthwhile.

Citrus Well Problems We See Around Homeland

  • Declining yield or pump cavitation as seasonal water levels fall, especially in late summer and through drought.
  • Mineral scaling and encrustation on screens, columns, and pump parts in mineral-rich groundwater, gradually choking flow.
  • Clogged drip emitters and micro-sprinklers from sediment, iron, or biological fouling when filtration is inadequate.
  • Worn or undersized pumps that cannot meet peak summer demand, much less a frost-protection surge.
  • Pressure-tank and control problems that cause short-cycling and shorten equipment life.

Maintenance That Keeps a Grove Producing

Citrus wells reward consistent care. We recommend periodic flow and water-level testing to catch a declining well before it fails, routine inspection of the pump, motor, and electrical controls, and steady cleaning of filters and emitters so the irrigation system keeps delivering. When a well loses output to mineral encrustation or fine sediment, well rehabilitation and hydrofracturing can often restore lost yield for a fraction of the cost of drilling a new well. Regular water testing also lets you track salinity and chloride trends before they ever harm the trees.

When to Call a Professional

Get in touch when you notice falling pressure or yield, air spitting through the lines, sand or cloudy water, rising power bills from a struggling pump, or any sign that the well cannot keep up during heat or a freeze. Frequently Asked Questions

How large a well does a Homeland citrus grove need?

It depends on acreage, soils, irrigation method, and whether you protect against frost. A common rule of thumb is enough sustained capacity to cover peak summer use, supported by storage. We test your well and size the pump and storage to your specific grove rather than relying on a generic number.

Why do my citrus leaves have burned tips and edges?

Tip and margin leaf burn usually points to chloride or general salinity in the irrigation water, to which citrus is especially sensitive. A water test will confirm it, and we can then discuss leaching, blending, treatment, or rootstock options.

Does a small amount of boron really matter for citrus?

Yes. Citrus can show boron toxicity once levels rise much above roughly 0.5 to 1 ppm, so even modest concentrations in well water deserve testing and a management plan.

Do I need a separate setup for frost protection?

If you use overhead sprinklers for freeze protection, you will need far higher instantaneous flow than routine irrigation, often 50 or more GPM per acre. Most growers meet that with on-site storage rather than oversizing the well.

How does SGMA affect my Homeland well?

The San Jacinto Groundwater Basin beneath the Homeland area is a high-priority basin managed under SGMA with an adopted sustainability plan. In practice, efficient irrigation, metering, and good maintenance let you use a shared resource responsibly while keeping the grove productive.

Can a low-yield well be improved without redrilling?

Often, yes. Hydrofracturing and rehabilitation can restore capacity in many wells for far less than a new well. We start with a diagnostic to determine what is realistic for your well.
