  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Sunset Cliffs well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well and Water-System Service Near Sunset Cliffs

It is dense, residential, and entirely served by the City of San Diego municipal water system, which means there are no commercial avocado groves and essentially no private agricultural wells within the neighborhood itself. We say that plainly rather than dress up a coastal cliffside community as farm country. What Sunset Cliffs residents do have is salt air, ocean-influenced soils, and a strong interest in healthy landscaping, and those who own well-served property elsewhere in San Diego County can rely on us for the real work. This guide covers how private well systems function, the issues we see across the county, and what honest pricing looks like.

If you keep a vacation or income property inland, own a parcel in the county's backcountry, or are simply researching wells and water quality, the fundamentals below apply whether your water serves a household, a garden, or a few acres of trees.

How a Coastal-County Well System Works

A private well is a connected chain of equipment, and a fault in one part usually shows up as a symptom somewhere else.

  • Borehole and casing: the drilled, lined shaft. Inland San Diego County wells, in granite and fractured rock, commonly reach 200 to 600 feet.
  • Submersible pump: lifts water to the surface, sized to depth and demand.
  • Pressure tank: stores pressurized water so the pump does not start every time you open a tap.
  • Pressure switch and controls: the inexpensive parts that govern when the pump runs.
  • Treatment and storage: filtration, softening, and tanks added where water quality or peak demand calls for them.

Coastal and Inland Water Quality Considerations

Two themes come up repeatedly in our region. The first is salinity. Wells near the coast, or in basins influenced by seawater intrusion, can show elevated chloride and salt, which is hard on plumbing and on salt-sensitive plants. Testing tells you what you are dealing with, and blending, treatment, or reverse osmosis can address it. The second is hardness and minerals, which are common in inland groundwater and cause scale in pipes and appliances. Even though Sunset Cliffs itself is on city water, owners with wells elsewhere benefit from knowing which of these issues their water faces before choosing a treatment system.

Problems We Diagnose Most Often

No Water at the Tap

A complete loss of water usually traces to a tripped breaker, a failed pressure switch, a burned-out pump, or a control fault. Because several causes produce the same symptom, a methodical diagnostic visit saves money over guesswork.

Weak or Surging Pressure

Pressure that drops when two fixtures run, or a pump that cycles rapidly, most often means a pressure tank that has lost its air charge. Other causes include a clogged filter or a developing leak.

Salt, Sediment, or Odor

Briny taste signals salinity; grit signals a worn pump or screen issue; a sulfur smell signals hydrogen sulfide. Each has a defined fix once tested.

Safe Checks Before You Call

  1. Reset the breaker once. Repeated tripping is an electrical fault that needs a professional.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Note the psi so we know where the system stands.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. A waterlogged tank sounds and feels heavy all the way up.
  4. Look for leaks. Damp soil along the supply line can indicate a hidden leak.
  5. Note recent events. A storm, a power outage, or a gradual decline each points to different causes.

Leave pump pulling and any wiring work to a licensed crew; those tasks combine high voltage with heavy equipment set hundreds of feet down a well.

Maintenance Keeps Coastal-Property Wells Healthy

For owners with wells, salt air and seasonal demand both argue for routine care. We suggest an annual check of pressure-tank charge and pump cycling, a water test every year or two that includes chloride where salinity is a concern, and scheduled sediment-filter changes. Wells past fifteen or twenty years deserve a proactive inspection so a pump can be replaced on your timetable instead of during a failure. A short maintenance visit is far cheaper than an emergency, and we are glad to fold an irrigation check into the same trip.

Why Salt Air Matters for Equipment

Even setting groundwater aside, the marine environment around Sunset Cliffs is tough on hardware. Salt-laden air corrodes exposed metal fittings, pressure-tank stands, electrical contacts, and well caps faster than it would inland. For any pumping or irrigation equipment installed near the coast, we favor corrosion-resistant materials, sealed connections, and stainless components where it counts. If you are installing a booster pump for a hillside garden or a cistern system to capture and reuse water, choosing the right materials at the outset saves repeated repairs down the line. This is exactly the kind of local detail an out-of-area contractor can overlook, and it is one reason we encourage homeowners to ask about materials before signing off on any installation.

Rainwater, Cisterns, and Smart Irrigation

Coastal San Diego sees long dry seasons punctuated by occasional heavy storms, and many Sunset Cliffs homeowners ask about capturing that rain for landscape use. A cistern paired with a small pump and pressure system can store winter runoff for spring and summer irrigation, easing pressure on the municipal supply and lowering bills. We design and install these systems with the same pumps, tanks, and controls used on wells, so the engineering is familiar territory for our crews. Whether you are watering succulents on a bluff lot or a row of fruit trees in a sheltered yard, an efficient drip or micro-sprinkler layout delivers water to the roots and minimizes the loss to coastal wind and evaporation.
