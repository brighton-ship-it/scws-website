Why Wells Go Dry

  • Drought Conditions: Extended dry periods lower the water table
  • Increased Demand: More wells in the area drawing from the same aquifer
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: Water table naturally drops in summer/fall
  • Pump Position: Pump may be positioned above current water level
  • Well Age: Mineral buildup reducing water entry points
  • Aquifer Depletion: Long-term overdrawing of groundwater

Solutions for Dry Wells in Borrego Springs

1. Well Deepening

If the water table has dropped, deepening your existing well may reach water again. This is often less expensive than drilling a new well.

2. Hydrofracturing

High-pressure water treatment can open new fractures in bedrock, allowing more water to flow into your well.

3. Pump Lowering

Sometimes simply lowering your pump deeper in the well can restore water production.

4. New Well Drilling

When rehabilitation isn't viable, we can drill a new well in a better location on your property.

Dry and Low-Yield Wells in Borrego Springs: A Community on the Front Line

Few places in California feel the reality of groundwater depletion as acutely as Borrego Springs. This desert town in eastern San Diego County, ringed by Anza-Borrego Desert State Park, draws every drop of its water from the Borrego Valley Groundwater Basin — a basin that the State of California has formally designated as critically overdrafted. For decades, pumping has pulled water out far faster than the desert can recharge it, and the water table beneath Borrego Springs has been falling by roughly two feet per year. For property owners with private wells, that long, steady decline is exactly why wells here run dry, and it shapes every decision about how to respond.

Southern California Well Service has more than 30 years of experience with desert wells, and Borrego Springs presents a uniquely challenging case. Understanding the basin is the first step to making a smart, durable decision about your water.

Why Borrego Springs Wells Go Dry

The Borrego Valley basin is an alluvial system with an upper, middle, and lower aquifer; the middle aquifer is several hundred feet thick near the center of the valley, and the lower aquifer reaches well over a thousand feet in the southern portion. The problem is not a lack of total water so much as a relentless mismatch between extraction and recharge:

  • Critical overdraft. The basin has been pumped at several times its natural recharge rate. State and federal studies have documented an overdraft on the order of thousands of acre-feet per year, and the official sustainability plan calls for cutting pumping dramatically over the coming years.
  • A falling water table. With levels dropping about two feet annually over many decades, wells that were once productive — especially those nearer the edges of the aquifer — have been running dry and being abandoned.
  • Wells outpaced by the decline. A well drilled to a depth that was comfortable 20 or 30 years ago may now have its screen and pump above the current water level.
  • Water-quality changes near the margins. As levels fall, some peripheral wells see declining quality alongside declining quantity.
  • Intense summer demand. Landscape, agriculture, and recreation drive heavy seasonal pumping that accelerates local drawdown.

How We Confirm a Borrego Springs Well Is Dry

In a basin declining this steadily, precise measurement is everything. Our technician sounds the well to establish the current static and pumping water levels, compares them against your well's completion record and the known basin trend, and measures recovery after a controlled drawdown. We also verify the pump and controls so we do not mistake an equipment failure for an aquifer problem. The picture that emerges — how far the water has fallen, how much usable column remains, and how the well recovers — tells us whether we are dealing with placement, capacity, or basin-level depletion. The diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward any work performed.

Your Options — and the Honest Trade-Offs in an Overdrafted Basin

Lower the Pump

When the basin's decline has left your pump hanging above the water, resetting it deeper in the existing casing is the immediate, lowest-cost fix — $800 to $2,500. In a falling basin, however, we will be candid: lowering the pump buys time, and you should plan for the long-term trend.

Deepen the Existing Well

Because Borrego's aquifers extend hundreds to over a thousand feet, deepening an existing well to follow the water down is often viable. At $5,000 to $15,000, it can extend a well's life significantly — though, again, in a critically overdrafted basin we plan for continued decline.

Add Storage and a Booster Pump

A storage tank with a booster pump is one of the most practical responses to a slow or marginal desert well. The well fills a cistern continuously while the booster delivers full household pressure on demand, smoothing out a low or intermittent yield. Budget roughly $3,000 to $7,000.

Flow-Management Controls

Low-water cutoffs and flow controllers protect the pump from running dry and let a modest yield serve the household reliably. In Borrego Springs, conservation-minded controls are not just protective — they align with the basin's mandated path toward sustainable use.

Hydrofracturing

Hydrofracturing is less commonly the answer in a thick alluvial basin than in fractured bedrock, but where a well's screen and gravel pack have fouled, rehabilitation techniques can restore lost capacity. Cost ranges $3,000 to $8,000; we will tell you honestly whether your well is a candidate.

Drill a New, Deeper Well

For a well that can no longer reach water, a new, deeper well is the most durable answer, with turnkey pricing of $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth. In Borrego Springs this is a significant decision tied to the basin's regulated future, and we will help you weigh it carefully and in light of local pumping rules.

When to Call a Professional

If your faucets sputter air, you are pumping sand, the pump short-cycles, or you lose pressure across the house, shut the pump off and call a licensed C-57 contractor. Running a pump dry destroys motors quickly. Beyond the immediate repair, Borrego Springs property owners benefit from a contractor who understands the basin's groundwater management framework — something a general plumber cannot offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do so many wells run dry in Borrego Springs?

Borrego Springs draws entirely from the Borrego Valley Groundwater Basin, which the State of California has designated as critically overdrafted. Pumping has long exceeded recharge, and the water table has been falling roughly two feet per year for decades, leaving older and shallower wells above the current water level.

How deep is the aquifer under Borrego Springs?

The basin has stacked aquifers: a middle aquifer several hundred feet thick near the valley center and a lower aquifer that reaches well over a thousand feet in the southern portion. That depth is why deepening an existing well is often viable, though the basin's ongoing decline must be factored in.

Is it worth deepening my well if the basin keeps dropping?

It can be. Deepening ($5,000 to $15,000) follows the water down and can add years of service. But in a critically overdrafted basin we are honest that the regional decline continues, so we also discuss storage, flow controls, and long-term planning before you invest.

Will a storage tank help my low-yield Borrego well?

Yes. A storage tank and booster pump let a slow or intermittent well fill a cistern around the clock while the booster supplies strong household pressure on demand. At roughly $3,000 to $7,000, it is one of the most practical desert solutions and supports conservation.

Do I need special permits to work on a well in Borrego Springs?

Borrego Springs falls under a state-mandated groundwater management framework for the critically overdrafted basin, in addition to San Diego County well rules. As a licensed C-57 contractor familiar with the area, we help you navigate the requirements that a general plumber would not be equipped to handle.
