What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Hinkley well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Well Water in Hinkley: A Local Guide

Hinkley is a small farming and ranching community in the Mojave Desert of San Bernardino County, lying northwest of Barstow along the Mojave River corridor. Spread across open desert with alfalfa fields, dairies, and scattered homesteads, Hinkley has no town water system, and residents depend on private wells drawn from the valley's alluvial aquifer. The community is unusually water-aware, and for good reason: groundwater quality has been a defining local issue for decades.

The aquifer beneath Hinkley sits in deep sand, gravel, and silt deposited by the Mojave River. That groundwater is typically hard and high in total dissolved solids, and iron and manganese are common across the basin. When Hinkley well owners see rust-colored water, it generally comes from naturally dissolved iron in that mineral-heavy aquifer or from steel hardware corroding within it. Because the community pays close attention to its water, getting an accurate test and a proper diagnosis matters more here than almost anywhere.

How Hinkley Water Becomes Rusty

Underground, in the oxygen-poor depths of the aquifer, iron dissolves into clear ferrous iron, so a Hinkley well can pump water that looks clean at the casing. Exposed to air at a faucet, toilet tank, or water heater, the iron oxidizes into rust-orange ferric particles that stain fixtures and laundry. The basin's hardness makes that staining especially tenacious.

Two other sources round out the picture. The Mojave River groundwater can be mineral-aggressive on equipment, so galvanized plumbing and older steel casings corrode and add rust directly. And iron bacteria can colonize a desert well, leaving a reddish slime and a musty smell that requires chlorination rather than a filter. Sorting iron from bacteria from corrosion is the job of a water test.

Common Rusty-Water Scenarios in Hinkley

Heavy mineral staining

Hinkley's high-TDS groundwater produces stubborn orange staining on fixtures and laundry, often alongside hard-water scale.

Sediment from agricultural demand

Properties irrigating fields or pasture pump heavily, which can draw sediment and minerals up from the aquifer and cloud the water.

Slime and odor in tanks

A rusty biofilm with a swampy smell points to iron bacteria, at home in the warm desert aquifer.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Hot or cold side? Rust only when hot usually means the water heater.
  • Whole property or one tap? A single rusty fixture points to a corroded line.
  • Does it settle out? Orange particles dropping in a glass confirm oxidized iron.
  • Check the pressure tank and filters. Worn parts release rust on each cycle.
  • Note the smell. Metallic, sulfur, or swampy each points to a different cause.

Treatment Options and What They Cost in Hinkley

Given the mineral load of Mojave River groundwater, treatment is usually multi-stage. Sediment filtration runs $300 to $900, and a water softener ($1,500 to $3,500) addresses the heavy hardness. Dissolved iron beyond a softener's range needs an oxidizing iron filter, and iron bacteria are cleared with shock chlorination. When the rust traces to worn equipment, a new pressure tank ($600 to $1,500) or a deep-set submersible pump ($2,500 to $5,500) solves it at the source.
