Power Outage Backup Solutions for Wells: How to Keep Your Water Running

When the power goes out in Southern California, most people lose lights and air conditioning. Well owners lose their water supply. Whether it's a PSPS (Public Safety Power Shutoff), wildfire-related outage, or simple grid failure, your submersible well pump needs electricity to run. With power outages becoming more frequent and lasting longer in San Diego, Riverside, and throughout fire-prone California, having a backup power plan for your well isn't optional—it's essential.

⚡ Backup Power Quick Guide

  • • Generator sizing: 5,000-7,500 watts for most wells
  • • Transfer switch: Required for safe operation ($300-$800)
  • • Solar + battery: $15,000-$35,000 for complete system
  • • Water storage: Always have 2+ days stored regardless

Why Well Owners Need Backup Power

Unlike homes on municipal water, well owners rely entirely on their own equipment to access water. When the power fails, you immediately lose:

  • Drinking water: No water from taps for drinking, cooking, or baby formula
  • Toilets: Modern toilets need water pressure to refill—they become unusable after one flush
  • Showers and bathing: No running water for hygiene
  • Livestock and animals: Horses, cattle, chickens, and pets all need water
  • Gardens and orchards: Irrigation stops, putting plants at risk
  • Fire protection: If a wildfire threatens during an outage, you have no water to protect your property

In 2019 and 2020, California saw PSPS events lasting 3-5 days in some areas. The 2023 atmospheric river storms caused multi-day outages throughout San Diego County. Rural properties in Ramona, Julian, Valley Center, Temecula, and similar areas are especially vulnerable because they're often last priority for power restoration.

Understanding Your Well Pump's Power Requirements

Before choosing a backup power solution, you need to know your well pump's electrical requirements:

Pump Voltage

  • 120V pumps: Shallow wells only (usually under 80 feet)—less common
  • 240V pumps: Most residential submersible pumps—requires 240V generator or inverter
  • Check your control box or pump specifications to confirm

Startup (Surge) vs. Running Wattage

This is the critical specification most people get wrong. Electric motors need 2-3x more power to start than to run continuously.

Pump Size (HP) Running Watts Startup Watts Minimum Generator
1/2 HP 1,000 W 2,100 W 3,000 W
3/4 HP 1,500 W 3,000 W 4,000 W
1 HP 2,000 W 4,100 W 5,000 W
1.5 HP 2,500 W 5,000 W 6,500 W
2 HP 3,000 W 6,000 W 7,500 W
3 HP 4,500 W 9,000 W 10,000 W

⚠️ Common Mistake: Undersizing the Generator

If your generator can't handle the startup surge, your pump won't start—and attempting to start it repeatedly can damage both the generator and your pump. Always size your generator for startup watts, not running watts.

Backup Power Options Compared

Option 1: Portable Gasoline Generator

The most affordable option for occasional use, but requires hands-on operation and fuel management.

Portable Generator: Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • • Lowest upfront cost ($800-$2,500)
  • • Portable—can use for other needs
  • • No installation required beyond transfer switch
  • • Can run other household loads too

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Requires manual startup
  • • Fuel storage and freshness concerns
  • • Loud operation
  • • No help if you're evacuated/away
  • • Needs transfer switch for safe well use

Recommended models for well pumps:

  • Honda EU7000iS: 7,000W inverter generator, quieter, $4,500-$5,000
  • Generac GP6500: 6,500W, good value, $1,000-$1,300
  • Champion 7500-Watt: Dual fuel (gas/propane), $900-$1,200
  • Westinghouse WGen7500: 7,500W, remote start option, $900-$1,100

Option 2: Standby (Whole-House) Generator

Permanently installed, automatically starts when power fails. The gold standard for reliable backup power.

Standby Generator: Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • • Automatic operation—works even if you're away
  • • Runs on propane or natural gas (no stale fuel)
  • • Quiet, enclosed design
  • • Powers entire home, not just well
  • • Increases property value

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Higher upfront cost ($5,000-$15,000 installed)
  • • Requires professional installation
  • • Needs large propane tank (250-500 gallons)
  • • Regular maintenance required
  • • Permit may be required

Recommended standby generators:

  • Generac Guardian 14kW: Most popular residential, $4,500-$6,000 + installation
  • Kohler 14RESA: Premium quality, quieter, $5,000-$7,000 + installation
  • Generac 22kW: For larger homes with 2+ HP pumps, $5,500-$7,500 + installation

Option 3: Solar with Battery Backup

The ultimate solution for energy independence—but requires significant investment and proper system design.

Solar + Battery: Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • • No fuel costs or storage needed
  • • Automatic operation
  • • Reduces/eliminates electric bills
  • • Quiet—no engine noise
  • • 25+ year lifespan for panels
  • • Tax credits reduce cost (30% federal ITC)

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Highest upfront cost
  • • Battery replacement every 10-15 years
  • • Weather dependent (cloudy days reduce capacity)
  • • Complex installation and permitting
  • • Need adequate roof/ground space

Key considerations for solar well backup:

  • Battery capacity: Need 10-20 kWh minimum to handle well pump startup surge
  • Inverter sizing: Must handle 240V and high surge current (6,000+ watts)
  • Popular batteries: Tesla Powerwall ($11,500), Enphase IQ Battery, LG RESU
  • System design: Critical to work with installer experienced with well pumps

Option 4: Hand Pump (Manual Backup)

For shallow wells under 200 feet, a manual hand pump can serve as a last-resort backup that requires no electricity.

  • Cost: $500-$1,500 for pump; $500-$1,000 for installation
  • Capacity: 3-5 gallons per minute with effort
  • Depth limit: Most effective under 100 feet; some work to 200 feet
  • Best brands: Simple Pump, Bison Pumps, Flojak

Transfer Switches: The Essential Safety Device

A transfer switch is required to safely connect a generator to your well pump. Never try to bypass this requirement—it's both dangerous and illegal.

Why You Need a Transfer Switch

  • Safety: Prevents backfeeding electricity into power lines, which can electrocute utility workers
  • Equipment protection: Isolates your home from grid power to prevent damage when power returns
  • Code compliance: Required by electrical code in all California jurisdictions
  • Insurance: Operating without a transfer switch may void homeowner's insurance

Types of Transfer Switches

  • Manual transfer switch: $200-$400 + $200-$400 installation. You flip the switch when generator starts.
  • Automatic transfer switch (ATS): $400-$1,500 + $300-$600 installation. Senses outage and switches automatically.
  • Interlock kit: $100-$200 + installation. Budget option that prevents breakers from being on simultaneously.

💡 Pro Tip: Install Before You Need It

During major outage events, electricians are booked solid and transfer switches may be backordered. Install your transfer switch now, while there's no emergency. Then when the power goes out, you simply start your generator and you're running.

Water Storage: Your First Line of Defense

Regardless of what backup power solution you choose, stored water provides immediate access without any equipment needed:

Storage Tank Options

  • Pressure tank upgrade: Larger pressure tanks (80-120 gallon) give you more water between pump cycles
  • Atmospheric storage tank: 500-2,500 gallon poly tank provides days of basic water
  • Gravity-fed system: Tank positioned uphill from home provides pressure without power

Emergency Water Storage

  • Minimum: 1 gallon per person per day for drinking/cooking
  • Recommended: 3-5 gallons per person per day (including basic sanitation)
  • Duration: Store enough for 7-14 days minimum
  • Containers: Food-grade 5-gallon jugs, 55-gallon drums, or IBC totes (275 gallon)

PSPS Preparation for Well Owners

California's Public Safety Power Shutoffs (PSPS) during high fire-risk conditions affect rural areas most severely. SDG&E, SCE, and PG&E all implement these shutoffs, sometimes with less than 24 hours notice.

PSPS Preparation Checklist

  • ☐ Sign up for utility PSPS alerts (SDG&E, SCE, or your provider)
  • ☐ When PSPS announced: Fill all bathtubs, buckets, and containers with water
  • ☐ Run dishwasher and washing machine before power goes out
  • ☐ Fill livestock water tanks to maximum
  • ☐ Verify generator has fresh fuel and starts properly
  • ☐ Charge all battery backup systems
  • ☐ Run well pump to ensure pressure tank is at maximum pressure

Special Considerations for Deep Wells

Wells over 400 feet deep (common in areas like Julian, Alpine, and parts of Riverside County) often have larger pumps with special power requirements:

  • 3-phase power: Some deep well pumps use 3-phase electricity, requiring specialized generators or phase converters
  • Higher HP motors: 2-5 HP pumps common in deep wells need larger generators (7,500-12,000 watts)
  • Soft starters: Installing a soft starter or VFD can reduce startup surge by 50-70%, allowing smaller backup systems
  • Multiple pumps: Some properties have two-stage pumping systems requiring more complex backup solutions

Frequently Asked Questions

What size generator do I need to run my well pump?

Most residential well pumps need a 5,000-7,500 watt generator to handle the startup surge. A 1 HP pump requires about 4,000 watts to start; a 1.5 HP pump needs around 5,500 watts. Always size for startup wattage, not running wattage, which is much lower.

Can I run my well pump on a portable generator?

Yes, but you need a transfer switch installed for safety. Never plug a generator directly into your home's electrical outlets or panel without a proper transfer switch—this is dangerous and illegal. A transfer switch costs $300-$800 installed and allows safe generator connection.

Will solar panels run my well pump during a power outage?

Only if you have battery storage. Grid-tied solar systems without batteries shut down during outages for safety. To run a well pump on solar during outages, you need solar panels, a battery bank (typically 10-20 kWh for a well pump), and a hybrid inverter designed for backup power.

How long can I run my well pump on a generator?

With proper fuel supply, indefinitely. A typical 6,500-watt generator uses 0.5-0.8 gallons per hour under load. A well pump only runs when you use water or the pressure tank needs refilling—typically 15-30 minutes per hour of use. Keep at least 20-50 gallons of fresh gasoline stored for extended outages.
