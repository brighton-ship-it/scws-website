Don't Wait Until Complete Failure

Many well emergencies could be prevented with attention to warning signs. If you notice any of these symptoms, call for service before you lose water completely:

  • Sputtering or air in the water lines
  • Decreased water pressure throughout the house
  • Pump running constantly or cycling on and off rapidly
  • Unusual noises from the well or pressure tank
  • Sudden increases in electric bills
  • Sandy or sediment-filled water
  • Water that looks, smells, or tastes different than normal

Addressing these warning signs promptly can prevent inconvenient emergencies and often costs less than emergency repairs.

The Urgent Problems We Fix in Corona

  • Complete loss of water — most often a failed submersible pump, a tripped control box, or a dead pressure switch.
  • Failed pressure switch or waterlogged tank — the leading causes of no-water and low-pressure calls, and usually the least expensive to fix.
  • Heat-driven pump burnout — a summer of near-continuous running is hard on any motor.
  • Electrical failures from summer surges — tripped breakers and cooked capacitors and control boxes.
  • Short-cycling — a tank that has lost its air charge makes the pump slam on and off, shortening its life fast.

How the Emergency Call Works

You reach a working technician who starts diagnosing on the phone. We help you check the well breaker, the pressure switch, and the pressure tank — and a fair number of Corona calls end right there with a reset or an inexpensive part. When a visit is needed, the truck rolls from Ramona or Anza carrying the switches, capacitors, control boxes, tanks, and common pump sizes that resolve most calls on the first trip.

On site, the technician tests power, checks the control box and capacitor, reads the pump's amperage, and inspects the tank and switch, so the repair is based on measurements rather than guesswork. You approve a clear price before we begin, and the $125 diagnostic is credited toward the repair.

What to Check Before We Arrive

  1. Reset the dedicated well breaker once. If it trips again immediately, leave it off and tell us — that suggests a shorted pump.
  2. Listen at the pressure switch for a click when you open a tap; silence usually means a switch or electrical fault.
  3. Tap the pressure tank top to bottom — a uniform water-filled sound means it's waterlogged.
  4. Note recent outages, surges, or a burning smell at the control box and pass that along on arrival.

Never open the control box or attempt to pull the pump yourself. Well circuits carry dangerous voltage and a dropped pump becomes a costly recovery.

Corona's Summer Load and Why Wells Fail When They Do

Corona sits where the Santa Ana River breaks out of the mountains, and its inland climate delivers the same brutal summers that stress every private well in the region. From late June onward, household demand climbs, horse properties toward the foothills draw hard on their troughs, and irrigation runs long into the evening. A submersible pump that idled along in spring is suddenly running most of the day, and that sustained duty is what tips a marginal motor, a fatigued capacitor, or a waterlogged pressure tank into outright failure — almost always at the least convenient moment.

Corona wells draw from the Temescal and Santa Ana groundwater areas, and properties nearer the foothills and Temescal Valley can see the water level dip during peak-demand stretches as many wells pull at once. When the level nears the pump intake, you'll notice air spitting from the faucets and a haze of sediment in the water; if it continues, the pump loses prime and can overheat. Catching those early warning signs and calling before the pump runs dry routinely turns what would have been a pump replacement into a far smaller repair.

What Happens During a Corona Emergency Service Call

A proper diagnosis is methodical, and here's the order our technician works in on your Corona property. We begin with the electrical supply, confirming voltage at the disconnect and inspecting the control box for the burnt contacts or swollen capacitor that summer surges leave behind. We then measure the pump's amperage under load — the clearest single read on whether the motor is sound — and check the pressure tank's air charge along with how the switch cuts in and out.

Those measurements let us identify the fault with confidence instead of replacing parts on a guess. A switch, capacitor, control box, or tank is usually handled on the first visit from the parts we stock. If the pump must come out of the ground, we're set up to pull it safely, install a properly sized replacement, and reconnect the electrical and plumbing to code. Before we leave, we run the system through several full pressure cycles, verify flow at the fixtures, and confirm the tank holds pressure — the final check that prevents a repeat callback.
