Sand in Your San Marcos Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your San Marcos well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your San Marcos well.

Sandy Well Water in San Marcos: What It Means and How to Fix It

San Marcos sits in the rolling inland hills of northern San Diego County, tucked between the granite ridges of Twin Oaks Valley, the slopes below Double Peak, and the rural pockets that spread toward San Elijo Hills and the Merriam Mountains. The bedrock across this part of the county is dominated by decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock of the Peninsular Ranges, with pockets of sandy alluvium filling the valley bottoms. That geology gives many San Marcos wells a distinctive character — and it is exactly why sand and grit showing up at your tap deserves prompt attention rather than a shrug.

Decomposed granite is essentially granite that has weathered into coarse, sandy particles. It makes for good building material and firm driveways, but underground it means a private well is often drawing water through — or right alongside — layers of loose granitic sand. When the mechanical parts of your well that keep that sand out begin to fail, the grit rides straight into your plumbing. Whether your well taps a sandy pocket in Twin Oaks Valley or draws from fractures in the harder rock up the hillsides, sand in the water is a signal that something has shifted.

Local well depths reflect that mixed geology. In the sandy valley fill of Twin Oaks Valley and similar low-lying pockets, useful water is often reached at more moderate depths, while fractured-granite wells on the higher slopes may need to go several hundred feet to find reliable production. Because so much of the water arrives through granitic sand and weathered rock, the screen and pump setting do the heavy lifting of keeping your water clean — and when either one is off, sand is usually the first symptom you notice.

Why Sand Shows Up in a San Marcos Well

Clean water and sudden sand rarely have the same cause. Here is what usually lies behind it:

  • A worn or failed well screen. In wells set in sandy or granitic-sand zones, a screen holds back the surrounding formation while letting water pass. Over years of use, corrosion or erosion widens the openings, and once that barrier is compromised, decomposed-granite sand flows into the well.
  • A pump drawing from the bottom of the hole. Fine sediment settles to the base of the borehole. A submersible pump set too low — or one that has slipped over time — will churn that layer and deliver it to your faucets.
  • Over-pumping the well. Many San Marcos hillside wells are modest producers. If the pump is oversized for the well's true yield, it draws water faster than the fractured rock can supply it, generating enough velocity at the intake to pull sand along with the water.
  • A falling water table. Inland San Diego County goes through long dry seasons and periodic drought. As groundwater levels drop, a pump may begin drawing from lower, sandier intervals it never reached before.
  • Casing damage. A corroded or cracked casing lets sand bypass the screen and enter directly. This is more common in older wells with steel casing that has been in the ground for decades.

Scenarios We See Around San Marcos

Patterns emerge in this terrain. Older properties in Twin Oaks Valley, where wells were drilled into sandy valley fill, tend to show sand first when late-summer demand peaks and levels drop. Homes higher up toward the Merriam Mountains or the ridgelines near San Elijo Hills more often have lower-yielding fractured-rock wells; on those, an aggressive or oversized pump is a frequent culprit. And any well that has recently had a replacement pump installed without a check of the sediment depth can begin producing grit simply because the new intake sits too low in the hole.

What to Look For Before Calling a Pro

A little observation makes the diagnosis faster and cheaper:

  1. Collect a settling sample. Fill a clear jar with cold water and let it stand. Fine, pale granitic sand suggests formation infiltration; reddish flakes suggest casing rust.
  2. Track when it happens. Constant sand, sand only at startup, or sand under heavy use each point toward different causes — settled sediment, screen failure, or over-pumping.
  3. Watch pressure and cycling. Grit combined with falling pressure or a rapidly cycling pump often means the pump is fighting a low water level.
  4. Inspect filters and tanks. Sediment collecting in a filter housing or in the toilet tank confirms the sand is coming up from the well.

The one thing to avoid is running the system for weeks while it "clears." Sand is abrasive and steadily destroys the very pump you are relying on.

How the Problem Gets Fixed

No two San Marcos wells are identical, so the first step is always a proper diagnosis rather than guessing. We measure the static and pumping water levels, check the pump's setting depth against the sediment at the bottom of the hole, evaluate the pump's condition and sizing, and inspect the casing where possible. Only then do we recommend a fix — because installing a filter when the real problem is a failed screen, or replacing a screen when the pump is simply set too low, wastes your money and leaves the sand coming.

The correct fix follows the cause. On San Marcos wells, our common solutions include:

  • Sand separators. A centrifugal separator at the wellhead spins grit out of the flow before it reaches the pressure tank, water heater, and fixtures.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration. A spin-down screen captures coarse sand and is easy to flush; downstream cartridge filters polish out finer particles.
  • Resetting the pump depth. Raising the pump above the settled sediment layer, or setting it to match the well's real water level, frequently stops sand entirely.
  • Proper pump sizing. Matching the pump to the well's yield lowers intake velocity so the fractured rock and sand pack are not pulled into the well.
  • Screen or casing work. Installing a new liner or screen where the original has failed, or sealing a breached casing section.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sand in my San Marcos well water dangerous to drink?

Sand itself is not usually a health hazard, but it signals a mechanical problem in your well and it damages your equipment. It can also carry other contaminants and should be diagnosed rather than ignored.

Why is my water sandy only in late summer?

Inland North County water tables drop during the long dry season. As the level falls, your pump may begin drawing from sandier, lower zones. Seasonal sand very often traces back to water-level changes.

Does decomposed granite make sand problems worse here?

It can. Decomposed granite weathers into coarse sand, so wells in these formations are more exposed to sand if the screen or pump setting is not right. A properly built and correctly maintained well still runs clean.

Can I just add a whole-house filter and move on?

A filter or separator protects your plumbing and appliances and is often a smart first step, but it will not repair a failed screen or a cracked casing underground. A full diagnosis tells you whether the well itself needs attention.

My pump is new — why is there sand now?

A newly installed pump set deeper than the old one can sit in the sediment at the bottom of the borehole. Raising or resetting the pump to the correct depth usually clears it.
