What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mentone Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Mentone wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in Mentone: Where It Helps and Where It Doesn't

Mentone sits just east of Redlands at the foot of the San Bernardino Mountains, around 1,600 feet, near where Mill Creek and the Santa Ana River drainage spill out of the canyons onto the valley floor. Its geology is mixed, and that matters a great deal for hydrofracturing. Much of the Mentone area sits on deep alluvial deposits, sand and gravel washed down from the mountains over thousands of years, and wells finished in that loose material are generally not good fracturing candidates. But parcels on the higher ground toward Mill Creek Canyon, the Crafton Hills, and the mountain front are often drilled into fractured bedrock, and those wells can respond very well. The honest first question for any Mentone property is therefore not "how much will fracturing help" but "is your well in rock or in sediment." We always answer that before recommending treatment.

Understanding the Two Kinds of Mentone Wells

A well drilled into the alluvial basin draws water from the spaces between sand and gravel grains. That formation already moves water freely, so high-pressure injection has little to fracture and the loose material can simply slough back, which is why we rarely recommend hydrofracturing for true valley-floor wells. A well drilled into the hard, fractured granitic and metamorphic rock of the mountain front is a different animal entirely. There, water moves only through cracks, and widening and connecting those cracks with high-pressure water can multiply the yield. If your Mentone well is several hundred feet deep into rock on the upper benches, it may be an excellent candidate. If it is a shallower well in river-valley sand, it probably is not, and we will tell you so.

Signs Worth Investigating

  • Declining yield over several years from a well you know is finished in rock
  • Slow recovery after heavy household or irrigation use
  • Seasonal shortages that worsen through the dry months and ease after winter
  • A newer bedrock well that never produced the flow the driller anticipated
  • The pump short-cycling or pulling the water level down to the intake

For any of these, our diagnostic visit is the same: confirm whether the formation is the real limit, and rule out equipment problems such as a worn pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a clogged screen. These inexpensive issues imitate low yield and are worth eliminating before any major work.

How Hydrofracturing Works When a Mentone Well Qualifies

On a qualifying bedrock well we begin with a baseline flow test, then run a camera survey to check casing condition and identify productive fracture zones. An inflatable packer isolates a section of the open borehole, and we inject clean water at pressures generally between 1,000 and 3,000 PSI to clean and extend the fractures. We commonly treat several zones, then develop the well to flush loosened sediment and run a post-treatment flow test to measure the gain. The work usually takes a single day. Throughout, we use only clean water, never chemicals, so there is no risk to your drinking water.

Realistic Results

Where a Mentone well is genuinely set in fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, typically two to five times the prior flow. Where a well is in alluvium, the odds are poor and we will steer you away. This selectivity is the whole point: we would rather decline a job than fracture a well that cannot benefit. Documented before-and-after flow testing keeps everyone honest about the result.

Cost and the Alternatives

A new well in the Mentone area runs roughly $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, depending on depth and whether it must reach bedrock. Hydrofracturing a suitable existing well typically costs $3,000 to $8,000, with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the work. For a bedrock well that has lost yield, fracturing is usually the smart first step. For a valley-floor well at the end of its life, the better path may be a new well, added storage, or a deeper borehole, and we will lay out those trade-offs plainly.

When to Call and What to Check First

Reach out when a known bedrock well has lost production, when recovery is slow, or before you commit to drilling a replacement. Beforehand, note whether your shortage is seasonal, whether the pressure gauge cycles rapidly (a tank symptom), and whether the pump runs long and loses prime (a yield symptom). That history helps us give you a fast, accurate read. If your real issue is pressure rather than supply, a constant-pressure system or booster pump may be the answer instead.

What to Check Before You Call

A little observation helps us diagnose your Mentone well quickly and can sometimes reveal a cheaper fix. First, try to learn whether your well is finished in rock or in valley sediment, since that single fact largely determines whether fracturing can help; an old well log usually says, and we can confirm with a camera. Note whether your shortage is seasonal, worsening through the dry months and easing after winter rains, which points to a yield limit rather than equipment. Watch your pressure gauge for rapid cycling between cut-in and cut-out, a sign of a waterlogged tank rather than a formation problem. And listen for a pump that runs a long time and then loses prime, which suggests the water level is being drawn down to the intake. The more of this history you can share, the faster and more accurately we can tell you what your Mentone well actually needs.

Water Quality on the Mentone Bench

Wells along the San Bernardino mountain front sometimes carry sediment, iron, or hardness picked up from the surrounding rock and soils. While hydrofracturing is a yield treatment rather than a water-quality treatment, the development and surging we perform afterward often clears accumulated sediment from the borehole, and we can advise on filtration or softening if your water chemistry warrants it. Addressing yield and quality together, when both are issues, gives you a well that not only produces enough water but delivers water you are comfortable using.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Mentone well is in rock or in sand?

Your well log, if you have it, usually says. If not, a camera survey and the well's depth and behavior tell us a great deal. Deeper wells on the higher ground toward Mill Creek and the Crafton Hills are often in fractured rock, while shallower wells on the valley floor tend to be in alluvium. We confirm before recommending anything.

Why won't hydrofracturing help a well in alluvial material?

High-pressure water works by widening and connecting cracks in solid rock. Loose sand and gravel has no fracture network to open, and the material can collapse back, so the technique offers little benefit. That is why we assess the formation first and decline jobs where fracturing cannot reasonably succeed.

Is the process safe for my water supply?

Yes. Hydrofracturing for water wells uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals or additives, so it does not contaminate groundwater. It simply reopens the natural fractures already feeding a bedrock well.

How long does treatment take?

A qualifying Mentone well is usually treated in a single day, four to eight hours depending on how many zones we target. We reinstall the pump and run a post-treatment flow test before leaving, and water is typically available again the same evening.

What does it cost to find out if my well qualifies?

Our diagnostic assessment is $125, and we credit it toward the work if you proceed. That visit tells you whether your Mentone well is a fracturing candidate and rules out cheaper equipment fixes, so you are never paying for treatment you don't need.

If fracturing won't help, what are my options?

For a valley-floor well that has reached its limit, options include adding storage to bridge peak demand, deepening the existing well, or drilling a new one. We explain the costs and trade-offs of each so you can make an informed decision for your Mentone property.
