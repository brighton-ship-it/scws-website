What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Barstow Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in the Barstow Area

Barstow sits in the heart of San Bernardino County's Mojave Desert, where the Mojave River bends through the Middle Mojave River Valley groundwater basin on its way toward the lower desert. Surface water here is unreliable, so groundwater is the backbone of supply for the city, outlying communities like Lenwood, Hinkley, Daggett, and Yermo, and the ranches and rural properties scattered across the surrounding desert. When a well in this country loses output, hydrofracturing can be a worthwhile rehabilitation option, provided the well taps the kind of rock that responds to it.

The geology around Barstow is layered. The floodplain aquifer of young and older river deposits is the most productive zone, while a broad regional aquifer of older alluvium and fan deposits, in places partly consolidated to cemented sedimentary rock of Tertiary age, underlies a wider area and can run as much as a thousand feet thick. Notably, bedrock constrictions force groundwater toward the surface at points along the Mojave River near Barstow, evidence of the harder rock that shapes the basin. Wells screened only in loose sand and gravel are generally not hydrofracturing candidates, but wells reaching fractured bedrock at the basin margins, or finished in the consolidated Tertiary deposits, are exactly the kind of well where high-pressure treatment can reopen tight fractures and restore flow.

How Hydrofracturing Works

The method uses clean water and pressure rather than chemicals. Our crew lowers an inflatable packer into the well to seal off a section of the borehole, then injects clean water at high pressure, commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI and higher for deep, tight rock. In a well bottoming in fractured bedrock or consolidated material, that pressure pushes into the existing cracks, flushing out fine sediment and mineral scale and opening hairline fractures so they connect to a broader water-bearing network. The packers let us isolate the most promising zone and concentrate pressure where it counts rather than wasting it on unsuitable material. A typical treatment takes four to eight hours.

Two things matter most for Barstow-area owners. The process uses only clean water, with no chemicals or additives, so it does not compromise water quality. And a thorough evaluation up front establishes whether your specific well, given what it is finished in, will respond. We will tell you plainly if it is not a good candidate rather than perform a treatment that cannot help.

Steps in a Treatment

  1. Baseline flow test to record current yield and water level.
  2. Downhole camera inspection to confirm the completion and check the casing.
  3. Packer isolation and pressurization of the bedrock or consolidated zones worth targeting.
  4. Re-development and re-test to clear the well and verify any improvement.

Common Causes of Declining Yield Around Barstow

In the Middle Mojave River Valley, low-yield calls tend to trace back to a few causes:

  • Water-level decline — sustained pumping and dry years have lowered groundwater levels across the basin, leaving some wells reaching for water.
  • Fracture and screen plugging — fine sediment and mineral scale accumulate in bedrock-margin wells and around screens over time, choking flow.
  • Aging wells — older or shallower wells may no longer dependably reach the saturated zone.
  • Desert drought and demand — hot, dry conditions sharpen demand precisely when supply is thinnest.

Where the decline comes from plugged fractures or a tight bedrock completion, hydrofracturing can recover lost flow. Where a well in loose alluvium has simply been outrun by a dropping water table, deepening or a new well may be the better investment, and our assessment will tell you which case you are facing.

What to Check and Signs You May Need It

These symptoms suggest a well running low rather than an equipment problem:

  • Air sputtering from faucets during heavy use.
  • A pump that runs much longer than it used to, or short-cycles.
  • Pressure that sags when several fixtures run together.
  • Sediment or cloudiness after a big draw that settles with rest.
  • A recovery time that has lengthened noticeably over the years.

Confirm the basics first. A faulty pressure switch, a waterlogged tank, or a worn pump can all imitate a low-yield well. A diagnostic separates a genuine supply shortfall from a mechanical fault and clarifies whether hydrofracturing is the right tool.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires high-pressure equipment and a sound grasp of local geology, so it belongs with a licensed C-57 contractor, especially in a layered basin like Barstow's, where the right call depends on what the well is finished in. How Hydrofracturing Compares to Other Options

In Barstow's mixed geology, choosing the right response saves money and frustration. Hydrofracturing is the least invasive and least costly option, and for a well tapping fractured or consolidated rock it can restore strong flow for far less than the alternatives. Deepening chases the water table or new water-bearing material lower in the borehole; it can be the right move when the table has fallen below an older well, but it costs more and carries no guarantee. A new well is the biggest investment and is sometimes unavoidable, yet it should seldom be the first step when a candidate well can be rehabilitated for a fraction of the price. Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing work on my Barstow well?

It depends on the completion. Wells reaching fractured bedrock or consolidated Tertiary deposits are good candidates; wells screened entirely in loose sand and gravel generally are not. A camera inspection confirms which yours is.

Does hydrofracturing introduce chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure is used, so there are no chemicals or additives and no effect on your water quality.

Can it fix a well affected by falling groundwater levels?

If the decline is from plugged fractures or a tight bedrock completion, yes. If a dropping regional water table has simply outrun an older well in loose alluvium, deepening or a new well may be the better choice.

How long does a treatment take?

The treatment runs four to eight hours, with re-testing within 24 to 48 hours, so most wells are back in service quickly.

Could the pressure damage my casing?

No. We inspect the casing first and use packers to isolate the zone, directing pressure into the open rock below the casing rather than against the pipe.

How much can my yield improve?

For a good candidate in fractured or consolidated rock, two to five times the pre-treatment flow is common, and we document the result with before-and-after flow tests.
