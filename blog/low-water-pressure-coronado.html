How Your Well Pressure System Works

A Coronado well builds and holds pressure with four components. The pump draws water from the San Diego Formation; on a shallow coastal water table a jet pump is common, while deeper installations use a submersible. The pressure tank stores water under compressed air so the pump does not start on every faucet. The pressure switch turns the pump on at the cut-in setting (often 40 psi) and off at cut-out (often 60 psi). Finally, the plumbing, screens, and any sediment filtration deliver water to your fixtures. A healthy system holds a steady 40 to 60 psi, so that band is the first thing we check. Weak flow nearly always traces to one of these parts.

Common Causes of Low Pressure on Coronado

The peninsula's coastal setting, fine beach sands, and brackish-margin aquifer create a distinctive list of problems:

  • Saltwater intrusion and variable yield: Because fresh and salt water mingle in the San Diego Formation near the coast, Coronado wells can see real swings in quantity and quality from month to month, which shows up as inconsistent pressure.
  • Fine sand clogging: The strand's sandy deposits work into screens, intakes, and filters, restricting flow and wearing pumps.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank: A failed bladder floods the tank, causing short-cycling and erratic pressure.
  • Corrosion from salty, humid air: Coastal conditions accelerate corrosion of pumps, fittings, and galvanized pipe, narrowing the flow path.
  • Worn or undersized pump: Older Coronado wells run pumps that no longer keep up with household and irrigation demand.
  • Pressure switch trouble: Pitted contacts or a low setting leave the home running at the bottom of the pressure band.

What to Check Before You Call

These quick, safe checks help narrow the issue:

  1. One fixture or the whole house? A single weak faucet usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge, not the well. Clean the aerator first.
  2. Read the gauge. Watch the pressure gauge near the tank during use. Note it if it stays below 35 psi or swings quickly.
  3. Tap the tank. Hollow on top and solid below is normal; solid all the way up suggests a waterlogged tank.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid cycling points to the tank or switch; constant running without pressure points to the pump, a leak, or a falling water level, which on Coronado can also mean tidal or seasonal influence.
  5. Look for sand. Gritty water or sediment in aerators points to a screen or intake problem common on the strand.

Leave the 240-volt wiring, well cap, and pressurized parts to a professional, the shock and pressure hazards are serious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Coronado well pressure change from month to month?

On the coastal San Diego Formation, fresh and salt water mingle, so yield and quality can vary seasonally and even with tides. We test the water and the system to separate aquifer behavior from equipment problems.

Could sand be causing my low pressure?

Very likely on the strand. Fine beach sand clogs screens, intakes, and filters and wears pump impellers. Proper sediment filtration and screen service usually restore flow.

Is my Coronado well at risk of saltwater intrusion?

Coastal wells can be. If your water turns brackish or salty along with low pressure, we test for intrusion and recommend solutions, which may include filtration changes or pump adjustments.

Do I need a permit for well work in Coronado?

Pump and tank swaps generally do not, but new wells and certain electrical work in San Diego County do. As a licensed C-57 contractor we handle the paperwork.

Salt and humidity accelerate corrosion of pumps, fittings, and steel pipe. We install corrosion-resistant components and recommend maintenance intervals suited to Coronado's coastal environment.

Maintenance Tips for Coronado Well Owners

Coronado's salty, humid coastal setting is hard on well equipment, so steady maintenance pays off. These habits help protect your pressure between professional visits:

  • Inspect for corrosion on the pump, fittings, and exposed pipe at least yearly, since coastal air accelerates rust.
  • Service sediment filters often to clear the fine beach sand that clogs screens and intakes.
  • Test the water periodically for salinity so you can catch any saltwater intrusion early.
  • Check the tank air charge twice a year to keep pressure steady and prevent short-cycling.
  • Use corrosion-resistant components when replacing parts so they last longer in the marine environment.
