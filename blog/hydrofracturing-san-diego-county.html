What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Diego County Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many San Diego County wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing Across San Diego County's Backcountry

San Diego County is one of the best places in California to own a well that responds to hydrofracturing (also spelled hydrofracking) — and also a place where it is important to know which half of the county you are in. The coastal cities and the deep alluvial valleys run on imported and municipal water or on sediment-fed wells where fracturing has little to offer. But head inland and uphill into the granitic backcountry — Ramona, Julian, Valley Center, Alpine, Jamul, Boulevard, and the rural canyons in between — and you reach a private-well belt that is dominated by fractured crystalline rock. That is exactly the geology hydrofracturing was made for. This county-wide overview explains where the technique excels, where it does not, and how to tell which describes your property.

The County's Split Personality

The backbone of inland San Diego County is the Peninsular Ranges batholith — a vast body of granite and related crystalline rock. Backcountry wells do not draw from a continuous water table the way a sand aquifer does; they produce from networks of fractures, joints, and weathered seams within that rock. Where a borehole intersects productive fractures, the well flows; where it misses them, it disappoints. Yields vary dramatically from one parcel to the next, and valley pockets like the Ramona Basin behave differently again, with higher yields in the sediment-filled lowlands and far more variable production on the surrounding hillsides.

This is the crucial honesty point. In the fractured-granite backcountry, hydrofracturing is a strong fit because there is a real fracture network to clean and extend. Along the coast and in the deep alluvial valley fill — and anywhere on municipal water — it is a weak fit or no fit at all, because there are either no fractures to open or no well to treat. Knowing your setting is the whole game.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates an existing drilled well rather than creating a new one. Over years of pumping, the fractures feeding a granite well silt up with fine sediment, mineral scale, and bacterial buildup, and the well recovers more slowly. To reopen those pathways, we lower an inflatable packer to isolate a target interval in the cased borehole and inject clean water at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes the existing fractures and, where the rock cooperates, extends them to connect with adjacent water-bearing seams. We then move the packer to treat additional zones down the hole. Only water is used — no chemicals, gels, or proppants. A residential job typically runs four to eight hours, followed by a flow test.

Where Hydrofracturing Shines in the County

  • Ramona. Home to one of the highest concentrations of domestic wells in the county. Valley wells may produce at 200-300 feet, while hillside wells tap variable fractured rock and are prime rehabilitation candidates when yields fall.
  • Julian and the mountain communities. Deep water tables and low natural yields — wells often exceed 400 feet and produce only a few gallons per minute — make every fracture count, and fracturing can meaningfully improve a marginal well.
  • Valley Center, Alpine, Jamul, and Boulevard. Rural residential, agricultural, and equestrian properties on granite, where declining or disappointing wells respond well to fracture cleaning and extension.
  • New backcountry wells that came in low. A freshly drilled granite well that missed the best fractures can sometimes be improved with a targeted treatment before anyone considers redrilling.

Where It Is a Weak Fit

We are equally clear about the other side of the county. Coastal and urban communities — and the suburban neighborhoods of inland San Diego — are overwhelmingly on municipal water, where any pressure or supply problem is a plumbing or meter issue, not a well issue. Wells completed in deep alluvial valley fill produce from loose sediment with no fracture network, so injected water simply dissipates without lasting benefit. For those properties, the productive answers are pump and pressure repairs, water treatment, deepening, or a new well — not hydrofracturing.

What to Check Before You Decide

  1. Find your well completion report. California well logs record depth and materials. Hard granite at the production zone points toward a fracturing candidate; sand and gravel to total depth does not.
  2. Identify your water source. If you are on a water agency or district, hydrofracturing is irrelevant; the fix is in your plumbing.
  3. Read the symptom pattern. Short-cycling and rapid tank drain-down suggest equipment; gradual multi-year decline suggests fracture clogging or a falling table.
  4. Check water quality. Staining or sediment calls for treatment, which fracturing does not provide.
  5. Compare with neighbors. Backcountry yields vary so much parcel-to-parcel that a neighbor's strong well does not guarantee yours, but a shared decline can flag a regional change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my San Diego County well a good hydrofracturing candidate?

If it is a backcountry well completed in fractured granite — common in Ramona, Julian, Valley Center, Alpine, Jamul, and Boulevard — it likely is. If it draws from deep alluvial valley fill, or if you are on municipal water, it is not. Your well log and a video inspection make the answer clear.

How much improvement can I expect?

For suitable granite wells, two to five times the pre-treatment flow is common, though the fractured backcountry varies widely. We run a before-and-after flow test so you see the measured result.

Why are backcountry wells so unpredictable?

Because they depend on intersecting productive fractures in granite. One property can produce excellent water at 300 feet while a neighbor needs 600 feet or more. Fracturing helps where a real but clogged fracture network exists.

Will hydrofracturing fix hard or stained water?

No. Hardness, iron, and manganese are treatment issues. Fracturing changes how much water the formation yields, not its quality.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Usually far cheaper — roughly $3,000-$8,000 versus $18,000-$42,000 for a new well in the county's hard rock — which is why we evaluate rehabilitation first whenever the geology supports it.

Does it use chemicals?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure and shares nothing but a name with oil-and-gas fracking.
