Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Scripps Ranch well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

What Actually Applies in an Urban Community Like This

In a city-water neighborhood, the well itself is rarely the issue. What does come up are pressure and irrigation challenges on larger lots, slopes, and common-area landscaping. A booster or constant-pressure system takes municipal water and raises pressure to reach distant or elevated irrigation zones, hillside plantings, or large gardens that the street pressure alone cannot serve well. Pressure tanks, pumps, and controls on these booster systems wear out and need service just like any well system does. For homeowners with extensive landscaping or a few backyard fruit trees, the same logic that governs a grove applies in miniature: deliver enough water, at adequate pressure, during the hot afternoon hours when plants need it most.

If you have inherited or are buying a rural property elsewhere in the county that does have a well, we are glad to help you understand it — its depth, yield, water quality, and what it will cost to keep running. That kind of honest, no-pressure assessment is part of what we do.

How a Booster and Irrigation System Works

It helps to understand the equipment, because the same parts show up whether water comes from a city main or a private well. A booster pump takes incoming water and raises its pressure; a pressure tank stores a cushion of that pressurized water so the pump does not restart every time a valve opens; and a pressure switch or a constant-pressure controller decides when and how hard the pump runs. On a constant-pressure system, a variable-speed drive holds steady pressure even as demand changes across irrigation zones, which is ideal for large or sloped landscapes. When any one of these components fails, the symptoms look similar to a failing well system: pressure that surges and sags, a pump that cycles rapidly, or zones that simply do not get enough water. Diagnosing which part is at fault, rather than replacing everything, is where experience pays off and where we focus.

For owners who do have a real well on outlying or inherited property, the very same logic scales up. A well-fed system simply adds the well and often a larger storage tank ahead of the pressure equipment. Understanding both ends of that spectrum lets us give Scripps Ranch property owners advice that fits whatever water source they are actually dealing with, instead of forcing every problem into a one-size-fits-all sales pitch.

Common Scenarios We See Around Scripps Ranch

  • Weak irrigation pressure on slopes. Elevated or distant zones often need a booster system to get adequate coverage.
  • Failing booster pumps or tanks. When a pressure-boosting system's pump or tank fails, irrigation coverage drops off.
  • Large-lot and HOA landscaping demands. Common areas and big lots can outstrip simple street-pressure irrigation.
  • Questions about a rural well elsewhere. Buyers and heirs of backcountry property often need a straight evaluation of an unfamiliar well.
  • Old, unused wells on legacy parcels. Some older properties retain a long-abandoned well that should be tested or properly decommissioned.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  • If irrigation pressure is weak, note whether it is one zone or the whole system; a single weak zone often points to a filter, valve, or emitter issue rather than the pump.
  • On a booster system, check the pressure gauge and listen for rapid pump cycling, which suggests a waterlogged tank or failing switch.
  • Confirm breakers and controls before assuming a pump failure.
  • For a rural well you are evaluating, gather any past pump, water-test, or drilling records you can find.

Keeping a Large Landscape Watered Through Summer

Scripps Ranch summers are hot and dry, and big lots and common areas feel it. A few habits keep irrigation reliable: inspect booster pumps and pressure tanks in spring before peak demand, clean filters and check emitters for clogs, and confirm that elevated zones are getting full coverage. If pressure has been creeping down, address it before the hottest months rather than during them. For homeowners nurturing a few fruit trees, deep, consistent watering during fruit development beats sporadic heavy soakings.
