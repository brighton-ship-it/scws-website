What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Victorville Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Victorville: A Realistic Look

Victorville anchors the Victor Valley in California's High Desert, straddling the Mojave River in San Bernardino County alongside Apple Valley, Hesperia, and Adelanto. At roughly 2,700 feet of elevation on the high Mojave plain, Victorville's water story is fundamentally different from the granite backcountry where hydrofracturing shines. The valley sits on a deep alluvial basin, hundreds of feet of sand, gravel, and clay washed down and deposited over geologic time, with granitic basement rock far below. That geology means we have to be candid: for most Victorville wells, hydrofracturing is a weak-to-moderate fit at best, and other approaches usually serve owners better.

Most of Victorville proper is on municipal water through Liberty Utilities and the Victorville Water District, drawing from the Mojave River basin managed by the Mojave Water Agency. Private wells are more common on the rural fringes and outlying parcels. If you own one and it is underproducing, the honest path is to understand why before reaching for any single fix, because in alluvial desert ground the cause and the cure are often different from what they would be in fractured rock.

Why Hydrofracturing Is Limited Here

Hydrofracturing works by opening and cleaning cracks in solid rock. It injects clean water at high pressure to extend a well's natural fracture network. In Victorville, most domestic and agricultural wells draw from the floodplain and regional aquifers, layers of unconsolidated sand and gravel, not from fractured bedrock. There are no significant rock fractures to open in that material, so high-pressure treatment has little to act on. The exception is a well drilled deep enough to be collared in the granitic basement, which is uncommon for typical wells but not impossible on parcels closer to the surrounding hills. For those rare rock wells, hydrofracturing could help, and we would evaluate the well log to know.

Understanding the Mojave River Basin Beneath You

The water under Victorville moves through one of the most studied groundwater systems in Southern California. Geologists describe two main layers: a shallower floodplain aquifer of young river deposits along the Mojave River corridor, which historically yields a large share of the basin's water, and a thicker regional aquifer of older alluvial and fan deposits that can run a thousand feet deep. Properties near the river corridor sometimes find water at relatively shallow depths, while wells out on the benches and mesas may have to reach four hundred to six hundred feet or more. Across that whole range, the water lives in the spaces between sand and gravel grains rather than in cracks in rock. That is the crux of why hydrofracturing, a rock technique, has limited reach in Victorville. It also explains why two neighbors can have very different wells depending on whether they sit over the floodplain or the regional aquifer, and why a generic recommendation is risky in this valley. Reading your specific well against the basin is what produces a recommendation you can trust.

What Usually Causes Low Yield in Victor Valley Wells

  • A dropping regional water table. The Mojave basin has faced long-term overdraft, and water levels in parts of the valley have declined over decades, leaving older or shallower wells above the productive zone.
  • Well screen incrustation and plugging. Desert groundwater is often mineral-rich, and scale plus fine sand can clog the screen, throttling a well that the aquifer could still supply.
  • Sand pumping. Alluvial wells frequently pull sand, which wears pumps and signals a screen or gravel-pack problem.
  • Pump set too high. As levels drop, a pump that was once well below water can end up near the surface of the water column, cycling and starving.

Better Options for Most Victorville Wells

Because the formation is alluvial, the high-value services here are usually not hydrofracturing. They are well screen rehabilitation to clear incrustation, deepening or a new well screened into the current productive zone, lowering or upsizing the pump to match a declined water table, and sediment filtration to handle sand. We mention this plainly because steering a desert owner toward a fracturing job that the ground will not reward would not be honest. The right move in Victor Valley is matching the solution to an alluvial aquifer, which is a different playbook than the granite communities.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Locate your well completion report. Depth and screen details tell us whether you are in alluvium, as most are, or rock.
  • Check for sand in the water or at fixtures, a strong sign of a screen or gravel-pack issue rather than a fracturing candidate.
  • Note whether the pump short-cycles, which can mean a dropped water level or an equipment problem.
  • Review whether neighbors have deepened wells recently, a clue that the local water table has fallen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing help my Victorville well?

For most Victorville wells, no, because they draw from alluvial sand and gravel rather than fractured rock. Hydrofracturing only benefits rock wells, which are uncommon here. We check your well log before recommending it.

My well is producing less water. What is the likely cause?

In the High Desert it is usually a dropping water table, a clogged or incrusted screen, or a pump set too high, rather than anything fracturing would fix.

What should I do about sand in my water?

Sand points to a screen or gravel-pack problem in an alluvial well. Screen rehabilitation and sediment filtration are the right responses, not high-pressure fracturing.

Is a new well my only option if mine is failing?

Not necessarily. Screen cleaning, lowering the pump, or deepening the existing well can restore production at lower cost. We assess before recommending replacement.

Why is desert well work different from mountain well work?

Mountain wells produce from rock fractures, which respond to hydrofracturing. Desert wells produce from porous sediment, so the effective fixes are screen, pump, and depth solutions.
