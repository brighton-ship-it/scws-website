Low Water Pressure on a San Marcos Property

San Marcos sits in the rolling inland hills of North County San Diego County, tucked between Escondido to the east and Vista and Carlsbad to the west, with the Twin Oaks Valley and Discovery Hills neighborhoods climbing the slopes around Cal State San Marcos. While most of the city is on municipal water through Vallecitos Water District, a number of older properties on the outskirts — in places like Twin Oaks Valley, Coronado Hills, and the agricultural pockets near the San Marcos foothills — still draw from private wells drilled into the region's decomposed granite. If your shower has slowed to a trickle or your irrigation lines no longer reach the far end of the yard, low water pressure is usually a sign that one specific component in your well system has drifted out of spec, not that the whole system is failing.

How Your Well Pressure System Actually Works

A private well doesn't push water straight from the ground to your faucet. A submersible pump sitting deep in the borehole lifts water up to a pressure tank, which stores a cushion of water under air pressure. A pressure switch watches that tank and tells the pump when to turn on and off — most homes around San Marcos run a 40/60 setting, meaning the pump kicks on at 40 PSI and shuts off at 60 PSI. When everything is healthy, your taps see steady pressure in that range. When any link in that chain weakens — a waterlogged tank, a mis-set switch, a tiring pump, or a partially blocked line — the pressure at the fixture drops, and that is exactly what you feel as “low water pressure.”

In the granite terrain common to inland San Diego County, wells are often drilled anywhere from 200 to 600 feet to reach a reliable fracture zone, and water travels a long vertical distance before it ever reaches your pressure tank. That depth means small inefficiencies — a slightly weak pump or a restriction in the drop pipe — show up faster as pressure loss than they would on a shallow well.

Common Local Causes of Low Pressure in San Marcos

After three decades of well work across North County, these are the culprits we find most often on San Marcos systems:

  • Waterlogged pressure tank. When the air bladder inside the tank fails, the tank fills entirely with water and loses its cushion. The pump then short-cycles — switching on and off rapidly — and pressure surges and sags instead of holding steady.
  • Mineral and scale buildup. San Diego County groundwater tends to be hard and mineral-rich. Over years, scale narrows pipes, clogs screens, and coats pump components, slowly choking flow.
  • Drifting or failing pressure switch. The switch contacts pit and corrode over time, so the pump cuts off early at, say, 48 PSI instead of 60 — leaving you with chronically weak pressure you may not even notice creeping in.
  • A pump nearing the end of its life. Submersible pumps typically last 10–15 years. As impellers wear, the pump still runs but can no longer build full pressure, especially against the lift of a deeper San Marcos well.
  • Sediment and iron in the lines. Fine granite sediment and iron staining are common locally and can clog filters, aerator screens, and even the pump intake.
  • Seasonal water-table drop. During Southern California's dry stretches, the static water level in a well can fall, forcing the pump to work harder and sometimes draw air, which shows up as sputtering, pressure-robbing flow.

What to Check Before You Call

A few simple observations will help you — and us — pinpoint the problem faster:

  1. Is it every fixture or just one? If only one faucet or showerhead is weak, the issue is almost certainly a clogged aerator or fixture, not the well. Whole-house low pressure points to the well system.
  2. Look at your pressure gauge. Most systems have a small gauge near the pressure tank. If it never climbs near 60 PSI, or bounces wildly, that tells us a lot.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking of the pressure switch or a pump that cycles every few seconds usually means a waterlogged tank.
  4. Tap the pressure tank. A healthy tank rings hollow near the top and sounds solid near the bottom. If it sounds full of water all the way up, the bladder has likely failed.
  5. Note any color or smell. Rusty water, sediment, or a sulfur odor points toward water-quality issues that may also be restricting flow.

What you should not do is start adjusting the pressure switch yourself — the contacts carry live 240-volt current, and a wrong move can damage the pump or injure you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my San Marcos well have good pressure in the morning but weak pressure later in the day?

This pattern usually points to a well that is recovering slowly. Overnight the borehole refills, so morning pressure is strong; by afternoon heavy use has drawn the water level down faster than the granite fracture can replenish it. It can signal a declining water table, a tiring pump, or a well that would benefit from hydrofracturing. A yield test tells us which.

Could hard water really be the reason my pressure dropped?

Yes. San Diego County groundwater is mineral-heavy, and over years scale builds inside pipes, valves, and pump components, slowly narrowing the passages water flows through. The drop is gradual, so many homeowners don't notice until pressure is clearly weak. Filtration or a softener protects the whole system going forward.

Is low pressure always the pump's fault?

Not at all — in fact, the pump is rarely the first thing we replace. A waterlogged pressure tank or a worn pressure switch causes low pressure far more often and costs a fraction of a pump. That's why we diagnose before we recommend anything.

How long does a pressure tank or switch repair take?

A pressure switch swap is typically under an hour. A tank replacement usually takes a couple of hours. Both are same-day jobs in nearly every case, so you are not left without water overnight.

Do you handle the electrical and the wellhead, or just the plumbing?

We handle the complete well system — pump, wiring, pressure tank, switch, and wellhead — because we are a licensed C-57 well contractor, not just a plumber. That means one company solves the whole problem.
