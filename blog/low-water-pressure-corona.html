How a Well Pressure System Works

Your Corona well builds pressure through four parts working together. The pump lifts water from the aquifer, typically a submersible unit in the deeper Temescal Valley wells. The pressure tank stores water under compressed air so the pump cycles less often. The pressure switch starts the pump at the cut-in setting (often 40 psi) and stops it at cut-out (often 60 psi). The supply lines, screens, and any sediment filtration deliver the water to your taps. Homes are designed for a steady 40 to 60 psi, so the first thing we measure is whether your system holds that band. Weak pressure almost always points to one of these components drifting out of spec or wearing out.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Corona Wells

Corona's alluvial Temescal aquifer, its high-dissolved-solids groundwater, and a mix of rural and aging infrastructure produce a familiar set of culprits:

  • Mineral scaling: Temescal Subbasin water is a calcium-sodium bicarbonate type with relatively high total dissolved solids, so scale builds inside pumps, tanks, and pipes, choking flow.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank: A failed air bladder floods the tank, causing short-cycling and pressure that bounces between weak and strong.
  • Worn or undersized pump: Older Corona wells run pumps that no longer meet modern household and irrigation demand, especially in summer.
  • Pressure switch trouble: Pitted contacts or a low setting leave the house running at the bottom of the pressure band.
  • Corroded or clogged pipes: Galvanized lines in older homes narrow with rust and sediment, restricting flow before the well is to blame.
  • Seasonal demand and recharge swings: Heavy summer irrigation and dry-year recharge limits can lower local water levels and reduce yield.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks help narrow things down:

  1. One fixture or the whole house? A single weak faucet usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge. Clean the aerator first before suspecting the well.
  2. Read the gauge. Watch the pressure gauge near the tank during use. If it stays below 35 psi or swings fast, note it.
  3. Tap the tank. Hollow on top and solid below is normal; solid throughout suggests waterlogging.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid on-off cycling points to the tank or switch; constant running without pressure points to the pump, a leak, or a low water level.
  5. Check for leaks. A meter that turns with everything off or a jump in your bill suggests a hidden leak.

Leave the 240-volt wiring, well cap, and pressurized parts to a trained technician, the hazards are real.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Corona well pressure drop in summer?

Heavy irrigation demand and reduced dry-season recharge in the Temescal Subbasin can lower local water levels just as your usage peaks. We measure pressure under load and test yield to find the true cause.

Is Corona's hard water causing scale in my system?

Likely yes. Temescal groundwater carries high dissolved solids and is calcium-rich, so scale accumulates in pumps and pipes. Treatment such as a softener and periodic descaling restore and protect flow.

How do I tell whether it is the pump or the tank?

Rapid pump cycling usually means the tank or switch; a pump that runs nonstop without building pressure usually means the pump, a leak, or a low water level. A gauge and tank test confirms it.

Do I need a permit for well work in Riverside County?

Pump and tank replacements usually do not, but new wells, deepening, and decommissioning do. We handle any required permits as a licensed C-57 contractor.

Could low pressure mean my Corona well is low-yield?

It can. A pump running without building pressure can signal a falling water level or low yield. We perform a yield test and recommend the right fix, from pump adjustment to hydrofracturing.

Maintenance Tips for Corona Well Owners

Proactive care keeps Temescal Valley wells flowing strong despite hard water and hot, dry summers. Build these simple steps into your routine between professional visits:

  • Schedule an annual inspection to check pump performance, tank charge, and switch settings before summer demand peaks.
  • Treat for scale with a softener and descale fixtures regularly, since Temescal water is mineral-rich.
  • Check the pressure tank air charge twice a year to prevent short-cycling and pressure swings.
  • Spread out irrigation so heavy summer watering does not overwhelm your pump and tank at once.
  • Monitor for leaks by watching your meter and bill, and call us at the first sign of a hidden loss.
