Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

What Citrus Trees Demand From a Well

Citrus irrigates on a long calendar and a heavy clock. In the warm, dry East Valley climate, a mature grove typically needs about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, the bulk of it delivered between late spring and early fall when evapotranspiration runs highest. For a commercial-scale block, meeting that figure means a well and pump that can sustain a dependable gallons-per-minute output through the hottest months without pulling the water level down faster than the aquifer recovers.

The Freeze Night That Sizes the System

Routine summer irrigation is rarely what determines well capacity — the deciding factor is frost protection. When cold air pools in the San Bernardino Valley on a clear winter night, growers may run sprinklers for hours to defend the crop, and water-based frost protection can call for 50 or more GPM per acre for the length of the freeze. A well built only for ordinary watering will come up short at the worst possible moment. We engineer citrus systems for that hardest night, not the comfortable average day.

System Design: Well Yield, Pumps, and Storage

Confirming Sustainable Yield

Everything begins with how much water the aquifer will reliably surrender, measured as sustainable GPM rather than a one-time figure on drilling day. After yield testing, we select equipment to match: a high-capacity submersible pump for deeper wells, or a vertical lineshaft turbine pump where high volume from a shallower lift is the smarter fit. A pump matched to its well runs efficiently and lasts; a mismatched one cycles, overheats, and fails early.

Why Storage Belongs in the Plan

Most wells cannot instantaneously meet a frost night or a peak-summer afternoon. Storage bridges that gap. We design systems that fill a lined pond or large tank steadily during off-peak hours, then let the grove draw on that reserve during short, intense demand. The result is a smaller, efficient well that can run continuously instead of an oversized well sized for a few extreme hours a year — plus a built-in buffer for a freeze event or an unexpected pump failure.

Water Quality Matters as Much as Quantity

Citrus ranks among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so the chemistry of your well water can make or break a grove. East Valley groundwater can carry elevated dissolved minerals, and we test for the constituents that injure citrus before recommending any system:

  • Salinity (EC): High total salinity limits water uptake and depresses yield. Citrus tolerates less salt than most crops, so even moderate EC deserves a leaching strategy.
  • Chloride: Citrus draws chloride into its leaves, where it produces the familiar leaf-tip and margin burn. Rootstock selection and irrigation method strongly influence how much a grove can withstand.
  • Boron: A needed micronutrient at trace levels, boron turns toxic to citrus above roughly 0.5 to 1 ppm. Its early symptoms imitate heat or drought stress, so only a water test reliably reveals a boron problem.

When test results run high, we can recommend blending, leaching fractions, rootstock-aware scheduling, or treatment so the water meant to nourish your trees is not slowly damaging them.

Groundwater, Drought, and the San Bernardino Basin

Loma Linda overlies groundwater within the San Bernardino Valley basin system, which is managed under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA). For growers, that framework makes efficient irrigation and disciplined pumping a long-term necessity rather than an afterthought. Years of drought have pressured water levels across the East Valley, and a well that performed comfortably ten years ago may now need a lowered pump setting, deepening, or rehabilitation to keep producing. We help Loma Linda growers understand their well's current standing and plan proactively, so they are not caught with a struggling well in the middle of the irrigation season. Periodic water-level readings give early warning of a falling table, letting a grower schedule a deepening or rehabilitation during the cooler months rather than in the heat of summer when every hour of downtime stresses the trees.

Common Issues, Maintenance, and the Right Time to Call

Problems We Encounter Most

  • Gradually declining yield and lengthening run times as the table drops in summer
  • Mineral scale and biofouling clogging drip emitters and micro-sprinklers
  • Pump motor failures driven by sediment, short-cycling, or power fluctuations
  • Pressure tank and control faults that disrupt irrigation scheduling
  • Deteriorating casing or screen in older wells silently cutting capacity

Maintenance Is the Cheaper Path

A scheduled inspection costs a fraction of an emergency repair during a heat wave or freeze. We recommend regular pump and well checks, water-level and flow testing, water-quality retesting every year or two, and consistent filtration upkeep to protect emitters. When yield slips, hydrofracturing or well rehabilitation often restores production for far less than a new well.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a Loma Linda citrus grove need annually?

A mature East Valley grove generally uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year, with demand peaking from late spring through early fall. Frost-protection nights can briefly require 50 or more GPM per acre on top of normal irrigation.

My citrus shows leaf burn even with regular watering - what's wrong?

Leaf-tip and margin burn typically signals chloride or salinity stress rather than drought. Because citrus is salt-sensitive, we recommend testing your well for EC, chloride, and boron to pinpoint the cause before it cuts your yield.

Should I add storage to my grove's water system?

Often it pays off. Storage lets you run a smaller, efficient well steadily and draw on a reserve during summer peaks or a freeze event. It is usually more economical than an oversized well and provides backup if a pump fails.

What does a new agricultural well cost in Loma Linda?

A turnkey agricultural well generally runs $25,000 to $60,000 or more depending on depth, casing, and capacity. We provide a clear written estimate after evaluating your site and water needs.

Can a low-yielding well be restored without a full replacement?

Usually, yes. Hydrofracturing, rehabilitation, or adjusting the pump setting can revive many older wells for $3,000 to $8,000 - well below the cost of drilling new. Our $125 diagnostic visit, credited toward the work, determines the best approach.

Is groundwater regulated in Loma Linda?

Yes. Loma Linda overlies San Bernardino Valley basin groundwater managed under SGMA, which promotes monitoring and efficient use. We help growers pump sustainably and prepare for changing water-table conditions.
