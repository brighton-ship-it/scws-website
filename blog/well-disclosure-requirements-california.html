Well Disclosure Requirements in California

Selling a California home with a private well? Understanding your legal disclosure obligations is crucial for a smooth transaction—and protecting yourself from future liability. California law requires sellers to disclose known material facts about their property, and your well is no exception. This comprehensive guide explains exactly what you must disclose, the forms involved, and how to handle common disclosure situations.

📋 Key Disclosure Principle

California requires disclosure of known material facts that could affect a buyer's decision. For wells, this means disclosing what you know—but you're not required to investigate or test if you haven't already done so.

Understanding California's Disclosure Law

California Civil Code Section 1102 et seq. requires residential property sellers to disclose known material defects and conditions. The law applies to all aspects of the property, including:

  • Water source and supply systems
  • Known defects or malfunctions
  • History of problems or repairs
  • Shared systems with neighbors
  • Regulatory or permit issues

The key word is "known." You must disclose what you actually know—you're not required to investigate, test, or discover problems you're unaware of. However, you cannot intentionally avoid learning about problems to avoid disclosure.

Required Disclosure Forms

Several California disclosure forms address well-related issues:

Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

The TDS is required for most residential sales in California. Well-related sections include:

TDS Section II.A - Water Supply:

  • Item 7: Asks about water supply source. You must indicate if the property has a private well vs. public/municipal water.
  • Item 7 (continued): Asks if you're aware of any problems with the water supply system.

TDS Section II.C - General Conditions:

  • Asks about any significant defects or malfunctions in major systems.
  • Shared well arrangements should be noted here.

Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)

While not legally required, the SPQ is commonly used in California transactions and asks more detailed questions:

  • Water source questions: Private well, community well, or public water
  • Well age: If known
  • Water quality issues: Any known problems with taste, odor, or contamination
  • Water quantity issues: Low flow, running out of water
  • Treatment systems: Softeners, filters, UV systems
  • Repairs: History of well service or repairs
  • Shared wells: Detailed questions about shared arrangements

Additional Local Disclosures

Some counties and cities have additional disclosure requirements:

  • San Diego County: May ask about groundwater management areas
  • Riverside County: May require septic and well relationship disclosure
  • Agricultural areas: May have additional water rights disclosures

What Specifically Must Be Disclosed

Here's a detailed breakdown of well-related items requiring disclosure if you're aware of them:

Well location Where on property (if known) "Location unknown" is acceptable
Well depth If documented "Depth unknown" is acceptable
Well age Year drilled if known "Age unknown" is acceptable

Shared Well Disclosure Requirements

Shared wells require additional detailed disclosures:

⚠️ Shared Well Documentation Is Critical

Shared wells can create significant complications in real estate transactions. FHA and VA loans have specific requirements for shared well agreements, and some conventional lenders won't finance properties with poorly documented shared wells.

Required Shared Well Disclosures

  • Existence of sharing: That the well serves multiple properties
  • Number of users: How many properties share the well
  • Written agreement: Whether a formal shared well agreement exists (provide copy)
  • Cost sharing: How maintenance and repair costs are divided
  • Access: Easements or rights to access the well
  • Disputes: Any current or past disputes with other users
  • Well location: Which property the well is physically located on

📄 Elements of a Good Shared Well Agreement

Property Identification

Clear description of all properties sharing the well

Cost Division

How maintenance, repairs, and replacement costs are split

Access Easements

Legal right to access well for maintenance

Dispute Resolution

Process for resolving disagreements

Transfer Provisions

How agreement transfers with property sale

Water Allocation

If applicable, how water is divided during shortages

What You Don't Have to Disclose

Understanding what you're not required to disclose is equally important:

  • Information you don't know: You're not required to investigate or test
  • Speculation: You don't need to guess about potential problems
  • Publicly available information: County well records, geological reports
  • Resolved issues: Problems that have been properly fixed don't require emphasis (but should still be mentioned)

💡 The "Should Have Known" Standard

While you're only required to disclose what you actually know, California courts have held sellers liable for things they "should have known" through reasonable observation. If your well makes strange noises or your water has an obvious smell, claiming ignorance likely won't protect you.

Consequences of Non-Disclosure

Failing to disclose known well problems can have serious consequences:

🔴 Contract Rescission

Buyer may be able to cancel the sale entirely

💰 Damages

Liability for repair costs discovered after sale

⚖️ Fraud Claims

Intentional concealment can lead to fraud lawsuits

📋 Punitive Damages

In egregious cases, additional penalty damages

California case law includes numerous examples of sellers held liable for well-related non-disclosures:

  • Seller who knew well ran dry seasonally but didn't disclose
  • Seller who had water tested, knew of contamination, but didn't share results
  • Seller who knew septic was too close to well but remained silent
  • Seller who concealed history of pump failures

Best Practices for Well Disclosure

✅ Disclosure Best Practices

Be Honest and Complete

If in doubt, disclose. Over-disclosure is safer than under-disclosure.

Document Everything

Keep records of all well service, tests, and repairs for accurate disclosure.

Use Clear Language

Describe issues in plain terms. Avoid technical jargon that could obscure problems.

Include Solutions

When disclosing problems, also disclose how they've been addressed.

Don't Minimize

Present facts objectively. Let buyers assess significance for themselves.

Provide Documentation

Attach test results, service records, and agreements to disclosures.

Sample Disclosure Language

Here are examples of proper disclosure language for common situations:

Past Bacteria Issue (Resolved)

"In March 2024, water tested positive for coliform bacteria. Well was shock chlorinated by [Company Name]. Follow-up test in April 2024 showed no bacteria detected. Test results attached."

Low Yield Well

"Well produces approximately 2-3 GPM. A 1,500-gallon storage tank was installed in 2022 to ensure adequate water supply. We have not experienced water shortages since tank installation."

Hard Water

"Water has high mineral content (hardness). Water softener installed 2020; requires salt replacement monthly. Softener lease information attached."

Shared Well

"Property shares well with [neighboring property address]. Well is located on neighboring property. Shared well agreement dated [date] attached. Costs split 50/50. No current disputes. Contact information for other well user provided separately."

Unknown Permit Status

"Well predates current permit requirements. We are unable to locate original drilling permit or well log. Well has functioned continuously during our ownership (since 2015) without issues."

Frequently Asked Questions

What must I disclose about my well when selling in California?

California requires disclosure of all known material facts about your well, including: that the property uses a private well (not municipal water), any known water quality issues, well equipment problems you're aware of, history of repairs or maintenance, shared well arrangements, permit status if known, and any past test results showing issues.

Do I have to test my well water before selling in California?

California law does not require sellers to test well water before selling. However, you must disclose any test results you have and any water quality issues you know about. Many buyers and lenders require testing as part of the transaction, but this is typically the buyer's expense unless negotiated otherwise.

What forms must I complete for well disclosure in California?

The primary form is the Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS), which asks about water source and known issues. Many transactions also use the Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ) for additional detail. If you have a shared well, you should also disclose the shared well agreement. Local disclosure forms may have additional questions.

What happens if I fail to disclose a known well problem?

Failure to disclose known material defects can expose you to legal liability. The buyer could potentially rescind the sale, seek damages for repair costs, or sue for fraud. California courts have consistently held sellers liable for failing to disclose known property defects, including well problems.

Do I need to disclose a well that's no longer in use?

Yes, you should disclose abandoned or unused wells on the property. Improperly abandoned wells can be environmental hazards and may affect property development options. If the well was properly destroyed according to county requirements, include that documentation.

What must I disclose about shared well arrangements?

For shared wells, you must disclose the existence of the sharing arrangement, provide the shared well agreement if one exists, explain how maintenance costs are divided, disclose any disputes or issues with other well users, and provide information about easements or access rights. FHA/VA loans have specific requirements for shared well documentation.

Is the well permit status a required disclosure?

If you know the well's permit status, you should disclose it. An unpermitted well isn't necessarily illegal if it predates permit requirements, but buyers and lenders should know. If you're uncertain about permit status, you can state that in the disclosure.

Do I need to disclose past well repairs?

Yes, significant well repairs should be disclosed, especially if they indicate recurring problems. This includes pump replacements, chlorination treatments for bacteria, repairs to well casing, or any work to address water quality or quantity issues. Keep records of all well service for disclosure purposes.
