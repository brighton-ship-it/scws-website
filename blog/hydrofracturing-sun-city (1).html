What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Sun City Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Wells in Sun City and Menifee

Sun City is an established community within the city of Menifee, in Riverside County — the original 1960s retirement development that gave the area its name, now surrounded by the fast-growing neighborhoods of Menifee, Romoland, and Quail Valley along the Highway 74 and I-215 corridors. The terrain here is a mix: a broad valley floor of alluvial and sedimentary deposits, studded with the rounded granitic knolls and rocky outcrops that are the surface expression of the Peninsular Ranges Batholith. That split geology is the key to understanding whether hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) is the right answer for a low-yield well on your particular parcel.

The honest reality is that Sun City sits in the Menifee/Perris area of the San Jacinto Groundwater Basin, and many valley-floor wells here draw from alluvial and sedimentary formations rather than hard fractured rock. Hydrofracturing is a weaker fit for those wells. But properties on or near the granitic hills — and there are many in and around Sun City — can be good candidates. We assess which situation applies before recommending anything, and we will tell you straight if fracturing is not your best option.

Where Hydrofracturing Helps — and Where It Does Not

Hydrofracturing works by using high-pressure clean water to reopen and extend natural fractures in hard bedrock, restoring the pathways groundwater uses to reach a well. That makes it highly effective for wells finished in granite or fractured crystalline rock — the kind found on the rocky knolls around Sun City.

It is far less useful for wells finished in loose alluvium, sand, gravel, or sedimentary layers, because there are no rock fractures to reopen; water moves through the pore space between grains instead. Menifee-area wells commonly reach productive zones at depths of about 200 to 400 feet in these mixed alluvial and sedimentary formations. If that describes your well, the better remedies are usually deepening the existing well, installing or replacing a well screen, redeveloping the borehole to clear sediment, or, when a well is truly spent, drilling a new well. We would rather point you to the method that actually solves your problem than fracture a formation that will not respond.

Why Geology Matters So Much Here

More than in most areas we serve, the right answer in Sun City genuinely depends on where your well sits. Drive around the community and you will see the contrast plainly: smooth, flat stretches of valley floor give way to clusters of weathered granite boulders and low rocky hills. Underground, those two settings behave completely differently. The valley floor is layered alluvium and sediment — older and younger alluvial deposits, with specific yields estimated anywhere from about 5 to 15 percent depending on how coarse and permeable the material is. The knolls and their flanks are granitic bedrock cut by fractures.

A well drilled into the alluvium gets its water from the spaces between sand and gravel grains, and the only way to improve it is to reach more or better saturated material — by deepening, re-screening, or redeveloping. A well drilled into the granite gets its water from fractures, and that is what hydrofracturing can reopen. Two neighbors a quarter mile apart can need completely different solutions. This is precisely why we lead with diagnosis rather than a sales pitch for one service.

How Hydrofracturing Works (For the Right Wells)

For wells finished in suitable bedrock, the process uses only clean water — no chemicals or additives:

  1. Baseline flow test to record current yield.
  2. Downhole camera survey to confirm casing condition and, critically, whether the well is in rock or alluvium.
  3. Packer isolation of the target zone.
  4. High-pressure injection at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI to flush and extend the rock fractures.
  5. Development and re-test to confirm improvement within 24 to 48 hours.

On-site work runs four to eight hours.

Common Causes of Low Yield Around Sun City

  • Falling water table. The San Jacinto Basin has seen long-term groundwater declines, and a dropping water level is a frequent cause of weakening wells in this area — one that fracturing does not fix.
  • Sediment infiltration and screen fouling. Common in alluvial wells; calls for redevelopment or a new screen, not fracturing.
  • Clogged rock fractures. On granitic-knoll properties, this is the classic hydrofracturing scenario.
  • Worn pump or pressure equipment. Sometimes the well is fine and an aging pump or pressure tank is the real issue.
  • Hard water and elevated dissolved solids. Parts of the basin run over 1,000 mg/L TDS, a water-quality issue addressed with treatment rather than rehab.

A Realistic Sun City Scenario

Say a longtime Sun City property notices its well slowly producing less each summer. If the driller's report shows the well bottomed in sand and gravel at 250 feet, fracturing is unlikely to help — the better conversation is about whether the water table has fallen and whether deepening or redeveloping the well makes sense. If instead the report shows the well drilled into granite on a rocky rise, and the camera shows silted-up fracture zones, hydrofracturing becomes a strong, cost-effective option. Same symptom, two very different fixes. The diagnostic visit is what tells us which story is yours, and we would rather spend an afternoon getting that right than guess.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Do you know whether your well is finished in rock or in sand and gravel? Your driller's report is the single most useful document, since it determines whether fracturing even applies.
  • Is the shortage gradual over years (often a falling water table) or tied to peak summer demand (often drawdown/recovery)?
  • Is the pump short-cycling, or is water coming up sandy or cloudy? Sandy water suggests a screen issue.
  • Has water quality changed — new staining, scale, or taste? That is a treatment question, separate from yield.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing a good fit for a Sun City well?

It depends on your parcel. Wells finished in the granitic knolls around Sun City can be strong candidates; wells in the valley-floor alluvium usually are not, and are better served by deepening, screen work, or redevelopment. We determine which applies before recommending treatment.

My well yield has dropped — does that mean I need fracturing?

Not necessarily. In the San Jacinto Basin, a falling regional water table is a common cause of weakening wells, and fracturing will not raise the water table. Diagnosis comes first.

Does the process use chemicals?

No — only clean water under pressure, with nothing left behind in the well.

What about my hard water?

Fracturing increases flow, not quality. Parts of the basin run high in dissolved solids; for hardness or high TDS you would want filtration or a softening system, which we also install.

How do I know if my well is in rock or alluvium?

Your original driller's report usually states it, and a downhole camera survey confirms it. This is the first thing we check, because it decides the right approach.

If fracturing is not right for my well, what then?

We will recommend the method that fits — deepening, a new screen, redeveloping the borehole, treatment, or a new well — and explain the cost and trade-offs honestly.
