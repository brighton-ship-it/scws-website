Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Bernardino County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Big Bear Lake well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Honest Water Talk for Big Bear Lake at Altitude

Big Bear Lake is an alpine resort town wrapped around a seven-mile reservoir in the San Bernardino Mountains, sitting at roughly 6,743 feet of surface elevation in San Bernardino County. It is a four-season destination, ski slopes in winter, hiking and boating in summer, and a community where vacation rentals and second homes outnumber year-round residences in many pockets. With that altitude and those hard winter freezes comes a blunt truth: you cannot grow avocados here. These subtropical trees cannot survive a mountain freeze, so any page promising avocado-grove irrigation in Big Bear Lake is selling a fantasy. What we offer instead is real, season-tested mountain well service for the homes and cabins that depend on private water.

If avocados brought you here, let us redirect you to what actually matters in Big Bear Lake: wells drilled into fractured granite, basin water near the lake, freeze protection that holds through January, and reliable service for properties that may sit vacant between guests. Those are the genuine water challenges of this town, and they are our daily work.

Groundwater Beneath Big Bear Lake

The Bear Valley groundwater basin is a story of two settings. Near the center of the lake and across the flats between Big Bear and Baldwin Lakes, alluvial deposits reach impressive thickness, well over a thousand feet in places, and some lower-lying properties can tap relatively productive basin water. Move toward the hillsides and the eastern end of the valley, however, and the alluvium thins dramatically, giving way to the hard crystalline granite that defines the mountains. On those lots, a well must penetrate solid rock and rely on intersecting fractures to yield water.

Recharge is driven almost entirely by snowmelt and rainfall seeping down through soil and rock. That makes the resource sensitive to drought and means yield can vary noticeably from one parcel to the next, and from a wet year to a dry one. A submersible pump draws the water up, a pressure tank and switch manage delivery, and the entire system must be built to survive sustained sub-freezing temperatures. Understanding whether your lot draws from basin alluvium or fractured granite is the foundation of every good decision about your well.

The resort character of Big Bear Lake shapes the water work we do in ways that surprise newcomers. A large share of the homes are short-term rentals or weekend retreats, which means the people relying on the water often are not the people who own or maintain the well. A guest arriving Friday night to a dry tap turns a minor pressure-switch fault into an after-hours emergency and a refunded reservation. Owners who treat their well as part of the guest experience, with scheduled inspections and reliable local support, avoid those costly surprises.

Common Scenarios We See Around the Lake

  • Hillside granite wells with modest or uneven yield that depend on fracture networks.
  • Basin wells near the lake that perform well but can see seasonal water-level shifts.
  • Freeze-related failures at wellheads, tanks, and exposed lines after hard cold snaps.
  • Vacation rentals where an unnoticed problem becomes an emergency between guest stays.
  • Mineral content, including iron, manganese, and hardness, that stains fixtures and fouls filtration.
  • Low-yield wells that may be good candidates for hydrofracturing to improve production.

Drought sensitivity is worth keeping front of mind. Because the basin recharges from snow and rain, a string of dry winters can lower water levels and tighten the fractures that feed hillside wells. Owners sometimes notice a well that performed fine for years suddenly struggling during a drought cycle, and the fix may be as simple as adjusting pump settings and adding storage, or as involved as hydrofracturing or deepening. Recognizing the pattern early prevents a slow decline from becoming a dry well.

What to Check Before You Call

Whether you live here year-round or manage a rental, a quick check helps us respond efficiently:

  1. Verify the pump has power and the dedicated breaker has not tripped after a storm.
  2. Listen for short, rapid pump cycling that points to a waterlogged tank or worn switch.
  3. Examine the wellhead and exposed plumbing for freeze cracks, especially after a cold snap.
  4. Read the pressure gauge; erratic readings indicate tank or switch trouble.
  5. Notice whether the well falls short only during peak demand, which suggests a yield ceiling.

Sizing matters more in the mountains than almost anywhere else. Because granite-well yield can swing with the seasons and with drought, a pump matched only to a well's best-day performance will fight a losing battle in a dry year, drawing the water level down, sucking air, and wearing itself out. The right approach pairs a pump sized to sustainable yield with enough storage to ride through peak demand, so a busy holiday weekend does not overwhelm the system. We design for the worst week of the year, not the best.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can avocados be grown in Big Bear Lake?

No. At nearly 6,800 feet with hard winter freezes, the climate cannot support frost-sensitive avocado trees. Commercial avocados grow in frost-free parts of San Diego County, not in the mountains.

Do all Big Bear Lake wells hit granite?

No. Properties near the center of the valley and the lake may tap thick basin alluvium, while hillside and eastern lots must drill through granite and rely on fractures. Yield depends heavily on which setting your lot falls in.

My rental cabin's well underperforms. Can it be improved?

Possibly. Hydrofracturing can enhance fracture connectivity around a granite well and raise its yield, generally costing $3,000 to $8,000, often less than drilling a replacement well.

How do I keep my system from freezing while the cabin is empty?

Insulated wellheads, buried and protected lines, and tanks kept in heated space all help. We can winterize and inspect a vacation property before each season so you are not surprised by a frozen line.
