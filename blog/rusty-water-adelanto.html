Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Adelanto well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Adelanto well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Where Iron and Sediment Come From in the Mojave Aquifer

Adelanto sits on sandy desert alluvium near the margins of the regional Mojave and Antelope Valley groundwater systems. The water table out here is deep, and wells in the Adelanto area commonly reach 250 to 600 feet to find a reliable supply. Water that has traveled that far down through desert sand and ancient lakebed deposits picks up a lot along the way: dissolved iron, fine sand, elevated total dissolved solids, and in some deep desert zones, naturally occurring arsenic. The same mineralized geology that makes High Desert water hard and high in TDS is what loads it with the iron that turns rusty at the surface.

Iron behaves the same way in Adelanto as it does anywhere: while it is dissolved in oxygen-poor groundwater deep below the desert, it stays clear and invisible. Pump it to the surface, expose it to air, and it oxidizes into the orange and reddish-brown that stains your sinks, your laundry, and your toilet tanks. What makes Adelanto distinctive is how often that iron arrives alongside fine sand and sediment. A deep desert well draws from sandy formations, and when the screen, the pump, or the casing starts to age, that fine grit comes up with the water and combines with oxidized iron to produce cloudy, rust-colored bursts.

Common Causes of Rusty Water on Adelanto Wells

Because Adelanto wells are deep and the water is sediment-prone, the rust you see usually comes from one of these sources:

  • Dissolved iron from the deep aquifer. The water looks clear at the wellhead and browns as it stands. Staining shows up at every fixture in the house at once, which points to iron in the groundwater itself.
  • Fine sand and sediment from the well screen. Sandy Mojave formations mean grit is a constant battle. A worn screen or an over-pumped well pulls sediment that scours fixtures and combines with iron to make the water look muddy and rust-tinted.
  • Corroding steel casing or drop pipe. Desert water that is hard and high in TDS is aggressive on steel. Older Adelanto wells with steel casing and pipe shed rust flakes as they corrode, especially noticeable right after the pump cycles.
  • A failing or undersized pump pulling from the bottom. When the water level drops, common in dry desert years, a pump set too low draws from the iron- and sediment-rich bottom of the borehole, suddenly worsening color and grit.
  • The water heater. As everywhere, if only the hot water is rusty, suspect the tank rather than the well.

What to Check Before You Call

Adelanto properties are often far from town, so a service call takes planning. A little troubleshooting first helps you describe the problem accurately and can sometimes solve it outright.

  1. Test hot versus cold. Run the cold tap, then the hot, at separate fixtures. Rust only on the hot side usually means the water heater; rust on both means the well or main line.
  2. Whole house or one tap? A single rusty faucet may be a corroded fixture or a clogged aerator. House-wide rust points upstream to the well.
  3. Look for sand. Fill a clear glass and let it settle. A layer of fine grit at the bottom is a strong Adelanto-specific clue that your well is pulling sediment, not just dissolved iron.
  4. Steady or intermittent? Constant orange tint suggests dissolved iron; bursts of rust and grit after the pump starts suggest casing corrosion or sediment from the screen.
  5. Watch your pressure. If discoloration comes with falling pressure, sputtering air, or a pump that runs longer than it used to, note it. In the desert those are warning signs the water level or the pump may be in trouble.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the rusty water from my Adelanto well dangerous?

Iron is mainly an aesthetic and nuisance problem rather than a serious health threat at typical levels. The bigger concern in the deep Adelanto aquifer is that iron often accompanies other constituents such as high TDS and, in some areas, arsenic, so we strongly recommend a full water test to understand everything in your supply before drinking it untreated.

Why does my Adelanto water come out with sand in it?

Adelanto wells draw from sandy desert formations, so fine sand is common when a well screen wears out or a pump over-draws. The grit often arrives mixed with oxidized iron, giving the water a muddy, rust-colored look. A sediment filter and, if needed, a screen repair usually solve it.

How deep are wells in Adelanto?

Because the High Desert water table is deep, most Adelanto wells run from about 250 to 600 feet. That depth influences pump selection and cost, which is one reason we confirm your well's specifics during a diagnostic visit before quoting any pump work.

Will a softener fix rusty water in the desert?

A water softener addresses the hardness that makes Adelanto water aggressive and can remove small amounts of iron, but it is not built to handle heavy iron or sand. For dependable results we pair a dedicated iron and sediment filter with a softener so each unit handles what it is designed for.

Could the rust be coming from my pump or casing rather than the water?

Absolutely. High-TDS desert water corrodes steel casing and drop pipe over time, and a pump set too low can pull iron and sediment from the bottom of the borehole. Bursts of rust after the pump cycles, rather than a steady tint, point toward these mechanical sources, which a diagnostic visit can confirm.
