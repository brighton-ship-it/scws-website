How a Well Pressure System Works

Four components keep water moving. The pump lifts groundwater from the aquifer. A pressure tank holds it under compressed air so the pump does not run on every tap. The pressure switch starts the pump at cut-in (often 28-30 PSI here) and stops it at cut-out, with the water delivered between those points being the drawdown. A healthy drawdown and a properly sized pump keep pressure steady across Muscoy's large lots, from the house to the barn or garden, even when regional demand is high.

Muscoy Geology and Its Pressure Challenges

Muscoy lies within the Bunker Hill Basin, a major groundwater resource in the Upper Santa Ana Valley underlain by deep alluvial deposits of sand, gravel, and silt carried down from the nearby San Bernardino Mountains. Wells here tap this alluvial aquifer, part of the larger Santa Ana River watershed, which has been heavily used for both urban and agricultural needs. While the basin benefits from mountain recharge, historical overdraft and ongoing demand create real challenges for well performance. The alluvial nature of the basin means wells often encounter fine sediment that infiltrates screens and casings, restricting flow. Past groundwater overdraft lowered water tables, and although mitigation has helped, older or shallower Muscoy wells can struggle with yield during dry periods. Proximity to urban San Bernardino also introduces potential contamination risks from historical activity, which can affect water quality and equipment longevity.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Muscoy

  • Pump damage from sediment and minerals: Fine alluvial sand erodes impellers, while hard-water scale from mountain runoff reduces efficiency and pressure.
  • Pressure tank failures: Mineral corrosion attacks bladders, and heat and high usage disrupt tank air pressure, causing inconsistent delivery or cycling.
  • Low yield from regional stress: Basin demand and historical overdraft mean older wells may not reach replenished layers, cutting volume and pressure in droughts.
  • Clogged infrastructure: Alluvial sediment blocks screens and pipes, and iron or manganese deposits constrict pipe diameters in aging systems.
  • Electrical and mechanical wear: Aging equipment on long-established lots, plus power fluctuations near the urban grid, interrupt pump cycles and reduce pressure.

What to Check Before You Call

A quick self-check helps. Compare fixtures: weak flow everywhere points to pump or tank; one weak tap points to a clog. Watch the pump for rapid cycling versus constant running without pressure. Inspect the water for sand or odd taste, which can mean alluvial clogs or basin mineral content. Check the tank air charge with a tire gauge (pump off, tank drained), near 28-30 PSI for a 30/50 switch. And note the season, since a dry-month drop often means yield under basin strain. Bring these to the call for a faster diagnosis.

Preventive Maintenance for Muscoy Wells

Prevention is cheaper than repair. Schedule a yearly inspection before summer demand peaks to catch sediment or tank issues early. Check the tank air monthly with a tire gauge so it matches cut-in. Flush sediment buildup with a wellhead filter, replacing it every few months, and flush outdoor taps annually. Protect equipment from heat with a cover near urban heat islands. Conserve during droughts to ease basin strain, and add a water softener against the high calcium and magnesium common in basin groundwater.
