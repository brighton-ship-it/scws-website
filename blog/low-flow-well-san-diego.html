Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Understanding Low-Yield Wells Around San Diego

While the urban core of San Diego runs on municipal water, the surrounding rural and semi-rural reaches of San Diego County, the inland valleys, foothill estates, and backcountry parcels east of the city, depend heavily on private wells. Across this region the dominant geology is the Peninsular Ranges batholith: decomposed granite, fractured crystalline rock, and patches of alluvium along creek beds and valleys. Wells finished in alluvial drainages can be quite productive, while those drawing from fractured bedrock often produce far less and depend on intercepting water-bearing fractures. When a property has a low-flow or low-yield well, it almost always traces back to which of these formations the well sits in and how that formation is behaving.

Low yield is frustrating, but it is rarely a dead end. The key is to measure what you truly have, understand the cause, and choose a solution that fits the geology under your feet rather than a one-size-fits-all upgrade. Here is how we evaluate and improve low-flow wells for San Diego-area property owners.

How to Test Your Well's Flow and Yield

Two numbers tell the story: the flow at your fixtures and the sustained yield of the aquifer. For a fast read on the first, run an outdoor hydrant into a five-gallon bucket and time the fill. Twenty seconds is 15 GPM; one minute is 5 GPM; two minutes or more puts you in clear low-yield territory at 2.5 GPM or less.

The more meaningful measurement is a sustained yield test. Because a pressure tank stores water and can briefly hide a weak well, we draw the level down, hold a steady pumping rate, and time the recovery. A San Diego County home generally needs 3 to 5 GPM of dependable supply, more with irrigation, a pool, or livestock. Valley alluvial wells frequently exceed that, while fractured-rock wells in the foothills and backcountry can settle at 1 to 3 GPM. Knowing the real sustained number is what separates an effective fix from an expensive guess.

Common Causes of Low Yield in the San Diego Area

  • Low aquifer yield in fractured rock. Wells in the granitic backcountry produce only what their fractures release. A parcel that taps a sparse fracture set will run low no matter how strong the pump.
  • Declining water table. After dry years, static water levels across the county's groundwater basins drop, and marginal wells feel it first as reduced flow and faster drawdown.
  • Clogged or encrusted screen. Mineral-rich groundwater leaves calcium, iron, and manganese scale on screen slots and gravel pack, gradually sealing off the openings where water enters.
  • Biofouling. Iron and sulfur bacteria form slimy colonies that plug intake zones, often signaled by staining or a sulfur smell.
  • Pump set wrong or sized wrong. A pump hung above the productive zone loses prime when the level drops, and an oversized pump pulls a modest well down faster than it recovers, producing short cycling and air at the taps.

It helps to remember that low yield and low pressure are not the same problem, even though they feel identical at the faucet. Pressure is about how forcefully water moves through your pipes, and yield is about how much water the well can actually supply over time. A home can have perfect pressure for ten minutes and then run dry because the well cannot sustain the draw. Sorting out which one you are facing is the first job of any honest evaluation, and it is why we always measure sustained yield rather than judging the well by a quick burst at the tap.

Solutions and Their Tradeoffs

Well Rehabilitation and Cleaning

For an older well that has slowly lost output, mechanical and chemical cleaning of the screen and casing can restore much of the original yield by removing scale and bacterial buildup. At roughly $1,500 to $5,000, it is the most cost-effective first step when a once-strong well has faded. It restores lost capacity but cannot exceed what the aquifer can supply.

Hydrofracturing

For fractured-rock wells, hydrofracturing injects high-pressure water to open and connect existing fractures, often doubling or tripling yield. It is well suited to the granitic terrain common across inland San Diego County and runs about $3,000 to $8,000. In a productive alluvial well it offers little, so diagnosis comes first.

Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach additional water-bearing zones or fractures. In alluvial settings deeper is not always wetter, and in crystalline rock results are unpredictable, so we lean on nearby well logs before recommending it.

Storage Cistern Plus a Booster or Constant-Pressure Pump

For chronic low yield, this is the solution we recommend most. Rather than fighting the formation, you let a slow well fill a large cistern, often 2,500 to 5,000 gallons, around the clock, then use a booster or constant-pressure pump to deliver strong, even household pressure on demand. A 2 GPM well running continuously produces nearly 2,900 gallons a day, far beyond typical household use, so the tank handles your peaks while the well quietly refills it. Installed cost is roughly $3,000 to $7,000 depending on tank size and controls. The tradeoff is the space the tank requires and a bit more equipment, but it turns an unreliable well into a steady one without altering the geology.

Right-Sizing the Pump

Sometimes the cure is simply matching the pump to the well, setting it at the correct depth, or adding a flow-control valve so it cannot outrun the aquifer. A properly sized pump and pressure tank cost $2,500 to $5,500 and resolve many low-water complaints on their own.

When to Call a Professional

If your flow has dropped, your pump is short cycling, the taps spit air, or the water has turned cloudy or sandy, a professional yield evaluation will save you money over trial and error. Frequently Asked Questions

Most of San Diego is on city water, so why do wells matter?

The urban core uses municipal supply, but the surrounding rural and backcountry parts of San Diego County rely on private wells. Those properties depend entirely on well yield, and low-flow problems are common in the area's fractured-rock terrain.

How can I tell if my well is low yield or just has a weak pump?

A sustained yield test answers it. If the well draws down fast and recovers slowly, the aquifer is the limit; if the level holds but pressure is weak, the pump or tank is to blame. Our $125 diagnostic measures both.

What is the best fix for a chronically slow well near San Diego?

For most low-yield wells, a storage cistern paired with a booster or constant-pressure pump is the most reliable answer. It lets a slow well fill the tank around the clock and delivers full pressure on demand.

Is hydrofracturing effective in San Diego County?

Yes, in fractured-rock wells. The inland and backcountry geology is largely granitic, and hydrofracturing opens existing fractures to boost yield, often two- to threefold. It does little in a productive alluvial well.

What does it cost to improve a low-yield well here?

Rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, a storage-plus-booster system $3,000 to $7,000, and a right-sized pump and tank $2,500 to $5,500, depending on your diagnosis.
