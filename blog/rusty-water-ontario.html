Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Ontario well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Ontario well.

Rusty Well Water in Ontario: An Inland Empire Guide

Ontario sits at the heart of the Inland Empire in southwestern San Bernardino County, spread across the broad alluvial plain at the foot of the San Gabriel Mountains, near the Santa Ana River and the Chino Basin. This is one of Southern California's most important groundwater regions, and beyond the city's municipal supply, agricultural operations, dairies, nurseries, and rural properties in and around Ontario draw heavily on private wells tapping the Chino and Cucamonga groundwater basins. When that well water turns rust-orange, stains the laundry, or tastes of metal, the culprit is almost always iron and manganese drawn from the basin aquifer, or corrosion attacking the metal in an aging well system.

Southern California Well Service has worked wells across the Inland Empire and beyond for more than 30 years. The deep alluvial sediments that fill the Chino Basin store enormous quantities of groundwater, and that water carries dissolved iron and manganese leached from the eroded granite and sediment washed down from the surrounding mountains. Because basin water chemistry varies with depth and location, accurate testing is essential before any treatment system is selected for an Ontario property.

How Iron Gets Into Inland Empire Wells

The sediments of the Chino Basin are rich in iron and manganese, minerals eroded over millennia from the San Gabriel range and deposited across the valley floor. Groundwater dissolves these minerals in a clear, ferrous form, and they only become visible as orange or black staining once the water meets oxygen at the surface. In the Ontario area, the rusty water we diagnose typically traces to dissolved ferrous iron from the basin aquifer; already-oxidized ferric iron forming inside the well or pipes; manganese, which causes darker staining and is widespread in basin groundwater; iron bacteria, which create the reddish slime found in toilet tanks and on screens; and corrosion of steel casings, drop pipes, and galvanized household plumbing.

On Ontario's agricultural and dairy properties, we frequently encounter older wells with steel casing that has corroded over decades of hard use, shedding rust into the water. Heavy seasonal pumping for irrigation can also draw down the water table and pull iron-rich sediment into the system, while the region's well-documented hard water compounds scale and mineral buildup. As anywhere, rust limited to the hot water usually points to a failing water heater rather than the well itself.

Is Rusty Water Dangerous?

Iron and manganese are secondary, aesthetic contaminants under EPA classification, meaning they are judged on taste, color, and odor rather than acute toxicity. Iron at typical well concentrations will not make a healthy person ill, but it stains fixtures and laundry, tastes metallic, scales up appliances, and feeds iron bacteria. Manganese is the one to watch, since the elevated levels sometimes seen in basin groundwater carry health concerns. Given the agricultural land use around Ontario, a thorough test is also a smart way to verify there are no other issues in your specific well.

Simple Checks to Do First

Before you call, a few observations help us zero in on the cause. Determine whether the rust appears in hot water only, which usually means the water heater rather than the well. See whether it is whole-house or confined to a single fixture, since one rusty tap typically indicates a local pipe. Look for reddish slime in the toilet tank, the hallmark of iron bacteria. Note whether the rust worsens during heavy irrigation, which suggests drawdown stirring sediment, and whether it appears only on first draw, which points to sediment in the pressure tank.

Solutions for Ontario-Area Wells

Iron and Manganese Filtration

A backwashing oxidizing filter, sized to your tested iron and manganese load, is the dependable core solution. For high-demand agricultural and dairy wells, we size the system for flow so both household and operational needs are met without starving for capacity.

Water Softening for Hard Water

Because Inland Empire water is notably hard, pairing iron removal with a softener protects appliances and improves everyday water quality. We size the two together so they function as an integrated system.

Shock Chlorination

For iron bacteria, full-system disinfection clears the slime, followed by a maintenance schedule to keep the colony from re-establishing.

Casing, Pump, and Yield Work

We line or replace corroded steel casing, swap galvanized plumbing for PEX or copper, and where an older basin well underperforms, hydrofracturing can improve yield.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Ontario well water both rusty and hard?

Chino Basin groundwater is typically high in iron, manganese, and hardness together. Pairing an oxidizing iron filter with a water softener treats both issues as a single system.

Is iron in my Inland Empire well water safe?

Iron is an aesthetic contaminant and not an acute health risk at typical levels, though it stains and tastes metallic. We test manganese as well, since basin water can run elevated.

Why does my well water get rustier during irrigation season?

Heavy pumping draws down the water table and can pull iron-rich sediment into your system. Sizing the well and treatment for your true flow demand prevents it.

Why is only my hot water discolored?

The water heater is the usual cause, not the well. A corroding anode rod or rusting tank affects hot water only, and servicing or replacing it generally fixes it.

Do you charge for a water test in Ontario?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any system or repair we install. It measures iron, manganese, and hardness so we size treatment correctly.

My agricultural well uses old steel casing. Could that be the rust source?

Often, yes. Decades-old steel casing corrodes and sheds rust into otherwise clean water. Lining or replacing the casing stops the contamination at its source.
