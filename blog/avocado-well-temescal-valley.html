Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in Riverside County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Temescal Valley well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Private Well Service in Temescal Valley

Temescal Valley is a growing community in southwestern Riverside County, strung along the Interstate 15 corridor between Corona to the north and Lake Elsinore to the south, framed by the Santa Ana Mountains and the Temescal Wash. It blends newer master-planned subdivisions with older semi-rural and equestrian properties, and that mix is where private wells still play a real role. While the area is better known for citrus history, vineyards, and rural homesteads than for avocado groves, plenty of Temescal Valley owners irrigate fruit trees, pasture, gardens, and small plantings, and a dependable well is the backbone of those properties. This guide is for Temescal Valley property owners who want to understand their water system, catch trouble early, and know what fair pricing looks like. Whether your well supports a household, an equestrian operation, or a few acres of trees, the fundamentals are the same.

The Components of Your Well System

Knowing the parts makes problems easier to describe and faster to solve.

  • Borehole and casing: the lined drilled shaft. In the alluvial valley fill and fractured rock of the Temescal area, well depths vary widely, often from roughly 150 to 500 feet depending on the parcel and the aquifer.
  • Submersible pump: lifts water from depth, sized to the well's yield and your demand.
  • Pressure tank: stores pressurized water and protects the pump from constant cycling.
  • Pressure switch: the small control that starts and stops the pump at set pressures.
  • Treatment and storage: filters, softeners, and storage tanks added for hardness, iron, or peak irrigation demand.

Common Well Issues in the Temescal Valley

Seasonal Yield Swings

Southwestern Riverside County runs hot and dry through summer, and water tables can drop during extended dry periods. A well that keeps up in winter may struggle in August, showing up as air spitting from faucets or pressure that fades under irrigation load.

Hard Water and Scale

Groundwater here is typically mineral-rich. Scale builds in pumps, pipes, and water heaters, shortening equipment life. Softening or scale treatment is a frequent and worthwhile fix.

Pressure Tank Failure

Rapid pump cycling or pressure that collapses when two fixtures run almost always traces to a pressure tank that has lost its air charge, one of the most common and least expensive repairs.

Iron, Manganese, and Odor

Rusty staining indicates iron and manganese, and a rotten-egg smell points to hydrogen sulfide. Both are common in the valley and both respond well to the right treatment.

What You Can Check First

  1. Reset the breaker once. Immediate re-tripping is an electrical fault, not a nuisance, and needs a professional.
  2. Read the gauge. Record the pressure at the tank before calling.
  3. Check the tank charge. With the pump off and pressure drained, the air valve should read about 2 psi below the pump's cut-in pressure.
  4. Walk the supply line. Wet ground or unusually green patches can reveal a buried leak driving up run time.
  5. Note the timing. A sudden failure and a slow seasonal decline point to different causes.

Leave pump pulling and any control-box or wiring work to a licensed crew, since those tasks combine high voltage with heavy equipment set deep in the well.

Maintenance for Hot, Dry Conditions

In Temescal Valley's climate, proactive care matters even more. We recommend an annual review of pressure-tank charge, pump cycling, and water quality, a water test every year or two for bacteria, nitrates, iron, and hardness, and sediment-filter changes on schedule. Because summer is exactly when a tired pump is most likely to fail, wells older than fifteen or twenty years are worth inspecting before the heat arrives so a replacement can be planned rather than rushed. Owners with irrigation should clear drip emitters each spring; a clogged line wastes scarce water and stresses trees during the hottest months.

Boosting a Low-Yield Well

If your Temescal Valley well struggles during summer, a new well is rarely the first answer. Adding a storage tank lets a modest well fill overnight and deliver strong daytime flow, while a constant-pressure system smooths delivery across the property. Where an older well has lost production, hydrofracturing can reopen fractures and restore yield for a fraction of the cost of drilling. We measure your actual yield and recommend the least expensive option that genuinely solves the problem, whether you are watering a few horses, a garden, or a stand of fruit trees.

Water Quality and Treatment Options

Beyond flow and pressure, water quality is a frequent concern for Temescal Valley well owners. The region's groundwater can carry hardness, iron, manganese, and occasionally nitrates from past agricultural use, so periodic testing is wise, especially on older parcels or where a septic system sits nearby. The good news is that nearly every issue has a proven treatment: water softeners for hardness, oxidizing filters for iron and manganese, aeration or specialized media for sulfur odor, and reverse osmosis for drinking water where dissolved solids run high. We collect samples, explain the results without jargon, and recommend only the equipment your water actually needs. Sizing treatment correctly matters, because an oversized system wastes money and water while an undersized one fails to keep up during peak demand.

Why Local Drilling Experience Counts

The geology beneath Temescal Valley is not uniform. Parcels closer to the wash sit over alluvial fill, while those against the Santa Ana foothills tap fractured bedrock, and the right approach differs between them. A contractor who knows the area can anticipate likely depths, casing needs, and yield before the rig ever arrives, which translates to a more accurate bid and fewer surprises.
