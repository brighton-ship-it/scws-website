What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Phelan Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Phelan

Phelan sits on the western edge of the high desert in San Bernardino County, spread across the alluvial fans and decomposed-granite benches that climb toward the San Gabriel Mountains. Most homes here, from the ranchettes off Sheep Creek Road to the parcels near Phelan Road and Baldy Mesa, depend on private wells because there is no municipal main running to the back half of these neighborhoods. When a well that once filled a stock tank in twenty minutes suddenly takes an hour, the worry is immediate: is the well going dry, or is it just clogged? For a great many Phelan wells, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is the repair that restores flow without the cost and disruption of drilling a brand-new well.

This guide explains how hydrofracturing works, why Phelan's fractured-granite geology makes it one of the better high-desert candidates for the technique, when it is the right call, and when your money is better spent elsewhere. We will not oversell it. Hydrofracturing only helps a specific kind of well, and part of our job is telling you honestly whether yours qualifies.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

A hard-rock well in Phelan does not draw water from a pool sitting underground. It draws from a network of natural cracks, seams, and fractures running through the granitic bedrock. Rainfall and snowmelt off the San Gabriels percolate down through the alluvium and into those fractures, and your borehole intersects them. Over years of pumping, the finest of those fracture pathways silt up with mineral scale, fine sediment, and clay washed in from the surrounding rock. The well does not run out of water so much as it loses its plumbing connection to the water that is still there.

Hydrofracturing reopens that plumbing. We lower an inflatable device called a packer into the cased borehole and inflate it to seal off a target zone. Then we inject clean water above the packer at controlled pressure, typically between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. That pressure pushes into the existing fractures, flushes out the silt and scale that has accumulated, and nudges hairline cracks open into wider, better-connected channels. The result is a borehole that once again taps the surrounding fracture network the way it did when it was new, and often better. No chemicals are used. The only thing going down the well is water, which is part of why the technique is well suited to drinking-water wells in a community like Phelan.

Why Phelan's Geology Is a Good Match

Hydrofracturing lives or dies on geology, and Phelan's geology is largely in its favor. The high desert here is underlain by granitic and crystalline bedrock, much of it weathered into decomposed granite near the surface but solid and fractured at the depths most wells reach. Wells across Phelan and neighboring Pinon Hills commonly run anywhere from roughly 300 feet to well past 600 feet, and many of the deeper ones are completed in hard fractured rock. That is precisely the formation hydrofracturing is designed for. When high-pressure water hits a fractured crystalline formation, it has somewhere to go: it travels along the existing seams and extends them.

There is a caveat worth stating plainly. Some Phelan-area wells, especially shallower ones on the thicker alluvial fans, draw from sand and gravel rather than rock. If your well is screened in loose sand or gravel, hydrofracturing is the wrong tool, because there are no rigid fractures to open; the pressure simply dissipates into the loose material. The honest answer for those wells is usually well deepening, a screen cleaning or rehabilitation, or in some cases a new well. The only way to know which category your well falls into is to look at the well log and run a quick assessment, which is exactly where we start.

Signs Your Phelan Well May Be a Candidate

  • Gradually declining yield — the well produces noticeably less than it did a few years ago, even outside of drought years.
  • Drawdown and slow recovery — the pump pulls the water level down quickly and the well is slow to refill after heavy use.
  • Short-cycling or air spitting — faucets sputter or the pump kicks on and off because the well cannot keep up with demand.
  • A low-yield new well — a recently drilled well in fractured rock that simply never produced the flow you expected.
  • Seasonal shortfalls — the well struggles through the dry, windy stretches of late summer and fall that Phelan knows well.

If your symptoms instead point to a worn pump, a failed pressure switch, or a waterlogged tank, hydrofracturing will not help, and a far cheaper repair will. A proper diagnostic separates a pump problem from a yield problem before you spend anything on the well itself.

The Process, Step by Step

  1. Diagnostic and flow baseline. We measure your current yield and recovery rate and review your well log to confirm the well is completed in fractured rock. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward the work if you proceed.
  2. Camera inspection. A downhole video survey checks casing integrity and helps us identify the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Equipment setup. The high-pressure pump, water supply, and packer assembly are staged at the wellhead.
  4. Zone isolation. Packers seal off the target section so pressure is concentrated where it will do the most good rather than wasted up the casing.
  5. Pressurization. Clean water is injected at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI to flush and open the surrounding fractures. We may treat more than one zone in a deeper well.
  6. Development and re-test. The well is purged of loosened sediment, then re-tested so we can document the before-and-after yield. The treatment itself usually runs four to eight hours.

Realistic Results and Expectations

Across the fractured-rock wells we treat in the high desert, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases. Typical improvement runs two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate, and occasionally more on a well that was badly clogged. We are deliberately careful with those numbers because results depend on the formation. A well sitting in genuinely productive but plugged granite can be transformed; a well drilled into a relatively dry, sparsely fractured pocket of rock has less upside no matter how much pressure we apply. Hydrofracturing improves the connection to whatever water the formation holds. It cannot manufacture groundwater that was never there.

That is why we run the pre- and post-treatment flow tests and give you a documented results report. You should expect honesty about the odds before we start, and proof of the outcome when we finish.

What to Check Before You Call

A few things you can gather ahead of time make the assessment faster and the recommendation more accurate:

  • Your well completion report. California well logs are filed with the state and often with the county; they show total depth, casing, and the formation the well is screened in.
  • The well's age and history. When was it drilled, and what has the flow done over time?
  • Recent changes. Did the drop happen gradually (often a yield issue) or suddenly (more often a pump or pressure-tank issue)?
  • Pump details. The pump's horsepower and set depth help us tell a yield problem from an equipment problem.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing uses pressures that can exceed 3,000 PSI inside a cased borehole. This is not a do-it-yourself project, and the wrong setup can damage casing or grout. Beyond the safety considerations, the real value a contractor brings is the diagnosis: knowing whether your Phelan well is a fractured-rock candidate at all, and steering you away from the procedure if it is not. If your well draws from sand and gravel, or if the symptoms point to the pump rather than the formation, the right professional will tell you so and point you toward a deepening, a rehabilitation, or a treatment system instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing work on my Phelan well?

It depends on what your well is drilled into. Wells completed in fractured granite or other crystalline bedrock, which describes many deeper Phelan wells, are good candidates. Wells screened in loose sand or gravel are not, and we will tell you which one you have before recommending anything.

Is hydrofracturing safe for my drinking water?

Yes. The process uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals of any kind. It is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking despite the similar name.

How long does the treatment take, and can I use water that day?

The treatment itself usually takes four to eight hours. We then develop and re-test the well; in most cases normal use resumes within a day or two, once the well has cleared loosened sediment.

How much will my yield improve?

On suitable fractured-rock wells we commonly see two to five times the prior flow rate, with success in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases. We measure before and after so you have documented proof rather than a promise.

What if hydrofracturing does not help my well?

If your well is not a good candidate, or if treatment does not deliver enough improvement, we will discuss alternatives such as deepening the existing well, rehabilitating the screen, or drilling a new well, and help you choose based on cost and your water needs.

How is this different from drilling a new well?

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates the well you already have for a fraction of the cost of a new one. It is almost always worth trying first when the geology supports it, before committing to an $18,000-plus new well.
