How a Private Well Pressure System Actually Works

A private well system in the San Diego County backcountry is a small chain of components, and weak pressure almost always traces back to one link. A submersible pump sits deep in the borehole and pushes water up to the surface. That water flows into a pressure tank, which holds a cushion of compressed air above a rubber bladder. As water fills the tank, the air compresses and stores energy; when you open a tap, that stored pressure delivers steady flow without the pump cycling on and off every few seconds. A pressure switch monitors the tank and tells the pump when to start (cut-in, often 30 or 40 PSI) and stop (cut-out, often 50 or 60 PSI). When any one of these pieces drifts out of spec, you feel it at the showerhead.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Tierrasanta-Area Wells

Drawing on more than 30 years serving San Diego County, we see the same culprits over and over in this terrain of decomposed granite and fractured crystalline bedrock:

  • Waterlogged pressure tank — When the tank loses its air charge or the internal bladder ruptures, water fills the space that air should occupy. The pump then short-cycles rapidly and pressure swings wildly. This is one of the most frequent calls we get.
  • Failing pressure switch — Switch contacts pit and corrode over time, or the spring setting drifts. A failing switch may cut the pump out too early, leaving you with chronically weak pressure.
  • Clogged sediment and screens — The decomposed granite common across Tierrasanta's canyons and Mission Trails sheds fine sediment that lodges in foot valves, screens, and inline filters, choking flow.
  • Dropping water table — During San Diego's recurring drought cycles, the fractured-rock aquifers that feed backcountry wells can draw down, and a pump that suddenly sits closer to the water level loses output.
  • Worn or undersized pump — On the elevated, hilly lots that define "The Island in the Hills," pumps work harder against gravity. An aging or undersized pump simply cannot keep up.
  • Leaks and partially closed valves — A cracked underground line, a leaking pitless adapter, or a half-shut main valve quietly bleeds away pressure before it ever reaches the house.

What You Can Check Yourself First

Before you call us, a few safe checks can save you a service visit or at least speed up the diagnosis:

  • Is it every fixture or just one? If only one faucet is weak, the problem is likely an aerator or a local fixture, not the well.
  • Check the pressure gauge. Most systems show 40–60 PSI. A needle far below that, or one bouncing rapidly, points to the tank or switch.
  • Confirm valves are fully open. Walk the line from the wellhead to the house and make sure every shutoff valve is wide open.
  • Tap the pressure tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow near the top and solid near the bottom. If it sounds full of water all the way up, it is likely waterlogged.
  • Look and listen at the pump. Rapid clicking of the pressure switch (short-cycling) or a pump that runs constantly without building pressure both signal a real problem.

Please do not open the pressure tank, alter the pressure switch wiring, or pull the pump yourself — these involve electrical hazards and pressurized components best left to a licensed technician.

Preventing Low Pressure Before It Starts

A little routine maintenance goes a long way toward avoiding emergency calls. Check your pressure tank's air charge once a year with a tire gauge, confirming it reads about 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in setting. Flush an outdoor spigot at full flow every few months to clear sediment from the lines before it can pack into screens and filters. Walk your wellhead and visible piping each season looking for damp spots or corrosion that betray a slow leak. If your system includes sediment cartridges, change them on schedule, because the fine granite particles common to the area clog filters faster than many homeowners expect. And schedule a professional inspection every couple of years so worn pump components and drifting switch settings get caught before they leave you without water on a hot weekend.

Frequently Asked Questions About Low Water Pressure in Tierrasanta

How do I know if my low water pressure is the pump or the pressure tank?

Watch the pump's behavior. If the pressure switch clicks on and off rapidly (short-cycling) and the tank sounds full of water when tapped, the tank is likely waterlogged. If the pump runs constantly but pressure never builds, the pump itself is usually worn or the well is producing too little water. Our $125 diagnostic isolates the cause precisely.

Why does my Tierrasanta well lose pressure during summer?

Tierrasanta and the surrounding San Diego County backcountry sit over fractured-rock aquifers that draw down during the region's recurring droughts. When the water level drops near the pump, output falls. We can test your well's yield and discuss options like lowering the pump, hydrofracturing, or storage to ride out dry months.

Is the sandy grit in my water related to the low pressure?

Often, yes. The decomposed granite common around Tierrasanta's canyons and Mission Trails sheds fine sediment that clogs screens, foot valves, and filters, restricting flow. Sediment filtration ($300–$900) usually solves both the grit and the pressure loss.
