Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many Riverside County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Flow and Low-Yield Wells in Murrieta: Getting Your GPM Back

A well that has quietly lost its strength is a distinctly Murrieta kind of headache. One season the shower is fine and the drip irrigation keeps the landscaping green; the next, the pressure tank is short-cycling, the second bathroom loses pressure the moment the kitchen tap opens, and the pump seems to run and run. When your gallons-per-minute (GPM) at the tap drop like this, you have a low-flow or low-yield well, and around the Murrieta and Temecula valleys it is one of the problems we're called out for most often.

Murrieta lies in southwest Riverside County, in the Murrieta-Temecula groundwater basin, tucked between the Santa Rosa Plateau to the west and the rising ground toward Winchester and French Valley to the north and east. Many wells here draw from thick alluvial sediments filling the valley, while properties on the plateau edges and in the hills toward Wildomar and De Luz tap fractured crystalline rock. Because those two settings behave so differently, the right fix for a low-flow well in La Cresta looks nothing like the right fix on the valley floor — and guessing wrong is expensive.

What "Low Flow" Actually Means Around Murrieta

Low flow means the well can't hand over water fast enough to meet the demand you place on it. Turn on a couple of fixtures and the pressure sags; run irrigation and the house goes weak. The underlying cause, though, can be any of several very different problems that all show up as the same weak stream:

  • A declining or seasonal water table. Groundwater levels in the Murrieta-Temecula basin fluctuate with pumping and with wet-versus-dry winters. When the static level drops below your pump's intake in late summer, output collapses even though the aquifer is still there — just lower.
  • A pump set too high, worn, or undersized. Wells drilled in a wetter year often have pumps hung above today's water level. And a tired submersible, or one too small for a growing household, throttles GPM regardless of how much water the well holds.
  • Clogged screen and perforations. Murrieta's hard water deposits mineral scale on the well screen, and iron-reducing bacteria build a biofouling slime that plugs the intake slots. This is a slow strangulation — yield fades over years until it finally becomes obvious.
  • Silting-in or partial casing collapse. Sand and fine sediment from the valley's alluvial fill migrate into the borehole and bury the lower screen. Older casings can also crimp or partially collapse, sealing off the best water-bearing zone.
  • Drought-reduced recharge. Hillside and plateau wells rely on rain soaking into rock fractures to recharge. A dry Southern California winter leaves those fractures unfilled, and a borderline well slides into genuine low-yield territory.

Local Scenarios: How This Plays Out in Murrieta

What We Check When Your Flow Drops

Rather than swapping parts and hoping, we measure the well so the fix matches the actual cause:

  1. Static water level — the resting water level with the pump off, telling us where the aquifer sits today.
  2. Pumping water level and drawdown — how far and how fast the level falls under load; steep drawdown flags a clogged screen or an over-taxed well.
  3. Yield and recovery (GPM) test — sustained flow plus how quickly the well refills afterward. This number drives every sizing decision.
  4. Pump depth and condition — whether the pump can even reach current water, and whether the motor is failing.
  5. Water quality signs — sand, iron staining, or sulfur odor, each pointing to a specific downhole issue.

Fixes, From Simplest to Most Involved

  • Lower or replace the pump. When water is present but out of reach or under-delivered, repositioning or upsizing the submersible restores flow immediately.
  • Well rehabilitation. Mechanical brushing combined with chemical or acid treatment strips scale and biofouling off the screen, reopening the intake and reviving yield — usually far cheaper than a new well.
  • Storage plus booster. A cistern or storage tank captures the well's around-the-clock output; a booster or constant-pressure system then supplies strong household pressure on demand, the go-to answer for truly low-yield plateau and hillside wells.
  • Deepen or drill new. If the aquifer has dropped out of reach or the casing has failed, deepening the borehole or drilling a new well is the durable solution.

When It's Time to Call a Pro

Continuous pump running, a pressure switch that chatters on and off, sand or murky water, or an outright loss of water on a hot afternoon are all signs to stop experimenting. A pump forced to run against a dry or clogged well overheats and burns out, converting a modest repair into a full replacement. Frequently Asked Questions

How much GPM does a Murrieta household really need?

Most single-family homes are comfortable at 3-5 GPM, and larger homes with several bathrooms and irrigation want 6-12 GPM. Plenty of Riverside County wells deliver only 1-3 GPM, which is low flow on paper but entirely workable with a storage tank and booster that bank water between peaks.

How do I know if it's my well or my pump?

A yield and recovery test settles it. Fast recovery with weak delivery means the pump is the limit; deep drawdown and slow recovery mean the well — its screen, depth, or water level — is the constraint.

Can rehabilitation fix a clogged well without drilling?

Frequently, yes. In Murrieta's hard water, scale and iron-bacteria biofouling are the usual culprits, and mechanical brushing plus acid or chemical treatment reopens the screen, often restoring most of the original yield.

Why does my plateau well weaken every fall?

Wells on the Santa Rosa Plateau and nearby hills rely on winter recharge through rock fractures. After a dry winter the water table falls through summer and autumn until it drops below the pump. Adding storage and lowering the pump smooths out the seasonal dip.

Is a storage tank really better than drilling deeper?

Often it is. If the well produces steadily but slowly, a storage-and-booster system lets you use far more water than the raw GPM suggests — at a fraction of the cost and disruption of deepening or re-drilling.
