Rusty Water from Your Highland Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Highland well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Highland well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Well Water in Highland: San Bernardino County Groundwater Explained

Highland sits at the base of the San Bernardino Mountains in San Bernardino County, just east of the city of San Bernardino and north of Redlands, where the Santa Ana River canyon opens onto the valley floor. The terrain is a mix of foothill bench land in East Highland and the flat alluvial basin toward Base Line and the historic citrus districts. While much of Highland is on municipal water, the foothill properties, the larger lots in the East Highland area, and the rural parcels climbing toward Seven Oaks and the mountains often rely on private wells. If your Highland well water is rusty, orange, or brown, the cause lies in the local geology and your well's hardware, and it is a problem we solve routinely.

Southern California Well Service has worked San Bernardino County since 2020. Highland's groundwater comes from the deep Bunker Hill basin and the alluvial fans shed off the San Bernardino Mountains, a setting very different from coastal sand or desert hardpan. Here is what turns that water rusty and how to fix it.

Why Highland Wells Produce Rusty Water

The alluvial fans and basin fill beneath Highland are loaded with sediment eroded from the granite and metamorphic rock of the San Bernardino Mountains. That material carries iron and, less often, manganese. In the deep basin aquifer, iron stays dissolved and invisible until it reaches air at your tap, then oxidizes into the familiar orange tint. Foothill wells drawing from fractured bedrock and shallower alluvium can also pick up iron where groundwater has moved through iron-bearing rock.

The second major source is the well casing and household plumbing. Many older Highland wells were completed with steel casing that corrodes over decades and contributes rust directly to the water column. Inside the home, aging galvanized steel pipe and a neglected water-heater anode rod add their own iron, which is why it pays to determine whether the rust is coming from the well or from the house.

Iron Bacteria and Seasonal Swings

Iron bacteria colonize wells that sit idle or run warm, leaving a reddish slime in pressure tanks and toilet reservoirs and producing an earthy odor. They are harmless but accelerate fouling. Highland wells can also show seasonal changes: the San Bernardino basin water table rises with wet-year snowmelt from the mountains and falls in drought, and shifting draw zones can change a well's iron content from one season to the next. A well that ran clear for years can turn rusty after a dry stretch lowers the water level.

A Step-by-Step Check

  1. Sample at the wellhead. Fill a clear glass at the source and at an indoor tap. If both are orange, the iron is in the water supply; if only indoor water is rusty, your plumbing or heater is the source.
  2. Hot or cold. Hot-only rust means the water heater.
  3. Color of stains. Orange-red is iron; black-brown is manganese; slimy red-brown is bacteria.
  4. Clears or constant. Sediment that settles overnight and clears after running points to suspended particles or casing debris; constant color points to dissolved iron.
  5. Pressure and cycling. Rust paired with falling pressure or rapid pump cycling can signal a drawdown or a failing pump pulling sediment.

Common Highland Well Scenarios

Certain situations come up again and again on Highland properties. A foothill home in East Highland gets orange water only on the hot side after years of service; the water heater finally rusted through its anode rod. A basin-floor property near the old citrus groves sees steady orange tinting that never clears, the mark of dissolved iron straight from the aquifer. A well that performed well through wet years starts pulling sediment and rust after a dry winter drops the water table and the pump draws from a different zone. And an older steel-cased well begins shedding flakes of rust that settle out in a glass overnight, pointing to casing corrosion rather than groundwater iron. Knowing which scenario you are in tells us whether to treat the water, service the pump, or address the casing.

Keeping Your Highland Water System Healthy

A little routine care prevents most rusty-water surprises on a Highland well. Replace the water-heater anode rod every few years before the tank corrodes, keep softeners and iron filters stocked and backwashing on schedule, and run the well regularly so iron bacteria do not establish in a stagnant system. Because the San Bernardino basin water table swings with wet and dry years, an annual water test, ideally after winter, lets you catch rising iron or manganese before it stains fixtures and laundry. We recommend a yearly system check in which we test the water, inspect the pump and pressure tank, and confirm your treatment equipment is performing, catching small issues while they remain inexpensive to fix. If your home still has original galvanized steel supply lines, plan to repipe with PEX or copper before internal corrosion both rusts your water and chokes your flow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Highland well water rusty?

Highland sits on alluvial fans and basin fill eroded from the San Bernardino Mountains, sediment that carries iron and sometimes manganese. The iron stays dissolved in the deep basin aquifer and oxidizes orange at your tap. Old steel casing and household plumbing can add rust too.

How do I tell if the rust is from my well or my house?

Fill a clear glass at the wellhead and another at an indoor tap. If both are orange, the iron is in the supply; if only indoor water is discolored, the source is your plumbing or water heater. Hot-only rust points specifically to the water heater.

Can a drought make my Highland well rusty?

Yes. The San Bernardino basin water table falls during dry years, and a lower level can shift the zone your well draws from, changing its iron content. A well that ran clean can turn rusty after a dry stretch. A water test and inspection confirm the cause.

What does it cost to treat iron in Highland well water?

A whole-house sediment filter runs $300 to $900, and a dedicated iron/manganese oxidation filter or softener runs $1,500 to $3,500, sized to your water test and the basin's hardness. We measure before we recommend so the system fits your actual chemistry.

Is rusty well water in Highland safe to drink?

Iron and manganese at typical concentrations are aesthetic issues affecting taste and staining, not acute health hazards. Because foothill and basin wells can also carry bacteria, we recommend a full water test to be sure of what you are drinking.

Do you replace old steel well casing?

Yes. Severely corroded steel casing can be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination, and we handle the full range of well work as a licensed C-57 contractor, from casing and pumps to treatment systems, with same-day emergency response.
