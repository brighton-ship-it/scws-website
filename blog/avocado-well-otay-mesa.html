Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Otay Mesa well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Agricultural and Residential Well Service for Otay Mesa

Otay Mesa sits in the far south of the City of San Diego, a broad, flat tableland that runs up to the international border with Tijuana. It has a genuine agricultural past: into the 1980s, the mesa was covered with vegetable fields, and at one point farming made up roughly three-quarters of the local water district's sales. Today most of that farmland has given way to warehouses, the border crossing, and residential development, but the mesa's history and its remaining open parcels mean that well water and irrigation are still part of the conversation here. What Otay Mesa has never really been is avocado country, the climate is warm and the soils and water are better matched to row crops and vegetables than to a frost-sensitive grove on the cooler inland slopes.

We believe in being straight with property owners. If you are searching for an "avocado well" on Otay Mesa, what you most likely need is a dependable agricultural or residential well for vegetable production, nursery stock, landscape irrigation, or rural household use. Southern California Well Service has more than 30 years of experience designing and maintaining exactly those systems across San Diego County, and we hold a C-57 water well drilling license to back it up.

How Wells Work on Otay Mesa

Historically, water on the mesa was a real constraint. Before a water district was established, farmers relied on wells, cisterns, catchments that captured runoff, and even water wagons to get by. The water table beneath Otay Mesa is relatively flat and slopes gently to the southwest, descending toward the Tijuana River valley and the ocean. Groundwater here exists in the local sediments, but yields and quality vary, which is why the area shifted largely to imported water as it urbanized.

The economics of mesa farming tell the rest of the story. When agriculture peaked here, water costs on Otay Mesa were among the highest agricultural rates in the country, and that expense, more than any soil or climate limitation, is what eventually pushed most growers off the land. For a property owner today, that same math is worth understanding: in some situations a well sized for irrigation can meaningfully offset the cost of metered municipal water, while in others the modest local yield and treatment needs make imported water the more sensible choice. We help owners run that comparison honestly rather than assuming a well is always the cheaper path.

For the open parcels that remain in agriculture or are returning to nursery and specialty production, a well-designed water system is still a competitive advantage. Drip irrigation matched to a correctly sized pump and a pressure-regulated delivery system uses water efficiently, keeps a grower's costs predictable, and reduces the salt stress that comes from over-applying mineral-laden water. When we design a system for an Otay Mesa grower, we look at the producing capacity of the well, the chemistry of the water, and the specific demands of the crop so the whole system works together rather than as a collection of mismatched parts.

A complete well system on a mesa property typically includes a submersible pump, a pressure tank, a pressure switch, and on agricultural parcels a storage tank to bank water for peak irrigation demand. Because groundwater quality on the mesa can be variable, water-quality testing is an important first step before designing irrigation around a well, especially for salt-sensitive crops.

It is also worth noting how much Otay Mesa has changed in a single generation. Vast tracts that were once lettuce and tomato fields are now distribution centers, the busy Otay Mesa border crossing, and new housing. That transition leaves behind a patchwork of older infrastructure, including capped wells, buried irrigation mains, and storage structures, that property buyers and developers regularly discover during grading. When those features turn up, they need to be evaluated and, in the case of unused wells, sealed properly so they do not become a pathway for surface contaminants to reach groundwater. This is routine work for us, and handling it correctly protects both the aquifer and the owner from future liability.

Common Well Issues We See

  • Variable yield from a water table that is not uniformly productive across the mesa.
  • Elevated dissolved solids in some local groundwater that can stress sensitive plantings.
  • Sediment intrusion from the mesa's sediments, fouling screens and filters.
  • Pump wear from long irrigation cycles in a warm, dry climate.
  • Pressure tank and switch failures causing short-cycling and weak delivery.
  • Legacy and abandoned wells on parcels that were farmed decades ago and need proper decommissioning.

What You Can Check First

If an existing system is misbehaving, start with the basics. Make sure the pump has power and that no breaker has tripped. Tap the pressure tank: a hollow ring at the top with a solid base means the air charge is intact, while a uniformly solid tank usually means it is waterlogged. A pump that cycles on and off every few seconds points to the pressure switch or tank, not necessarily the pump. For irrigation, inspect drip lines for clogged emitters and look for low-pressure zones that suggest sediment or scale. If water quality changes suddenly, note the timing, because that history speeds up diagnosis.

When to Call a Pro

Pulling a pump, doing electrical work at the well, testing water chemistry, evaluating yield, or decommissioning an old well are all jobs for a licensed contractor. A forgotten, improperly sealed well from the mesa's farming era can be a real groundwater hazard. Frequently Asked Questions

Can avocados be grown on Otay Mesa?

Not really as a commercial crop. The mesa's warm climate and variable water are better suited to vegetables and row crops, which is what historically grew here. Avocados do best on the cooler inland slopes of north and central San Diego County.

Is groundwater reliable on Otay Mesa?

It is variable. The water table is relatively flat but not uniformly productive, and quality differs from parcel to parcel. That is why much of the mesa moved to imported water as it developed. We test before recommending a well-based plan.

I bought a parcel that used to be farmed. Could there be an old well?

Quite possibly. Many mesa parcels were farmed for decades and may have legacy wells. An unsealed abandoned well is a contamination and liability risk, and we can inspect and decommission it to county code.

Why does my irrigation water seem to stress my plants?

Elevated dissolved solids or hardness in local groundwater can stress sensitive plantings. We test for salinity and hardness and recommend treatment or blending so your water works for your crop.

Do I need a permit to drill or abandon a well in San Diego?

Yes. Both new wells and decommissioning require permits and inspection. As a licensed C-57 contractor, we handle permitting and build or seal to code.
