What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Cardiff-by-the-Sea Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Coastal Wells in Cardiff-by-the-Sea

Cardiff-by-the-Sea is a coastal community within the City of Encinitas in San Diego County, perched on marine terraces above the surf between San Elijo Lagoon and the bluffs. Most homes here are on municipal water, but some North County properties—particularly inland of the immediate coast—still rely on private wells. If you own one of those wells and it has lost yield, you may have read about hydrofracturing and wondered whether it could help. We want to be candid up front: coastal Cardiff wells are frequently not good candidates, and understanding why will save you money.

The reason comes down to geology. Hydrofracturing works by creating and reopening fractures in hard, crystalline bedrock—the fractured granite found across inland San Diego County. Cardiff and the surrounding coastal strip sit on a very different foundation: layered sandstone and marine-terrace sediments, part of the coastal formations that blanket this stretch of the county. These weakly cemented sands and sandstones do not behave like granite. When you pressurize them, they tend to slump or simply re-fill rather than holding a fracture open, so the treatment rarely produces a lasting improvement. An honest contractor will not sell you a fracture job on a well like that.

How Hydrofracturing Works (and Where It Fits)

When a well is a good candidate, the process is straightforward and chemical-free. We seal off a section of the casing with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. That pressure flushes silt and mineral scale from existing rock fractures and forces them to open wider and reach deeper into the surrounding bedrock, opening new pathways for groundwater. Only clean water goes down the hole—no chemicals, sand, or gels of any kind. It is entirely unlike the chemical fracking used in oil and gas extraction.

The key word is bedrock. Some inland Encinitas and North County properties—away from the coastal terraces—do reach fractured crystalline rock and can be excellent candidates. The right answer for your specific well depends on what formation it is finished in, which is why an on-site assessment matters so much here.

Why Coastal Cardiff Wells Lose Yield

When a sandstone or marine-sediment well slows down, the cause is usually different from a granite well:

  • Screen plugging: Sand-aquifer wells use a screen, and over time that screen can clog with fine sediment, iron bacteria, or mineral incrustation, choking flow.
  • Pump or equipment failure: A worn submersible pump or a failed pressure tank can imitate a low-yield well without the formation being the problem at all.
  • Saltwater intrusion risk: Near the coast, over-pumping can pull saltwater toward the freshwater zone, degrading water quality and effectively limiting how hard the well can be run.
  • Aquifer drawdown: Dry years and nearby pumping can lower the shallow water table that coastal wells depend on.

What to Check and Signs of a Problem

  • The pump runs longer than it once did to refill the pressure tank.
  • Water pressure sags during peak use or in the late afternoon.
  • Faucets sputter air, indicating the water level is dropping to the pump.
  • You notice increasing sand, sediment, or turbidity in the water—a classic screen-plugging sign in coastal wells.
  • Water tastes increasingly salty or brackish, which can signal intrusion.

Time the pump's tank-refill cycle during quiet and busy periods; a big difference points to a recovery problem. But on a coastal well, the fix is more often development, screen rehabilitation, or pump work than hydrofracturing.

When to Call a Professional

Because coastal wells have so many possible causes—and because the wrong treatment can waste money or even worsen intrusion—a professional diagnosis is especially worthwhile here. Living With a Coastal Aquifer

Coastal North County wells sit in a delicate balance with the ocean. The freshwater you pump is essentially floating above denser saltwater, and pumping too aggressively can pull that interface upward and inland. Sustainable use—pumping at a rate the aquifer can replenish—protects both your water quality and your neighbors'. Part of our job is helping coastal well owners understand that balance so the well keeps producing good water for the long term, rather than chasing short-term yield at the cost of intrusion.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing right for a coastal well in Cardiff-by-the-Sea?

Often it is not, and we will tell you so honestly. Hydrofracturing works in hard, fractured bedrock. Coastal wells in Cardiff typically draw from sandstone and marine-terrace sediments rather than fractured crystalline rock, and those formations do not hold open fractures the way granite does. We assess each well individually—some inland Encinitas properties do reach fractured rock and can be candidates.

Why doesn't hydrofracturing work in sandstone and sand aquifers?

Hydrofracturing relies on creating and reopening cracks that stay open in solid rock. Loose or weakly cemented marine sediments and sands tend to collapse or simply re-fill rather than holding a fracture, so the treatment usually does not produce lasting gains. For those wells, proper development, screen rehabilitation, or pump work is usually the better answer.

What can improve a low-yielding coastal well instead?

Depending on the cause, options include mechanical or chemical well development to clean the screen, replacing a worn pump, addressing a failed pressure tank or switch, or in some cases rehabilitating the well screen. Our diagnostic identifies which of these fits your situation.

Should I worry about saltwater intrusion near the coast?

It is a real consideration for coastal San Diego County wells. Over-pumping a coastal well can draw saltwater toward the freshwater zone. Part of a responsible assessment is making sure any work we recommend does not worsen that risk, which is another reason we evaluate coastal wells carefully before treating them.
