How Your Pressure Tank Keeps the Water Flowing

A bladder pressure tank stores water under pressure so your pump doesn't have to run every time you open a tap. Inside, a rubber diaphragm divides a factory-charged pocket of air from the water side. Incoming water compresses the air; when you use water, the air pushes it back out to your fixtures. The pump only kicks on when pressure falls to the switch's cut-in setting and shuts off at cut-out. The gallons delivered in between — the drawdown — determine how often your pump has to start. Protect the drawdown and you protect the pump, which in El Cajon typically costs $2,500-$5,500 to replace once it's worn out.

Why El Cajon's Climate Is Hard on Well Equipment

The valley's summer heat is a genuine factor for well systems here. Pressure tanks and switches mounted in unshaded well houses or against sun-baked walls run hot, and heat accelerates rubber-bladder fatigue and stresses electrical contacts. Combine that with the mineral content common in East County groundwater, and El Cajon tanks can reach the end of their service life toward the shorter end of the usual 10-15 year window. We routinely recommend shading exposed equipment and pairing tanks with sediment filtration ($300-$900) where the water carries grit or scale.

The Symptoms That Bring El Cajon Homeowners to Us

  • Short-cycling: the pump snapping on and off every few seconds — the number-one sign of a waterlogged tank with a failed bladder or lost air charge.
  • Jumpy pressure: strong flow that suddenly weakens and recovers as you use water.
  • Banging pipes: water hammer when a faucet or valve shuts, caused by the missing air cushion.
  • A heavy, solid tank: tap up the side — a solid thud high on the tank plus dead weight means it's full of water it should not be holding.
  • Water at the Schrader valve: press the air valve on top; water instead of air confirms a ruptured bladder.
  • Rust at the base: corrosion weeping from the bottom seam means the steel shell has failed and recharging won't help.

Getting the Air Pre-Charge and Size Right

A pressure tank only performs when its air pre-charge matches the pump's settings. The rule is simple: set the pre-charge 2 PSI below cut-in. On a common 40/60 switch, that's about 38 PSI measured with the tank drained and the pump off. Many El Cajon "dead tank" calls turn out to be nothing more than a pre-charge that has slowly leaked down over the years — a quick recharge restores normal cycling, provided the bladder is still intact.

Sizing follows your pump's gallons-per-minute and your household's peak demand, not just the number of bathrooms. A typical El Cajon valley home is well served by a 32- to 44-gallon tank; a foothill property running irrigation on a hot afternoon while the house is in use may need an 86-gallon tank, dual tanks, or a constant-pressure booster system ($2,000-$4,500) to avoid the cycling that wears pumps out. An undersized tank is the single most common cause of premature pump failure we see.

What You Can Safely Inspect First

  1. Run one fixture and time the gap between pump starts — under a minute points at the tank.
  2. Watch the pressure gauge; a needle that whips between high and low in seconds confirms lost drawdown.
  3. Tap and lift-test the tank for the tell-tale solid, heavy feel.
  4. With the power off and tank drained, briefly press the air valve — water means a blown bladder.
  5. Scan the base for rust and weeping seams.

Leave the wiring alone. The pressure switch operates on live 240-volt power, and any wellhead electrical work belongs with a licensed technician for your safety.

Keeping Your El Cajon Tank Healthy

A pressure tank rewards a little yearly attention. With the pump off and the tank drained, verify the air pre-charge and reset it to 2 PSI below cut-in. Shade any equipment exposed to the valley sun to slow bladder fatigue. Watch the pressure switch for pitted contacts, which East County's mineral water can cause over time. If your water stains fixtures or leaves scale, sediment filtration ($300-$900) or a softener ($1,500-$3,500) protects the whole system. And when cycles start getting louder or recovery slower, a well inspection ($150-$400) can flag a tank on its way out before it leaves you without water on a hot day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does El Cajon's summer heat really shorten pressure tank life?

Yes. Heat accelerates rubber-bladder fatigue and stresses switch contacts, so tanks in unshaded East County well houses often wear out toward the shorter end of the 10-15 year range. Shading the equipment and keeping the pre-charge correct both help.

My pump runs constantly but pressure is weak. Is that the tank?

Not usually. A pump that runs nonstop without building pressure more often points to the pump, a leak, or a dropping water level, whereas a bad tank causes rapid on-off cycling. Our $125 diagnostic identifies the true cause before any parts are replaced.

What should the air pressure in my El Cajon tank be?

Set it 2 PSI below your pump's cut-in — about 38 PSI on a 40/60 switch, checked with the tank drained. If water comes out of the air valve instead of air, the bladder has ruptured and the tank needs replacing.
