Agricultural Wells in Glen Avon: What Local Growers Need to Know

Glen Avon sits in the Jurupa Valley, tucked into the Santa Ana River bottomlands of western Riverside County between Rubidoux, Pedley, and the Pyrite Street corridor. For generations this stretch of the valley floor has supported dairies, small family farms, and backyard groves, and groundwater has always been the workhorse behind them. The alluvial deposits laid down by the Santa Ana River make Glen Avon one of the more productive shallow-aquifer pockets in the region, which is exactly why so many operations here still rely on private agricultural wells rather than expensive metered district water. If you farm, ranch, or keep livestock anywhere from Mission Boulevard down toward the river, a properly built and maintained irrigation well is the difference between a profitable season and a scramble for water in July.

How an Agricultural Well System Works on a Glen Avon Farm

An irrigation or livestock well is more than a hole in the ground. A complete agricultural system on a Glen Avon property usually includes the well casing and screen, a submersible pump set well below the static water level, the drop pipe and wiring, a pressure or storage tank, the controls, and the distribution plumbing that feeds your drip lines, sprinklers, or stock troughs. In the Santa Ana bottomlands, static water levels are often relatively shallow compared to the deeper desert and foothill wells elsewhere in the county, so many Glen Avon ag wells fall in the roughly 80 to 300 foot range, with shallower wells tapping the river alluvium and deeper ones reaching into the mixed deposits and weathered bedrock below.

The single most important number on any farm well is how much water it can deliver without overdrawing, measured in gallons per minute (GPM). A small grove or a few acres of pasture might be perfectly served by 15 to 25 GPM, while a working dairy or a larger row-crop operation can demand 50 to 100+ GPM during peak summer irrigation. Sizing the pump to the well's tested yield is critical. An oversized pump pulls the water level down faster than the aquifer can recharge it, which causes the pump to cycle, suck air, overheat, and burn out years early. We test drawdown, set the pump at the right depth, and often pair it with a variable frequency drive (VFD) so output ramps to match demand and the motor runs cooler and longer.

Local Conditions That Affect Glen Avon Wells

Glen Avon's groundwater is generally hard, like most of the inland Santa Ana watershed, and several parts of Jurupa Valley have a documented history of elevated nitrate from decades of dairy operations and historic agricultural activity in the river bottom. Hard water leaves scale on drip emitters, sprinkler nozzles, and float valves, and it shortens the life of pumps and pressure tanks. Nitrate is mostly a concern when the same well also supplies drinking or household water, but it is worth testing for on any farm where families or workers drink from the tap. We routinely pull water samples and recommend the right treatment, whether that is a sediment filter, an iron and manganese system, a softener for scale control, or simply a dedicated potable line.

Sediment is the other recurring issue in river-bottom wells. The same loose alluvial sand and gravel that makes the aquifer productive can migrate into a poorly screened or aging well, grinding down pump impellers and clogging filters. If your water has turned cloudy or you find grit in your screens, it is usually a sign the well screen, gravel pack, or pump is due for attention. Seasonal swings matter too: Glen Avon summers regularly run well over 100 degrees, and peak irrigation demand in July and August is when an undersized or worn system is most likely to fail, exactly when you can least afford it.

What to Check Before You Call Us

When something goes wrong with a farm well, a few quick checks can tell you whether it is a simple fix or a deeper problem. Before you call, take a look at these:

  • Power and breakers. Confirm the pump breaker has not tripped and that any disconnect or fused panel at the wellhead is on. Heat and rodents are both common causes of tripped or chewed circuits out here.
  • Pressure gauge and switch. If your pressure reads zero or the pump will not start, a failed pressure switch is one of the cheapest and most common culprits. If pressure swings wildly, your tank may have lost its air charge.
  • Water clarity. Note whether the water is cloudy, sandy, or has an odor. Grit points to a well or pump screen problem; rotten-egg smell points to bacteria or sulfur.
  • Drawdown behavior. If output starts strong and then drops off after a few minutes of irrigating, the pump may be outrunning the well, or the water table may have dropped seasonally.
  • Recent changes. New plantings, a bigger herd, or added acreage often mean the old pump simply cannot keep up with the new demand.

Sharing what you find when you call lets us bring the right parts on the first trip, which saves you a return visit and keeps your crops or animals watered.

When to Call a Professional

Some things are safe to check yourself, but agricultural wells involve high-voltage three-phase power, heavy pumps hanging on hundreds of feet of pipe, and confined-space hazards at the wellhead. Call a licensed contractor when the pump will not start after you have confirmed power, when you have lost water entirely, when output has dropped sharply, when you see persistent sand or cloudiness, or when you are planning to expand and need more GPM than your current setup delivers. We also handle the permitting and proper construction standards that Riverside County and the regional water authorities require for new ag wells, so the work is done right and on record. Pulling a submersible pump from a deep well is not a do-it-yourself job, and a botched attempt can drop equipment down the casing and turn a repair into a far costlier recovery.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are agricultural wells in Glen Avon?

Because Glen Avon sits on the Santa Ana River alluvium, many ag wells here are relatively shallow, commonly in the 80 to 300 foot range. Wells that tap the river-bottom sand and gravel can be on the shallower end, while those reaching into deeper mixed deposits and weathered bedrock go further down. We test the specific aquifer at your site before recommending a depth.

How many gallons per minute do I need for my Glen Avon operation?

It depends on what you are watering. A small grove or a few acres of pasture may only need 15 to 25 GPM, while a dairy or larger row-crop farm can require 50 to 100+ GPM during peak summer. We size the pump to your well's tested yield so it never outruns the aquifer.

Is the water in Glen Avon hard or contaminated?

Glen Avon groundwater is generally hard, and parts of Jurupa Valley have a history of elevated nitrate from past dairy and agricultural activity. Hard water scales drip emitters and shortens pump life, while nitrate matters most when the well also supplies drinking water. We test your water and recommend filtration, softening, or treatment as needed.

Why is my well producing sand or cloudy water?

In river-bottom wells, fine alluvial sand can migrate into the well through a worn screen or failing gravel pack, or as the pump ages. Sand grinds down impellers and clogs filters. If your water has turned gritty or cloudy, have the well screen and pump inspected before the damage spreads.

How much does a new agricultural well cost in Glen Avon?

A complete turnkey ag well typically runs $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth, casing, pump size, and water treatment needs. Smaller jobs like a pump replacement run $2,500 to $5,500 and a pressure tank $600 to $1,500. We give written estimates and credit the $125 diagnostic toward approved work.
