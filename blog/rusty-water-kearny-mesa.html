What Causes Rusty Tap Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron affecting your Kearny Mesa home.

Rusty Water Solutions for Kearny Mesa Homes and Businesses

Kearny Mesa is a busy, built-out neighborhood near the geographic center of the City of San Diego, in San Diego County. Bounded roughly by I-805, SR-163, and Highway 52, it is a mix of mid-century homes around Birmingham and Ronson, dense commercial corridors along Convoy Street and Clairemont Mesa Boulevard, and light-industrial blocks. Almost every property here is served by the City of San Diego's municipal water system rather than a private well, so when Kearny Mesa residents see rusty, orange, or brown water, the cause is rarely the source water itself. Far more often it is corrosion inside the building's own plumbing — or a temporary disturbance in the public mains.

That distinction is good news, because plumbing-side rust is usually localized and affordable to fix once you know where it is coming from. The challenge in an older urban neighborhood like Kearny Mesa is that decades of galvanized steel pipe, aging water heaters, and periodic main work all produce similar-looking discoloration. Pinpointing the real source is what turns a frustrating mystery into a quick repair.

How Corrosion and Iron Turn Tap Water Rusty

The rusty color you see is almost always iron oxide — rust — and in a municipally served home it comes from a few well-understood places:

  • Galvanized steel pipe corrosion. Homes built before the 1970s were commonly plumbed with galvanized steel. Over decades the protective zinc coating wears away and the steel underneath rusts from the inside out, shedding orange particles into your water and slowly choking flow.
  • Water-heater breakdown. Every tank water heater contains a sacrificial anode rod that corrodes on purpose to protect the steel tank. When that rod is used up, the tank itself begins to rust, releasing brown water that shows up first on the hot side.
  • Municipal main flushing and breaks. The City periodically flushes hydrants and repairs mains. Both stir up the fine iron-and-mineral sediment that settles in any pipe network, producing short-lived discoloration that clears on its own.
  • Iron and manganese sediment in service lines. Even treated water carries trace minerals that accumulate over time. A pressure change or nearby construction can briefly release them.
  • Corroded fittings and old valves. Rusting steel nipples, unions, and shut-off valves at fixtures can color the water at just one tap.

Where iron is genuinely present in the water and needs ongoing treatment — most relevant on the rare acreage or older parcel with a private well on the edge of the mesa — an oxidizing or air-injection filter converts dissolved iron and manganese into particles that a media bed removes, while a softener can manage lighter iron loads along with hardness. For the vast majority of Kearny Mesa homes on city water, though, the fix is repair or replacement of the corroding component, not a treatment system.

Common Rusty-Water Scenarios in Kearny Mesa

These are the patterns we see most often across the neighborhood:

  • The 1960s Birmingham-area home with whole-house rust. Original galvanized supply lines reach end of life and begin shedding rust throughout the house, often paired with weak pressure.
  • Brown hot water only. A classic sign the water heater's anode rod is spent and the tank is corroding — common in homes where the heater is 10-plus years old.
  • Sudden discoloration neighborhood-wide. When several homes on the same street see rust at once, a hydrant flush or a main repair is almost always the cause, and it clears within hours.
  • One rusty bathroom faucet. A corroded supply valve or fitting at that single fixture, easily isolated and replaced.
  • Light-industrial or commercial buildings on Convoy. Larger, older steel service lines and infrequently used fixtures can hold rusty, stagnant water that clears after a good flush.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick tests will tell you a lot:

  1. Hot, cold, or both? Rust on hot water only means the water heater. Both sides means the issue is upstream in your service line or shared plumbing.
  2. One fixture or whole house? A single rusty tap is a local fitting; whole-house rust points to your main supply pipe or the public main.
  3. Ask a neighbor. If others on your block see it too, it is a municipal main event, not your house.
  4. Flush a cold tap. Run cold water several minutes. If it clears and stays clear, it was settled sediment from a main disturbance.
  5. Check the water heater age. If it is over 8–10 years old and the hot side is rusty, the heater is the likely culprit.
  6. Note weak pressure. Rust plus declining pressure across the house is a strong sign of failing galvanized pipe.

When to Call a Professional

Call when the discoloration is persistent, returns after flushing, affects the whole house, or comes with falling water pressure — all signs of corroding interior pipe that needs replacement. Brown hot water that does not clear means it is time to service or replace the water heater. If you are one of the few Kearny Mesa-area properties on a private well and you see a sudden, lasting change in color, taste, or odor, treat that as a reason to test the well and inspect the casing and pump. A licensed C-57 contractor can evaluate your plumbing, test the water at multiple points, and tell you whether you need a simple repair, a repipe, or a treatment system — rather than guessing.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm on City of San Diego water in Kearny Mesa. Why is it rusty?

Because the rust is almost always coming from inside your home or the local mains, not the treated source. The two most common causes are corroding galvanized pipe in older houses and a failing water heater. A recent hydrant flush or main repair can also cause temporary discoloration that clears within hours.

Is rusty tap water dangerous?

Iron itself is an aesthetic concern at typical levels, not an acute toxin, but rusty water tastes metallic, stains laundry and fixtures, and can signal deteriorating pipe. If the color persists or comes with an odd taste or smell, have it checked rather than ignoring it.

Only my hot water is brown — what does that mean?

Your water heater is the source. A corroding tank or a spent anode rod releases rust on the hot side. Flushing the tank and replacing the anode often fixes it; if the tank is badly corroded, replacement is the durable solution.

The whole house is rusty and pressure is low. What's wrong?

That combination strongly suggests aging galvanized steel pipe rusting from the inside, which both colors the water and restricts flow. A repipe in copper or PEX is the lasting fix, and we can scope whether a partial or full replacement makes sense.

Do I need a water treatment system on city water?

Usually not. Treatment systems address iron and minerals in source water, which is mainly a private-well issue. On city water, the right move is to repair or replace the corroding component. We will tell you honestly which one you actually need.
