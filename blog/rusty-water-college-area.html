What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your College Area water.

Rusty Water in the College Area: What's Really Causing It

The College Area is the San Diego neighborhood surrounding San Diego State University, an established eastern-central community in San Diego County made up of older single-family homes, student rentals, and apartment complexes. Like the rest of urban San Diego, the College Area is served by the City of San Diego's municipal water system, so genuine private wells within the neighborhood are essentially nonexistent. When residents and landlords here notice rusty, orange, or brown water, the source is almost never the city's supply and almost always the aging plumbing inside the building, typically corroding galvanized-steel pipe. This article focuses on that real-world cause, while also explaining well-sourced iron for readers who own rural property elsewhere in the county.

Galvanized Plumbing in Older College Area Homes

Much of the College Area's housing stock dates to the mid-twentieth century, the period when galvanized steel was the standard for residential water lines. Galvanized pipe is steel coated in protective zinc, and that coating slowly sacrifices itself to shield the steel. Once it is depleted, after many decades, the bare steel rusts from the inside. The rust collects on the pipe walls and periodically sheds, traveling to faucets as orange flakes that tint the water. That is why a College Area home receiving clean municipal water can still produce rusty water at the tap. The most telling sign is discoloration that is heaviest in the morning, after water has sat motionless overnight, and that clears once the tap runs for a minute or two. Many rental properties in the neighborhood still operate on at least some original galvanized plumbing.

The Pressure Problem That Comes With It

Galvanized corrosion does not just discolor water; it strangles it. As rust and scale accumulate inside the pipes, the open diameter shrinks, sometimes severely in lines that are sixty or seventy years old. The result is weak showers, slow fixtures, and low pressure across the home. In multi-unit College Area rentals, tenants often report both rusty water and poor pressure, and the two share a single root cause. A filter might capture some rust, but it does nothing for the restricted flow or the ongoing corrosion, which is why repiping is the genuine remedy for older buildings.

How Iron Appears in Well Water

For readers who own rural or East County land served by a private well, the rust originates underground rather than in the pipes. Groundwater dissolves naturally occurring iron as it passes through soil and fractured rock, carrying it invisibly until it reaches oxygen at the tap and oxidizes into rust. Wells can also develop iron bacteria, which leave a slimy reddish residue and a musty odor. Well-sourced iron is removed by oxidation and filtration, and iron bacteria are cleared with shock chlorination plus filtration. These are well-service solutions, distinct from the repiping that fixes a College Area plumbing problem.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Run cold water at several faucets. Rust everywhere points to the main service line or building risers; rust at only some fixtures points to the branches feeding them.
  2. Compare hot and cold at one faucet. Rust isolated to the hot side usually indicates the water heater.
  3. Watch the timing. Morning-heavy rust that clears with running is the classic galvanized signature.
  4. Consider the building's age and whether it has ever been repiped.
  5. If you own a private well elsewhere, check the toilet tank for orange slime and note any musty odor.

Why Student Rentals See This So Often

The College Area's mix of long-held single-family homes converted to rentals and older multi-unit buildings near San Diego State means a large share of the plumbing has been in service for many decades. Rentals are also less likely than owner-occupied homes to have been proactively repiped, so original galvanized lines often remain in place well past their reliable lifespan. The combination of heavy daily use by multiple tenants and aging pipe accelerates the appearance of rust and the decline in pressure. For owners, recognizing this pattern early allows for planned repiping rather than emergency repairs, and it heads off the steady stream of tenant complaints that corroded plumbing tends to generate.

How the Two Fixes Work

Understanding the mechanics clarifies why diagnosis matters. When the cause is galvanized corrosion, the cure is repiping: a plumber maps the runs, opens limited wall sections, replaces the steel with PEX or copper, and restores the surfaces, often finishing a single-family home in a few days. When the cause is well-sourced iron, the cure is treatment that oxidizes the dissolved iron into particles and filters them out, with the media backwashing periodically. The two approaches have nothing in common, which is exactly why an accurate up-front diagnosis protects College Area owners from spending on equipment that cannot solve their actual problem. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the College Area have private wells?

No. The neighborhood is entirely on City of San Diego municipal water, so rusty water here is almost always caused by corroding galvanized plumbing in older homes and rentals, not by the source water.

Why is the water orange first thing in the morning?

That pattern is the hallmark of galvanized pipe corrosion. Water that sits overnight collects rust from the pipe walls, then clears after you run the tap for a minute.

Is rusty water unsafe?

Iron is not a recognized health hazard at typical levels, but it tastes metallic and stains. The bigger concern is what the corrosion is doing to your plumbing and pressure over time.

Can a filter fix the problem?

A sediment filter reduces visible particles but does not stop the pipes from rusting or restore lost pressure. For galvanized plumbing, repiping with PEX or copper is the permanent fix.

I'm a landlord with rusty water in an older rental. What should I do?

Have the plumbing assessed. We can determine whether targeted repairs will work or whether a building-wide repipe is the smarter investment, and provide a clear estimate either way.
