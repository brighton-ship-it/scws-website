What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Loma Linda Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells Around Loma Linda

Loma Linda sits in the San Bernardino Valley of San Bernardino County, tucked between Redlands, Colton, and the rising ground toward the Crafton Hills and the San Bernardino Mountains. The city core is on municipal water, but the rural parcels on its edges and in the surrounding foothills, the properties climbing toward Mentone, Yucaipa's outskirts, and the mountain front, often depend on a private well. When one of those wells starts producing less than it used to, hydrofracturing is frequently the most cost-effective way to recover the lost yield without committing to a new well.

Hydrofracturing, also called hydrofracking, forces high-pressure water into a low-yield bedrock well to reopen the natural fractures it draws from. Where a well is completed in the hard granitic rock of the mountain front, the technique has a strong record of restoring production, at a fraction of the cost of drilling new.

How the Treatment Restores Flow

A hard-rock well produces from cracks in the granite, not from an open pool of water. Groundwater seeps through fracture seams to reach the borehole, and the well yields only as much as those seams allow. Over years of pumping, the fractures nearest the well clog with sediment and mineral scale, and output falls. Hydrofracturing reverses that decline. We set an inflatable packer to isolate a section of the well and inject clean water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI, flushing the clogged fractures and opening tight cracks nearby so the borehole reconnects to water it had lost. No chemicals are involved, and the on-site work generally takes four to eight hours.

Loma Linda Geology: Valley Floor Versus Mountain Front

Loma Linda's geology is genuinely mixed, and that mix decides whether hydrofracturing will help. Much of the valley floor sits on deep alluvial basin fill, sand, gravel, and older sediments shed from the San Bernardino Mountains. Wells finished in that deep alluvium are weaker hydrofracturing candidates, because they produce from loose sediment rather than fractured rock, and there are no rock fractures for pressure to open. The stronger candidates are wells drilled into the granitic bedrock along the mountain front and the Crafton Hills, where fracture flow governs yield. We always check your well log and, when needed, run a downhole camera to determine which situation you are in before recommending anything, because being honest about that distinction is the whole point.

Signs Your Well May Be a Candidate

  • Declining production over recent years.
  • Running dry under irrigation or peak household demand.
  • Slow recovery after the pump shuts off.
  • Frequent pump cycling from a borehole that cannot keep up.
  • A new bedrock well that never produced as hoped.

Realistic Results in San Bernardino County

In the granitic rock of San Bernardino County's mountain front, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of treated bedrock wells, with successful jobs commonly raising flow two to five times above baseline. Those are honest ranges, not guarantees, because the fracture pattern underground is never fully visible. We protect you with measurement, baselining your flow before treatment and re-testing afterward, so the result is documented. For valley-floor alluvial wells, we are upfront that the odds are lower and that other options may serve you better.

Our Step-by-Step Process

  1. Diagnostic and flow test ($125, credited) establishing your current yield.
  2. Camera inspection to confirm casing and locate productive fracture zones.
  3. Hydrofracturing staged high-pressure injection, usually a single day on site.
  4. Post-treatment flow test after the well settles over 24 to 48 hours.
  5. Documented report a clear before-and-after.

Cost Versus Drilling a New Well

Hydrofracturing typically costs $3,000-$8,000. A new turnkey well in the San Bernardino Valley generally runs $18,000-$42,000 depending on depth, casing, and pump, and a replacement pump, if yours is also failing, is about $2,500-$5,500. On a sound bedrock well, trying hydrofracturing first is almost always the financially sensible move.

When Deepening or Redrilling Makes More Sense

Hydrofracturing is not the answer for every well, and we will tell you when it is not. If a camera shows failed casing, a silting borehole, or a well that never reached a producing fracture, deepening or drilling new is the sound recommendation. A deep alluvial valley-floor well, likewise, has no rock fractures for the process to open, and in those cases a different solution will serve you better. We would rather give you that honest guidance than charge for a treatment unlikely to help.

Why the Mountain-Front Wells Respond Best

The reason wells along the Crafton Hills and the base of the San Bernardino Mountains tend to respond so well is the nature of the rock itself. The granitic bedrock there is naturally jointed and fractured from the same tectonic forces that built the range, which means a hydrofracture has plenty of existing cracks to clean and connect. By contrast, the deep basin fill on the valley floor stores water in pore space between grains of sand and gravel, a setting where pressure has nothing to fracture. Understanding that difference is exactly why we read your well log first; it tells us whether your well is tapping the fractured rock that hydrofracturing was designed to improve.

Groundwater Pressure in the San Bernardino Basin

Groundwater levels across the San Bernardino Valley have been under steady pressure from decades of demand, and that regional decline can make a marginal well feel like it is failing faster than it really is. Hydrofracturing does not create new groundwater, but on a sound bedrock well it widens the fracture network so the well draws more efficiently from whatever water is available and recovers faster between uses. For a foothill property running a household and some irrigation on a single well, that improved recovery is often what restores everyday reliability through the long, dry inland summers.

Getting More Years From an Aging Foothill Well

Many wells on the rural edges of Loma Linda and the surrounding foothills were drilled years ago, and owners often assume a slowdown means the well is finished. That is frequently not the case. A bedrock well that is still structurally sound can usually be rehabilitated with a single hydrofracture, deferring the much larger expense of a new well well into the future. The important thing is to act before the casing or borehole deteriorates beyond saving, which is why we encourage owners to call at the first signs of decline rather than waiting for the well to quit. Catching it early keeps the cheaper, less disruptive option on the table.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Loma Linda well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

It depends on whether your well is in granitic bedrock along the mountain front, a strong candidate, or in deep alluvial valley fill, a weaker one. We check your well log and run a camera when needed to tell you honestly before recommending treatment.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. It uses only clean water under pressure, so it does not affect water quality the way oil-and-gas fracking can.

How much yield improvement is realistic?

On sound bedrock wells, successful treatments commonly raise yield two to five times, though the rock determines the result. We measure before and after so the gain is documented.

How does the cost compare to a new well?

Hydrofracturing runs about $3,000-$8,000 versus $18,000-$42,000 for a new turnkey well, which is why we recommend trying it first on a sound bedrock well.

Will the improvement last?

Often for many years. Minerals re-accumulate slowly with pumping, so some wells benefit from a repeat treatment later, but a single hydrofracture frequently lasts the better part of a decade.

How quickly can you come out?

We provide same-day emergency service for non-producing wells and schedule routine diagnostics promptly across San Bernardino County.
