Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When Your Well Pump Won't Start in Big Bear City

At 6,700-plus feet on the eastern shoulder of the San Bernardino Mountains, Big Bear City sits in San Bernardino County where private wells draw from the alpine valley's fractured granite and the glacial-alluvial fill of Baldwin Lake basin. When a homeowner here turns on a tap and hears nothing but silence, it is rarely a coincidence — high-altitude winters, hard mineralized water, and a rural power grid all conspire against well equipment. This guide walks you through exactly what is happening below your feet, what you can safely check yourself, and when it is time to phone a licensed contractor.

Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years pulling, diagnosing, and resetting pumps from Erwin Lake and Sugarloaf to the lake's north shore. The patterns repeat themselves, and once you understand how the parts of a pressure system depend on one another, a "dead" pump becomes far less mysterious.

How Your Big Bear City Well System Actually Works

Most homes in Big Bear City run a submersible pump set hundreds of feet down inside the casing, though older parcels near the valley floor sometimes still use jet pumps. Whatever the type, six things have to cooperate for water to flow:

  • Power supply — a dedicated 240-volt circuit feeds the pump. In a mountain community where winter storms knock out SCE service and surges follow restoration, this is the single most common failure point.
  • Pressure switch — the small box on the tank piping that senses pressure and tells the pump when to run. Its contacts pit and corrode, and at altitude the tiny diaphragm can freeze if the pump house is unheated.
  • Capacitor or control box — three-wire submersibles rely on a control box (with a start capacitor and relay) mounted near the tank. A blown capacitor is a textbook "hums but won't start" symptom.
  • Pressure tank — the bladder tank stores pressurized water so the pump isn't cycling every time someone washes their hands. A waterlogged tank causes rapid short-cycling that eventually trips breakers.
  • Motor and wiring — the down-hole motor and the wire splices inside the well can degrade, especially where rodents or moisture reach above-ground junctions.
  • Water level in the well — Big Bear's aquifer fluctuates with snowpack. After a dry winter, a low static level can let a pump break suction or trigger its low-water shutoff.

Common Big Bear City Scenarios

A few situations come up again and again in this valley. After a hard freeze, we get calls from cabins where the pressure switch or above-ground piping iced over while the owners were away — the pump is fine, but the switch can't signal it. Following a winter power outage, surge damage to the control box capacitor leaves the motor humming without spinning. And during late summer in a drought year, wells near Baldwin Lake and the higher Lakewood/Whispering Forest benches occasionally pull the water table down to the pump intake, so the motor's thermal protection cuts in and out.

Hard, mineral-rich water is the slow killer here. Calcium and iron scale builds inside the pump and on the switch contacts over years, which is why a pump that "suddenly" died often had warning signs — sputtering, longer recovery, or a tank gauge that wandered.

What You Can Safely Check Yourself

  1. Check the breaker. Open your panel and look for the double-pole 240V breaker labeled for the well. If it's tripped, reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop — that is a fault, not a fluke.
  2. Listen at the pressure tank. With water demand on, put your ear near the pressure switch. A faint click means it is trying to call for the pump. Total silence points to the switch or its power.
  3. Read the pressure gauge. Note the PSI. A reading stuck at zero with no pump response, or a gauge that swings wildly, tells a story a technician can use.
  4. Inspect for freeze and rodent damage. In an unheated pump house, look for frost on piping or chewed wire insulation at the junction box.
  5. Verify other 240V loads. If your electric dryer or range also acts up, the problem may be upstream of the well entirely.

Important: Never open the control box, probe the pressure switch terminals, or touch well wiring with the breaker on. These systems carry 240 volts and the down-hole motor can deliver a lethal shock.

Realistic Cost Ranges

Most "won't start" calls in Big Bear City resolve at the low end of these ranges:

  • Pressure switch replacement: $150–$350
  • Pressure tank replacement: $600–$1,500
  • Control box / capacitor repair: typically folds into the diagnostic and switch range above
  • Full submersible pump replacement: $2,500–$5,500

  • Iron/manganese filtration or a softener for hard water: $1,500–$3,500

If a well has truly run dry from a low water table, hydrofracturing ($3,000–$8,000) or, in rare cases, a new well ($18,000–$42,000) may be discussed — but that is the exception, not the rule, for a pump that simply won't start.

Reading the Symptoms Before We Arrive

The more you can tell us when you call, the faster we resolve the outage. A pump that is completely silent with a tripped breaker points one direction; a pump that runs continuously but never builds pressure points another; and a pump that clicks on, runs a few seconds, then shuts off usually means short-cycling from a waterlogged tank or a low water level. In Big Bear City we also ask whether the home was recently vacant, since unoccupied cabins are the ones most prone to freeze-related switch failures. Noting the season, the last storm, and any unusual sounds turns a guessing game into a targeted repair.

Mineral scale deserves special mention in this valley. Because Big Bear groundwater carries significant calcium and iron, the pitting it leaves on pressure-switch contacts and the buildup it leaves on pump impellers shorten equipment life noticeably compared with softer-water regions. If your pump finally quit after years of faithful service, scale is frequently the underlying culprit, and pairing a new pump with appropriate filtration protects your investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Big Bear City pump hum but not start?

A hum with no water almost always means the motor is getting power but cannot spin — usually a failed start capacitor in the control box, a seized motor, or a stuck check valve. It needs professional diagnosis, not repeated breaker resets.

Could a winter freeze be why my pump won't run?

Yes. A frozen pressure switch or above-ground pipe can prevent the pump from receiving the signal to start even though the pump itself is undamaged. Thawing and insulating the pump house often restores operation.

How deep are wells in Big Bear City?

It varies widely with the fractured granite, but many valley wells fall in the roughly 200–500 foot range. Depth affects both pump sizing and how a low snowpack year impacts your water level.

Is it safe to keep resetting the breaker?

No. A breaker that trips more than once is protecting you from a real fault — a shorted motor, damaged wire, or overloaded circuit. Repeated resets risk fire and can destroy the motor.
