When Your Norco Well Needs Rehabilitation

Wells drawing from the Temescal and Riverside alluvial groundwater basins — part of the broader Santa Ana River watershed — operate in sand-and-gravel alluvium that gradually loads up screens and gravel packs with mineral and biological deposits. Norco's groundwater also tends to be hard, rich in dissolved calcium and magnesium, which accelerates scale formation. Over the years that buildup quietly chokes off flow. Watch for these warning signs that your well is due for attention:

  • Declining yield: The well delivers less water than it once did, and the pump runs noticeably longer to refill the pressure tank or fill stock troughs.
  • Hard-water scale symptoms: Heavy calcium-carbonate crusting on fixtures, valves, and stock tanks often mirrors the scale building up on the well screen below ground.
  • Iron and manganese staining: Reddish-brown or blackish stains on troughs, concrete, and laundry point to iron and manganese — minerals that also feed screen-clogging deposits.
  • Rotten-egg odor or slime: A sulfur smell or slimy buildup signals iron-bacteria biofouling, a common and aggressive cause of lost capacity in alluvial-basin wells.
  • Sand or sediment in the water: Grit at the faucet or in fixtures suggests the screen or gravel pack is failing and fine alluvial material is migrating into the well.
  • Rising power bills and short cycling: A pump straining against a clogged formation draws more electricity and may switch on and off rapidly as available water drops.
  • Nitrate or quality shifts: Changes in taste, clarity, or water-test results — including nitrate concerns common in agricultural and equestrian areas — warrant a professional look downhole.

Rehabilitation Methods We Use in Norco

No two wells decline for exactly the same reason. The correct approach depends on your well's age, construction, depth, and the precise nature of the fouling. Because Norco wells are typically completed in unconsolidated sand-and-gravel alluvium rather than fractured rock, our methods are tuned to protect screens and gravel packs while clearing them thoroughly. Here is how we work.

Video Inspection

Every rehabilitation begins with a downhole camera survey. Before we recommend a single treatment, we run a video log to document casing condition, screen integrity, the depth of scale or biofouling, sediment accumulation, and the static water level. On a Norco horse property where a well may date back decades, this inspection tells us whether we are dealing with calcium-carbonate scale, iron-bacteria slime, sand intrusion, or structural casing damage. It prevents you from paying for treatments that won't fix the actual problem, and it gives us a clear before-and-after benchmark.

Mechanical Cleaning

Mechanical methods physically dislodge encrustation from the well screen and casing. Using wire brushing, surge blocks, and high-pressure jetting, we scrub away the hardened calcium-carbonate scale and iron-and-manganese deposits that block the open area of the screen. In sandy alluvial wells, mechanical work must be done with care to avoid damaging slotted screens, which is exactly the kind of judgment three decades of field experience provides. Mechanical cleaning is most effective when paired with chemical treatment.

Chemical Treatment / Acidizing

Chemical rehabilitation targets the deposits that brushing alone cannot reach — inside the gravel pack and out into the surrounding formation. Acid treatment dissolves calcium-carbonate scale and iron and manganese precipitates, while specialized biocides and dispersants break down the iron-bacteria biofilm that causes so much trouble in Santa Ana River watershed wells. Acidizing is frequently the most cost-effective single step, and when the problem is primarily mineral scale or biofouling, it can restore a large share of a Norco well's original yield. All chemicals are selected for the specific fouling identified during inspection and handled in accordance with water-supply safety standards.

Well Redevelopment

After cleaning and chemical treatment, redevelopment pulls the loosened material back out of the well and re-establishes a clean, stable flow path. Air-lift pumping and surging draw fine sediment, dissolved scale, and dead biofilm out of the gravel pack and screen slots where they have collected over years of operation. This step is especially important in Norco's sand-and-gravel alluvium, where fines steadily migrate toward the screen. Proper redevelopment is often the difference between a temporary improvement and a lasting one.

Screen Cleaning

The screen is the heart of the well, and its open area determines how freely water enters. We give it focused attention — clearing slot-by-slot encrustation, flushing sand and sediment that has bridged across openings, and verifying with a follow-up camera pass that the screen is genuinely open again. For older Norco wells suffering from chronic sand fouling, restoring screen performance can dramatically reduce grit, protect the pump, and recover lost capacity.

Rehabilitation vs. New Well: What Makes Sense?

The honest answer depends on your well's condition, and we will tell you plainly which way the numbers point. A typical well rehabilitation in Norco costs far less than drilling a replacement — generally a few thousand dollars versus the much larger investment a brand-new well demands. Rehabilitation almost always makes sense when the casing is structurally sound, the screen is salvageable, and the aquifer beneath your property still holds water. If our video inspection reveals collapsed casing, a corroded-through screen, or a genuinely depleted zone, we will say so rather than sell you repeated treatments that cannot succeed. For the majority of Norco's alluvial-basin wells, though, rehabilitation restores reliable flow and postpones the cost of a new well by many years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes Norco wells to lose production?

In Norco's hard-water alluvial basins, the usual culprits are calcium-carbonate scale, iron and manganese deposits, iron-bacteria biofouling, and sand or sediment migrating into the screen. Rehabilitation can often restore flow without drilling a new well.

I have horses and use my well for livestock and irrigation. Can you work on a property like mine?

Absolutely — equestrian and rural-residential wells are the heart of what we do in Horsetown USA. We understand that livestock and pasture irrigation cannot wait, and we schedule rehabilitation to minimize downtime so your animals stay watered throughout the process.

How much does well rehabilitation cost in Norco?

Most rehabilitations run $1,500–$6,000 depending on depth and the type of fouling. A video inspection is $300–$600, and our $125 diagnostic is credited toward the work if you proceed. That is far less than the $18,000–$42,000 a new well typically costs.

My water smells like rotten eggs and stains the trough. What is happening?

That combination usually points to iron and manganese plus iron-bacteria biofouling — very common in Santa Ana River watershed wells. A combination of chemical treatment, mechanical cleaning, and redevelopment typically clears it and restores cleaner water.

How often should a well be rehabilitated?

Many wells benefit from rehabilitation every 10–20 years, but hard water, heavy irrigation use, and iron bacteria can shorten that interval. Periodic video inspections catch fouling early, when it is cheapest to address.

Should I rehabilitate or drill a new well?

If the casing is sound and the aquifer still holds water, rehabilitation is almost always the smarter investment. Our downhole camera survey tells us for certain, and we give you an honest recommendation either way.
