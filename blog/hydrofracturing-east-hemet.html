What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your East Hemet Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in East Hemet

East Hemet sits on the floor of the San Jacinto Valley in Riverside County, where citrus groves, avocado orchards, horse properties, and older homesteads have relied on private wells for generations. The valley is blessed with thick alluvial deposits and a productive groundwater basin managed by the Hemet-San Jacinto Watermaster, but not every well shares equally in that bounty. Properties nearer the San Jacinto Mountain foothills, on the eastern and southern edges of the community, often draw from harder, fractured rock where yields can be stubbornly low. When an East Hemet well underperforms, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is frequently the most economical way to boost production without the cost and disruption of a new well. For the many East Hemet families who irrigate orchards, pasture, and large lots, a reliable well is not a convenience but a daily necessity.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a proven well rehabilitation technique. Our crew lowers inflatable packers into your well casing to isolate a target zone, then injects clean water at high pressure, typically between 1,500 and 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure cleans out the silt, fine sand, and mineral scale that have accumulated in the formation and reopens or extends the natural fractures that carry groundwater to your well. The result is a more efficient, freer-flowing well, achieved using only clean water with no chemical additives. Most treatments are completed in a single four-to-eight-hour day.

Why East Hemet Wells Lose Yield

The San Jacinto Valley has two very different well environments, and understanding which one you are in explains a lot:

  • Valley-floor alluvial wells (typically 150 to 350 feet deep) usually enjoy good yields, but over decades the perforations and the surrounding sand-and-gravel formation can become clogged with fines and hard-water scale, slowly choking production.
  • Foothill and mountain-front wells (often 300 to 500 feet deep) draw from fractured granitic and metamorphic rock. These wells depend on a limited network of fractures, and if those fractures are tight or partially sealed, the well may have always been a weak producer.
  • Heavy seasonal irrigation demand. East Hemet's agricultural and equestrian properties draw hard on their wells through the long, hot Inland Empire summers, exposing any well that recovers slowly.
  • Aging infrastructure. Many local wells are decades old, and the formation around an older borehole simply works less efficiently than it once did.

Hydrofracturing is especially effective on the foothill fractured-rock wells and on alluvial wells suffering from clogging, because in both cases the problem is restricted flow into the casing rather than a lack of groundwater in the region.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Time your recovery. After running water heavily, note how long the well needs to refill. A long recovery is a classic sign of a formation that hydrofracturing can improve.
  • Inspect the pressure tank and switch. A waterlogged tank ($600-$1,500) or a worn pressure switch ($150-$350) can mimic a weak well. These are inexpensive fixes worth ruling out first.
  • Watch your pump's behavior. Rapid on-off cycling or a pump that loses prime usually means the water level is dropping faster than the well recovers.
  • Find your driller's log. The original report tells us your depth and whether you are in valley alluvium or foothill rock, which shapes the treatment plan.
  • Note any sand or grit. Sediment in the water changes how we approach the job and whether screen repair is also needed.

When to Call a Professional

If your East Hemet well runs short during summer irrigation, recovers slowly, or has lost production over the years, schedule a professional evaluation. Hydrofracturing uses specialized high-pressure equipment and is not a DIY task. We start with a diagnostic visit ($125, credited toward any work performed), including a flow test and, where appropriate, a downhole camera inspection to confirm the well is a sound candidate. Foothill wells in solid fractured rock respond best; wells finished entirely in loose sand or gravel generally are not good candidates, and we will say so plainly.

Hydrofracturing vs. a New Well

Drilling a new well in East Hemet means permitting, days of drilling, casing, and a new pump installation, all of which add up quickly. Hydrofracturing lets you get more from the borehole you already own. Many East Hemet wells that owners assumed were failing turn out to be plugged or under-developed rather than truly depleted, and a single treatment can add years of reliable service. We recommend a new well only when the evidence shows the existing one cannot be economically saved.

Frequently Asked Questions

My East Hemet well is on the valley floor in sandy soil. Will hydrofracturing help?

If your well is clogged with fines or scale that restricts flow into the casing, hydrofracturing can clear those pathways and restore yield. If the well is finished entirely in loose, unconsolidated sand with no fractures to open, it may not be a good candidate. Our diagnostic determines which applies.

How much improvement is realistic?

For suitable wells in Riverside County's fractured and alluvial formations, yield commonly improves two to five times, with success in roughly 70 to 80 percent of good candidates. We document your flow before and after.

Will this affect my Watermaster-adjudicated rights?

No. Hydrofracturing simply helps your existing well operate more efficiently. It does not create a new water right or change your adjudicated allocation. We work within all applicable local and state requirements.

How long does the treatment take?

The injection itself usually takes four to eight hours, completed in a single visit. We run a post-treatment flow test, and most owners see improved, stabilized flow within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it safe for my water quality?

Yes. The process uses only clean water under pressure with no chemicals. We can also test and advise on water quality if you have noticed hardness or sediment, both common in the valley.

What happens if my well still does not produce enough afterward?

If a treated well underperforms, the diagnostic information we gathered guides the next step, whether that is deeper rehabilitation or a new well. The investigation is never wasted, and we present every option with honest pricing.
