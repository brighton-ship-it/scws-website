Well Pressure Tank Problems: Symptoms, Causes & How to Fix Them

Published February 1, 2026 | 10 min read

Your well pressure tank is the unsung hero of your water system—it maintains steady pressure, reduces pump wear, and stores water for immediate use. But when well pressure tank problems develop, you'll notice symptoms ranging from annoying pressure fluctuations to a pump that never stops running. This guide covers everything you need to know about diagnosing a waterlogged pressure tank, fixing a pressure tank not holding pressure, and performing basic well tank troubleshooting before calling a professional.

How Well Pressure Tanks Work

Before diving into problems, understanding how your pressure tank works helps you diagnose issues more effectively. Modern well systems use bladder-type pressure tanks (also called diaphragm tanks), which have largely replaced older galvanized air-over-water tanks.

The Bladder Tank Design

Inside your pressure tank is a rubber bladder surrounded by compressed air. When your well pump runs, water fills the bladder and compresses the surrounding air. This compressed air acts like a spring—when you open a faucet, the pressurized air pushes water out without the pump needing to run.

The Pressure Cycle

Your system operates between two pressure settings controlled by the pressure switch:

  • Cut-in pressure: When tank pressure drops to this level (typically 30-40 PSI), the pump turns ON
  • Cut-out pressure: When tank pressure reaches this level (typically 50-60 PSI), the pump turns OFF
  • Drawdown: The usable water between cut-in and cut-out—this is what you use before the pump kicks on again

A properly functioning tank allows the pump to run for several minutes during each cycle, then rest while you use the stored water. This cycling pattern is crucial for pump longevity.

Pre-Charge Air Pressure

The air pressure in your tank (when empty) should be set to 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure. For a 30/50 system, that means 28 PSI. For a 40/60 system, it's 38 PSI. This pre-charge is critical—wrong air pressure causes many common problems.

Common Well Pressure Tank Problems

1. Waterlogged Pressure Tank

A waterlogged pressure tank is the most common problem we encounter. It occurs when the rubber bladder fails, allowing water to fill the entire tank with no air cushion remaining.

Symptoms of a Waterlogged Tank:

  • Rapid pump cycling: The pump turns on and off every few seconds or with minimal water use
  • Tank feels heavy: A waterlogged tank is noticeably heavier than normal
  • Solid sound when tapped: Knock on the tank from bottom to top—it should sound hollow at the top (air) and solid at the bottom (water). If it's solid throughout, it's waterlogged
  • Cold or sweating tank: The entire tank feels cold to the touch or has condensation
  • No air from Schrader valve: When you press the valve core, water comes out instead of air

Why Bladders Fail:

  • Age and normal wear (10-15 years typical lifespan)
  • Chlorine or chemical exposure degrading the rubber
  • Sediment or debris rubbing against the bladder
  • Over-pressurization or pressure spikes
  • Manufacturing defects (less common)

2. Pressure Tank Not Holding Pressure

When your pressure tank won't hold pressure, the system can't maintain consistent water delivery. This manifests as declining pressure during use or pressure that drops when no water is being used.

Causes of Pressure Loss:

  • Low air pre-charge: Air naturally escapes over time; tanks need periodic air top-ups
  • Leaking Schrader valve: The air fill valve on top can develop leaks
  • Failed bladder: A ruptured bladder can't maintain the air/water separation
  • Tank shell leak: Corrosion or damage allowing air to escape
  • Pressure switch issues: Sometimes the tank is fine but the switch isn't reading pressure correctly

3. Rapid Pump Cycling (Short Cycling)

If your pump turns on and off repeatedly within seconds or minutes, your pressure tank is almost certainly the culprit. This behavior is extremely hard on pump motors and can lead to premature pump failure.

Short Cycling Causes:

  • Waterlogged tank: No air cushion means minimal drawdown capacity
  • Undersized tank: Tank too small for your pump's output
  • Incorrect air charge: Too much or too little air affects drawdown
  • Check valve leak: Water draining back to the well after pump stops
  • Pressure switch differential too narrow: Cut-in and cut-out too close together

4. Water Hammer and Pressure Surges

Banging pipes, shuddering faucets, and pressure spikes when fixtures turn off can indicate pressure tank problems:

  • Insufficient air cushion: The tank can't absorb pressure surges
  • Tank too far from fixtures: Long pipe runs without expansion compensation
  • Failed check valve: Causing water to slam back

5. Visible Tank Damage

Physical signs of trouble include:

  • Rust or corrosion: Especially around fittings and the base
  • Bulging: Indicates internal pressure problems
  • Water around the tank: Leaking from shell or fittings
  • Unusual sounds: Gurgling, hissing, or rumbling from inside

DIY Well Tank Troubleshooting

Many pressure tank issues can be diagnosed—and some fixed—without calling a professional. Here's a systematic well tank troubleshooting approach:

Step 1: Check the Air Pressure

This is the most important diagnostic step. You'll need a tire pressure gauge capable of reading 0-50 PSI.

  1. Turn off the pump at the breaker
  2. Open a faucet to drain all water from the tank (let it run until it stops)
  3. Locate the Schrader valve on top of the tank (looks like a tire valve)
  4. Remove the cap and check the air pressure
  5. Compare to your system's cut-in pressure minus 2 PSI

What you'll find:

  • Correct pressure (28-38 PSI typical): Tank air charge is fine—problem is elsewhere
  • Low pressure: Add air to the correct level (bladder may still be intact)
  • No pressure/water sprays out: Bladder has failed—tank needs replacement

Step 2: Perform the Tap Test

With the system pressurized normally, tap on the tank from bottom to top with your knuckles:

  • Bottom half solid, top half hollow: Normal—water below, air above
  • Solid all the way up: Waterlogged—bladder has failed
  • Hollow all the way: No water—pump or supply problem

Step 3: Check the Schrader Valve

Apply soapy water around the valve and watch for bubbles indicating a leak. If the valve leaks, you can replace just the valve core (available at any auto parts store) without replacing the tank.

Step 4: Inspect for Visible Leaks

Check all fittings, the tank shell, and the floor beneath the tank. Even small drips indicate problems. Look for rust stains showing where water has been escaping.

Step 5: Test the Pressure Switch

If the tank seems fine, the pressure switch may be the culprit. Remove the cover and look for:

  • Burnt or pitted contacts
  • Ant nests (a common problem)
  • Corrosion or moisture damage
  • Debris blocking the pressure sensing port

Safety note: Always turn off power before opening the pressure switch cover.

How to Add Air to Your Pressure Tank

If your tank has low air pressure but the bladder is intact, you can restore proper function:

  1. Turn off the well pump at the circuit breaker
  2. Open a nearby faucet and let it run until water stops flowing
  3. Close the faucet
  4. Connect an air compressor or bicycle pump to the Schrader valve
  5. Add air until you reach 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (typically 28-38 PSI)
  6. Turn the pump back on at the breaker
  7. Let the system pressurize and check for proper operation

Important: If you need to add air more than once per year, your bladder is likely failing and the tank will need replacement soon.

When to Call a Professional

Contact a well service professional when:

  • Water comes out of the Schrader valve instead of air
  • The tank is visibly damaged, corroded, or leaking
  • Adding air doesn't solve the problem
  • You're not comfortable working around electrical components
  • The pump still short-cycles after tank troubleshooting
  • You need to determine proper tank sizing for replacement
  • Multiple components seem to be failing

Pressure Tank Replacement Cost

When repair isn't possible, here's what to budget for pressure tank replacement:

  • Tank only (20-30 gallon): $150-$400
  • Tank only (40-50 gallon): $300-$600
  • Tank only (80+ gallon): $500-$1,000
  • Professional installation: $150-$400 labor
  • Complete replacement including installation: $400-$1,200

Factors affecting cost include tank size, brand quality, accessibility, and whether other components (pressure switch, fittings) need replacement. Premium tanks from brands like Flexcon or Well-X-Trol cost more but often last longer.

Extending Your Pressure Tank's Life

Proper maintenance can significantly extend tank lifespan:

  • Check air pressure annually: Top off as needed to maintain proper pre-charge
  • Install a sediment filter: Debris wears bladders prematurely
  • Address water quality issues: High chlorine or mineral content degrades bladders faster
  • Ensure proper sizing: Undersized tanks cycle more and wear faster
  • Maintain your whole system: Pump and tank issues often relate to each other

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well pressure tank is waterlogged?

A waterlogged pressure tank will feel heavy and sound solid when tapped all the way up instead of hollow at the top. Other signs include rapid pump cycling (turning on and off frequently), fluctuating water pressure, and the tank feeling cold or sweating. Check the air pressure at the tank's Schrader valve—it should be 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (typically 28-38 PSI). If no air comes out, the bladder has failed.

How long do well pressure tanks last?

Quality well pressure tanks typically last 10-15 years with proper maintenance. Factors affecting lifespan include water quality (sediment and chemicals can damage bladders), correct air pressure maintenance, and whether the tank was properly sized for your system. Cheaper tanks may fail in 5-7 years, while premium tanks can last 20+ years.

Can I add air to my pressure tank to fix it?

If your bladder tank still has an intact bladder, you can add air through the Schrader valve on top. First, turn off the pump and drain the tank completely by opening a faucet. Then add air until you reach 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (usually 28-38 PSI). However, if the bladder has ruptured, adding air won't help—you'll need to replace the tank.

What size pressure tank do I need for my well?

Pressure tank size depends on your pump's flow rate and desired run time. A general rule is 1 gallon of drawdown capacity per GPM of pump output. For a typical home with a 10 GPM pump, a 20-30 gallon tank works well. Larger tanks reduce pump cycling and extend pump life. Undersized tanks cause rapid cycling which wears out pumps prematurely.

Why does my pressure tank keep losing air?

Pressure tanks lose air for three main reasons: a failed bladder (most common), a leaking Schrader valve (the air fill valve on top), or a tank corrosion hole. If you need to add air more than once a year, the bladder is likely failing. Check the Schrader valve with soapy water for bubbles. If neither is the issue and air still escapes, the tank shell may be compromised and needs replacement.
