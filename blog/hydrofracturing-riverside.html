What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Riverside Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

Hydrofracturing for Water Wells in Riverside, CA: An Honest Look

If you own a private well in or around the City of Riverside, you have probably searched for ways to coax more water out of it, and "hydrofracturing" keeps coming up. Before you spend a dollar, here is the candid truth from a contractor who has worked these formations for decades: for most wells inside Riverside and the surrounding Inland Empire communities, hydrofracturing is usually the wrong tool. It is a great technique in the right geology, but Riverside's geology is mostly not that geology. This guide explains why, and points you toward the fixes that actually restore water in this part of Riverside County.

The City of Riverside sits in Riverside County, in the heart of the Inland Empire, with Moreno Valley to the east, Corona to the west, and Jurupa Valley, Rubidoux, and Grand Terrace just across the way. The Santa Ana River winds through the area. Most homes and businesses inside the city are served by Riverside Public Utilities municipal water rather than private wells, and where groundwater is tapped, it generally comes from the Bunker Hill and Riverside-Arlington alluvial groundwater basins. Those are deep beds of sand, gravel, and clay laid down over geologic time, not the cracked, solid bedrock that hydrofracturing was invented to treat.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works, and Why Rock Matters

Hydrofracturing (sometimes called hydrofracking, though it is nothing like oil-and-gas fracking and uses only clean water) injects water at high pressure, typically well over a thousand PSI, into a sealed-off section of a well. The goal is to open and connect existing hairline fractures in solid rock so groundwater that was trapped in those cracks can finally flow into the borehole. It works because hard rock holds its shape: a fracture you open stays open, forming a new plumbing pathway.

Now picture Riverside's deep alluvial basin instead. The aquifer here is loose sand and gravel with layers of clay. There are no rock fractures to open. Push high-pressure water into sand and you do not create a lasting channel, you just disturb material that immediately slumps back together. Worse, you can collapse the sand pack around your screen, drive fines into the formation, and leave the well producing less water than before. This is the core reason we tell most Riverside-area owners that hydrofracturing is a poor fit: the method needs rock, and the basin is sediment.

Common Well Problems in Riverside's Alluvial Setting, and the Right Fixes

When an alluvial-basin well slows down, the cause is almost never "not enough fractures." It is usually one of these, and each has a proven remedy that has nothing to do with hydrofracturing:

  • Screen incrustation and mineral scale — Calcium, iron, and manganese minerals build up on the well screen and plug the slots. The fix is rehabilitation: acidizing to dissolve the scale, mechanical brushing of the casing and screen, and surging or jetting to pull the loosened material out. This frequently restores much of the lost yield at a fraction of the cost of a new well.
  • Biofouling — Iron bacteria and other organisms form slime that clogs the screen and gravel pack, often producing rotten-egg odor or reddish slime. Rehabilitation with the right chemical treatment plus aggressive surging knocks it back; periodic maintenance keeps it from returning.
  • Sand pumping — Grit in your water and wear on the pump usually means a screen or gravel-pack problem, or a pump set too low. The answer is screen/pack repair, correct pump placement, or in some cases a properly designed new well, not fracturing.
  • Declining static water level — If the whole basin's water table has dropped (common during dry stretches), no amount of fracturing helps. The realistic options are deepening the existing well, drilling a new, deeper well, or lowering and resizing the pump.
  • Water quality issues — Sediment, hardness, iron, and manganese are treatment problems, solved at the surface with filtration and softening, not down the borehole.

In short, for a Riverside alluvial well the high-value moves are well rehabilitation, well deepening, a properly engineered new well, and water treatment. Hydrofracturing sits at the bottom of that list and is relevant only in narrow cases described below.

The Rare Case Where Hydrofracturing Could Apply Near Riverside

There are pockets where the conversation changes. If your property sits on the harder bedrock margins, up in the foothills, or along the crystalline rock that fringes the basin rather than in the deep sand-and-gravel center, your well may actually draw from fractured rock. In that specific situation, hydrofracturing can be worth evaluating. The honest test is simple: what does your well completion report say you are drilled into? Fractured granite or hard rock, maybe. Sand and gravel, almost certainly not. A reputable contractor will read that report and tell you the truth instead of selling you a service that the geology will defeat.

What to Check Before You Call Anyone

  • Your well log / completion report. It lists the formation, depth, and screen interval. This single document usually settles whether you are in alluvium or rock.
  • When the slowdown started. A gradual decline over years points to incrustation or biofouling. A sudden drop points to pump or pressure-system failure.
  • Whether neighbors are affected too. If everyone's static level dropped, it is a basin-wide water-table issue, not your screen.
  • Water appearance. Reddish slime suggests iron bacteria; gritty water suggests sand intrusion; cloudy or scaling water suggests mineral content.
  • Pump behavior. Short-cycling, air spitting, or running dry tells us whether the problem is the well, the pump, or the pressure tank.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing right for a Riverside well?

For most wells in and around the City of Riverside, no. The city sits over deep alluvial sand-and-gravel basins, and hydrofracturing only works in fractured rock. In sediment it can actually reduce yield by disturbing the sand pack. It is worth considering only if your well is completed in hard bedrock, typically on the foothill or basin-margin areas. Rehabilitation, deepening, a new well, or treatment are usually the better answers here.

My well is producing less water. What should I do first?

Start with a diagnostic. A flow test and camera inspection will reveal whether the cause is screen incrustation, biofouling, a dropping water table, or a pump problem. Each has a specific fix, and identifying the right one prevents wasted spending.

What is the most common fix for a slowing Riverside well?

Well rehabilitation, meaning acidizing, brushing, and surging to clear mineral scale and biofouling off the screen, is the most common and most cost-effective remedy for alluvial-basin wells in this area.

Could hydrofracturing damage my well?

In loose sand and gravel, yes, it can. High-pressure injection can collapse the sand pack and push fines into the formation, leaving the well producing less than before. That risk is exactly why we do not recommend it for typical Riverside basin wells.

How do I know what my well is drilled into?

Check your well completion report or driller's log, which lists the formation and screen interval. If it shows sand, gravel, and clay, you are in alluvium. If it shows fractured granite or hard rock, you may be a candidate for fracturing. We are happy to read it with you.
