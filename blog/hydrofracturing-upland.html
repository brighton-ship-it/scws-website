What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Upland Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Upland wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Wells in the Upland Foothills

Upland sits at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains in the western San Bernardino County foothills, with Mt. Baldy rising to the north and neighbors in Rancho Cucamonga, Claremont, and Alta Loma all around. The bulk of Upland is on municipal water through the City of Upland and Cucamonga Valley Water District, but as you climb toward San Antonio Heights and the northern foothill neighborhoods, you reach properties on larger lots, some on private wells, where the question of well yield becomes real. For those foothill wells, hydrofracturing can be a worthwhile option, though Upland's geology makes it a moderate rather than a slam-dunk fit, and we will be straight with you about that.

The reason for the qualified answer is the ground itself. Upland is built on the broad alluvial fan that spills out of San Antonio Canyon, a deep apron of sand, gravel, and cobble washed down from the San Gabriels over millennia. Where a well draws from that loose alluvial material, hydrofracturing has limited value, because there are no rock fractures to open. But the higher foothill wells often reach down to, or are collared in, the granitic and metamorphic basement rock of the mountains, and those wells can respond well to high-pressure treatment. Knowing which situation you have is the whole point of an assessment.

How Hydrofracturing Works

When a well is finished in rock, hydrofracturing injects clean water at high pressure, commonly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, into an isolated section of the borehole. The pressure flushes sediment and mineral scale from the existing fractures and extends them outward to tap water-bearing seams the well did not originally reach. In hard foothill bedrock this can multiply yield. In loose alluvium it cannot, which is why the formation your well sits in matters so much in Upland specifically.

Why Upland's Geology Calls for an Honest Look

It is worth spending a moment on what lies beneath Upland, because it is the single biggest factor in whether hydrofracturing will help you. San Antonio Canyon, the great cleft below Mt. Baldy, has been dumping rock and sediment onto the valley floor for hundreds of thousands of years, building the alluvial fan that most of the city is built on. Near the canyon mouth and up in the foothill neighborhoods, that fan is coarse, with cobbles and gravel, and the granitic mountain rock is not far below. Farther out toward the valley, the deposits grade into finer sand and silt and run very deep. A domestic well in Upland could be tapping any part of that range. The high foothill wells that reach bedrock are the ones where high-pressure fracturing pays off, because there are genuine rock fractures to clean and extend. The deeper basin wells that pump from saturated sand are producing from pore space between grains, not from cracks, and no amount of pressure will open fractures that the formation does not contain. We would rather explain this distinction plainly than sell a treatment that the ground will not reward, and it is the reason an Upland assessment always starts with the well log and the geology rather than with a sales pitch.

Common Causes of Low Yield in the Upland Area

  • Declining regional water table. The Chino and Cucamonga groundwater basins beneath the valley have seen long-term pumping pressure, and dry years drop levels further, leaving shallower wells short.
  • Sediment-clogged screens. Alluvial wells draw fine sand and silt that can plug the well screen over time, mimicking a low-yield aquifer when the real issue is a blocked intake.
  • Rock fractures silting up. Foothill wells finished in granite suffer the same fracture clogging seen elsewhere in the mountains, and these are the strong hydrofracturing candidates.
  • An undersized or worn pump. Sometimes the well is fine and the equipment is the bottleneck.

When Hydrofracturing Fits, and When It Doesn't

For an Upland foothill well drilled into the granitic basement of the San Gabriels, hydrofracturing is a sound, cost-effective step to try before drilling new. For a well that bottoms in the alluvial fan sand and gravel, it is usually the wrong tool, and a better answer is well screen rehabilitation, deepening, or in some cases a new well sited and screened for the basin. We determine which by reviewing your well log and, when needed, running a video inspection and a yield test. This honest sorting is exactly why it pays to bring in a licensed contractor rather than assuming hydrofracturing is automatically the fix.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Find your well completion report if you have it. It tells us the depth and whether the well is screened in alluvium or open-hole in rock, which drives the recommendation.
  • Watch your pressure tank and switch. Short, rapid pump cycling is usually an equipment issue, not the aquifer.
  • Note whether you produce sand or cloudy water, a classic sign of a failing screen in alluvial ground.
  • Track whether shortages track the dry season, pointing to a falling water table rather than a mechanical fault.

In the Upland area, hydrofracturing a suitable rock well typically runs $3,000 to $8,000. A turnkey new well runs $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth and basin conditions. Related work includes pump replacement ($2,500–$5,500), pressure switch ($150–$350), pressure tank ($600–$1,500), sediment filtration ($300–$900), which alluvial-fan wells often need, iron or manganese treatment and softeners ($1,500–$3,500), and constant-pressure or booster systems ($2,000–$4,500).

What an Upland Assessment Involves

When we come out, the first thing we want is your well completion report, the document filed when the well was drilled that records its depth, the materials encountered, and how it was finished. That single sheet often answers the rock-versus-alluvium question on its own. If it is missing, a downhole video survey lets us see the casing, any screen, and the formation directly. We pair that with a flow and recovery test to measure how the well performs under load and how quickly it bounces back. Only then do we make a recommendation. For a rock-finished foothill well that is silting up, that recommendation is frequently hydrofracturing. For an alluvial well producing sand, it is more likely a screen cleaning, a filtration upgrade, or a new well engineered for the basin. The point is that you get a decision grounded in your specific well, not a generic prescription, and that is the most valuable thing a licensed contractor brings to an area as geologically mixed as Upland.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing a good fit for an Upland well?

It depends on your geology. Foothill wells finished in the granitic basement rock of the San Gabriels respond well; wells in the alluvial fan sand and gravel generally do not. We check your well log to advise honestly.

How do I know if my well is in rock or alluvium?

Your well completion report usually states the formation and whether the well is screened. If you do not have it, a video inspection can clarify the construction.

My water has sand in it. Will hydrofracturing fix that?

Probably not. Sand usually indicates a failing screen in alluvial ground, which calls for screen rehabilitation or filtration rather than fracturing.

How much can yield improve if my well is a good candidate?

In suitable foothill rock, two to five times is common. We document a before-and-after flow test so you can see the result.

What if my well is not a candidate?

We will recommend the right alternative, such as screen cleaning, deepening, or a new well, and your diagnostic fee is credited toward that work.

Do you handle the whole job?

Yes. As a full-service C-57 contractor we handle diagnosis, hydrofracturing, pumps, tanks, filtration, and new wells, so you deal with one licensed company.
