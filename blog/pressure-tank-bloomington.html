Pressure Tank Service for Bloomington Wells

Bloomington sits on the west side of San Bernardino County at 1,100 feet elevation, nestled between Colton and Rialto. The local geology — Santa Ana River alluvium over San Bernardino Basin sediments — creates productive but high-maintenance aquifers. Wells here typically range from 150 to 400 feet deep, drawing from layered alluvial deposits recharged by mountain runoff and percolation from seasonal storms.

Pressure tanks in Bloomington face unique challenges: hard water with high TDS (300-600 ppm), iron and manganese staining, and occasional sand intrusion from shallow alluvial layers. Urban well owners also deal with proximity to the I-10 freeway and industrial zones, which can stress aquifers through overdraft and contamination risks.

How Pressure Tanks Work

Your pressure tank stores water under pressure so your pump doesn't have to run every time you open a faucet. Inside the tank, a rubber bladder separates water from compressed air. As the pump fills the tank, it compresses the air, building pressure. When you turn on a tap, stored pressure pushes water out — no pump needed until the tank drains down to its cut-in pressure (usually 40 PSI).

Standard pressure settings: Cut-in 40 PSI, cut-off 60 PSI. Your pump runs 30-90 seconds to refill the tank, then shuts off. This cycle should happen every few hours of normal use — not every few minutes.

Common Pressure Tank Problems in Bloomington

Based on years of service in San Bernardino County's urban well zones, here's what we see most:

1. Waterlogged Tank (Bladder Failure)

Symptom: Pump cycles every 15-30 seconds, even with minimal water use. No air cushion left.

Cause: The rubber bladder inside tears or deteriorates (typical after 7-12 years). Water fills the entire tank, eliminating the air cushion that provides storage capacity.

Solution: Replace the tank. Bladders aren't serviceable in modern tanks — the entire unit must be replaced.

2. Low Air Pressure

Symptom: Pump cycles frequently (but not as extreme as waterlogged). Pressure gauge swings widely between cuts.

Cause: Air charge leaks out over time through the valve stem or bladder permeation.

Solution: Recharge air to 2 PSI below cut-in pressure (38 PSI if cut-in is 40). Use a standard tire pump on the Schrader valve at top of tank. Important: Turn off pump and drain tank completely first — recharging under water pressure can rupture the bladder.

3. Sediment/Sand Intrusion

Symptom: Reduced flow, gritty/brown water, sluggish system response.

Cause: Shallow Bloomington wells often produce fine sand or silt from alluvial layers. Without filtration, sediment accumulates in the tank and pipes.

Solution: Install a sediment filter (5-10 micron) before the tank. Replace tank if heavily contaminated — sediment can't be flushed from bladder-type tanks.

4. Iron/Manganese Staining

Symptom: Orange/brown stains on fixtures, rust-colored water after pump runs.

Cause: High iron content in Santa Ana Basin aquifers (common in Bloomington/Colton area). Iron precipitates out when exposed to air in the tank.

Solution: Install iron filter or oxidation system before the tank. For severe cases, consider chlorination + filtration.

5. Pressure Switch Failure

Symptom: Pump won't turn on/off at correct pressures, or won't turn on at all.

Cause: Mineral deposits clog the pressure port, or contacts corrode from humidity.

Solution: Clean or replace switch. New switches cost $20-50; replacement takes 15-20 minutes.

Installation and Replacement

Tank replacement is straightforward for a licensed contractor but involves risks if done incorrectly.

Installation Steps:

  1. Shut off pump at breaker (240V shock hazard).
  2. Drain tank via hose bib or drain valve.
  3. Disconnect plumbing — unions or threaded connections.
  4. Remove old tank and inspect mounting area for rust/leaks.
  5. Install new tank — use thread sealant (not tape) on NPT fittings.
  6. Reconnect plumbing and pressure switch.
  7. Charge air to 2 PSI below cut-in (38 PSI for 40/60 system).
  8. Test system — open taps, check for leaks, verify pressure cycles.

Labor time: 1-2 hours for standard replacement. Add time for plumbing upgrades or difficult access locations.

Preventive Maintenance

Regular maintenance extends tank life and prevents failures:

  • Check air pressure quarterly — recharge as needed to maintain 2 PSI below cut-in.
  • Inspect for leaks — fittings, valve stem, pressure relief valve.
  • Test pressure switch annually — clean contacts, adjust settings if needed.
  • Monitor for short cycling — if pump runs every few minutes, diagnose immediately.
  • Flush sediment filter monthly (if installed) to prevent clogs.
  • Drain tank annually — removes accumulated sediment.

Replace vs. Repair Decision

Replace if:

  • Tank is waterlogged (bladder failure)
  • Tank is 10+ years old
  • Visible rust or corrosion on tank body
  • Tank undersized for your pump
  • Chronic failures or recurring problems

Repair if:

  • Tank just needs air recharge
  • Pressure switch failed but tank is fine
  • Simple plumbing leak at a fitting
  • Tank is less than 5 years old with minor issue

Modern tanks are sealed units — bladder replacement isn't an option.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do pressure tanks last in Bloomington?

Expect 8-12 years with proper maintenance. Hard water and high TDS in Bloomington can shorten lifespan if untreated. Quality brands (Amtrol, Goulds) last longer than budget models.

Can I install a pressure tank myself?

Technically yes if you're experienced with plumbing and 240V electrical. However, mistakes risk water damage, pump burnout, or shock hazards. Licensed contractors carry insurance and guarantee proper installation.

What size pressure tank do I need?

Depends on your pump's flow rate. A 1 HP pump (12-15 GPM) needs a 60-80 gallon tank minimum. Undersized tanks cause short cycling and premature pump failure.

Why is my pump running every 30 seconds?

Either your tank is waterlogged (bladder failed) or has lost its air charge. Check air pressure first — it's a cheap fix. If no air holds, the bladder is torn and the tank needs replacement.

How much does a new pressure tank cost in Bloomington?

Installed: $400-1,200 depending on size. A 60-gallon Amtrol costs around $350 for the tank plus $200-400 labor. Emergency service adds $100-200.

Can a pressure tank explode?

Extremely rare with modern tanks. Pressure relief valves prevent over-pressurization. Old tanks without relief valves (pre-1990s) can rupture, but this is nearly impossible with current equipment.

What air pressure should my tank have?

2 PSI below your cut-in pressure. If your system is 40/60 (cut-in 40 PSI, cut-off 60 PSI), set air to 38 PSI. Check with pump OFF and tank drained.

Do I need a sediment filter before my pressure tank?

Yes, if your well produces sand or silt (common in shallow Bloomington alluvial aquifers). A 5-10 micron filter protects the tank, pump, and fixtures from abrasive particles.

How often should I check my pressure tank?

Quarterly. Check air pressure, inspect for leaks, test pressure switch operation. Takes 10 minutes and catches problems early.

Will a bigger tank reduce my electric bill?

Slightly — maybe $3-5/month. Larger tanks reduce pump cycle frequency, saving a bit on electricity. The main benefit is longer pump life — frequent cycling (short-cycling) kills pumps fast.
