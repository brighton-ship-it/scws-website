How Your Pressure Tank Works

Your pressure tank is one of the most critical components of your well system — and one of the most overlooked. It serves as the buffer between your well pump and your household plumbing, storing pressurized water so the pump doesn't have to turn on every time you open a faucet or flush a toilet.

Inside a modern bladder-type pressure tank, a rubber bladder separates the water from a pre-charged air pocket. When the pump fills the tank, it compresses the air pocket, building pressure throughout your plumbing. When you open a faucet, the compressed air pushes water out of the tank and into your pipes. The pump stays off until pressure drops to a preset low point — typically 30 PSI for a standard 30/50 system — then kicks on to refill the tank.

This cycle is called the pump duty cycle, and it's critical to pump longevity. A properly sized pressure tank keeps pump starts to a minimum, typically 6 to 8 cycles per hour during peak use. Without a functioning pressure tank, your pump would start and stop with every glass of water, every toilet flush, every shower. That rapid cycling — called short cycling — burns out pump motors, damages wiring, stresses pipe connections, and wastes electricity.

In the Birdland area, where many homes were built between the 1950s and 1970s, we frequently encounter older diaphragm-style tanks or even galvanized steel tanks that have been in service for decades. While these tanks may still function, they're far less efficient than modern bladder tanks and are typically well past their expected service life.

Signs Your Pressure Tank Is Failing

Pressure tank failures rarely happen all at once. More often, they degrade gradually, and homeowners grow accustomed to the symptoms before realizing there's a real problem. Here are the warning signs we see most often when servicing pressure tanks in the Birdland area:

  • Pump short cycling: Your pump turns on and off every few seconds or every time water is used. This is the single most common sign of a waterlogged pressure tank. The air charge has leaked out, the bladder has ruptured, or the tank is otherwise unable to store pressurized water. Short cycling is extremely hard on pump motors and will significantly shorten pump life if not addressed.
  • Fluctuating water pressure: Pressure surges when the pump kicks on, then drops quickly. You might notice it as a pulsing effect in the shower or pressure that's strong for a moment, then weakens before the pump starts again.
  • Waterlogged tank: Tap on the side of your tank from top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow near the top (where the air pocket is) and solid near the bottom (where water sits). A waterlogged tank sounds uniformly solid from top to bottom — meaning it's completely full of water with no air cushion left.
  • Visible rust or corrosion: San Diego's coastal-influenced air can accelerate corrosion on outdoor equipment. Look for rust on the tank exterior, at pipe connections, or weeping from seams — any of these indicate compromised tank integrity.
  • Leaking from the tank body: Any water dripping from the tank itself (not pipe connections) means the tank shell has corroded through and needs immediate replacement. Do not attempt to patch a leaking pressure tank.
  • Air spurting from faucets: If you get bursts of air mixed with water when you open a tap, the bladder inside the tank may have ruptured, allowing the air charge to mix directly with the water supply.
  • Higher-than-normal electric bills: A failing pressure tank forces the pump to run far more often than necessary. If your electric bill has crept up without an obvious explanation, your pressure tank may be the cause.

Pressure Tank Sizing Guide

One of the most common mistakes we see is undersized pressure tanks. A tank that's too small for your household causes excessive pump cycling, which shortens pump life and wastes energy. Here's how we determine the right tank size for Birdland-area homes:

The goal is to limit pump cycling to no more than 6 to 8 starts per hour during peak demand. To calculate the right drawdown capacity, you need to know your pump's flow rate and your household's peak demand. As a general guideline:

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: 30 to 50 gallon tank (minimum). A 44-gallon Well-X-Trol WX-203 is our standard recommendation for smaller Birdland homes with modest water demands.
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: 50 to 85 gallon tank. The WX-250 (62 gallon) or WX-302 (86 gallon) handles the higher demand of larger households with multiple bathrooms running simultaneously.
  • Properties with irrigation wells: 85+ gallon tank, or a dedicated second tank for the irrigation circuit. Running landscape irrigation off a small residential pressure tank is a common cause of premature pump failure in the Birdland area.
  • Dual-purpose systems: If your well supplies both household water and irrigation, we typically recommend a larger tank (85+ gallons) or a parallel tank configuration to handle the combined demand without overworking the pump.

Keep in mind that a pressure tank's rated size is not the same as its drawdown capacity. A 50-gallon tank on a 30/50 pressure system actually delivers only about 14 to 16 gallons of water between pump cycles. That's why seemingly "large" tanks are necessary even for modest households. We calculate the exact drawdown needed based on your pump's GPM rating and your peak usage patterns.

Pressure Tank Maintenance Tips

Regular maintenance extends the life of your pressure tank and protects your pump from the damage caused by undetected tank failure. Here's what we recommend for Birdland-area well owners:

  • Check air charge every 6 months: Turn off the pump, drain the tank completely, then check the air pressure at the Schrader valve on top of the tank with a standard tire gauge. It should read 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (28 PSI for a 30/50 system). Add air with a bicycle pump or small compressor if it's low. Never check air pressure with the tank full of water — you'll get a false reading.
  • Listen for short cycling: Stand near your pressure tank and run water in the house. If you hear the pump clicking on and off more than once per minute, your tank likely needs service. You can also watch the pressure gauge — healthy systems show a steady drop from cut-out to cut-in pressure, not a rapid bounce.
  • Inspect for corrosion regularly: Check pipe connections, the tank base, and the area around the Schrader valve for rust, mineral buildup, or moisture. San Diego's coastal humidity can accelerate corrosion on outdoor equipment, especially in areas with morning fog.
  • Keep the area around the tank clear: In tight Birdland utility spaces, it's tempting to store items around the pressure tank. Keep at least 12 inches of clearance on all sides for ventilation, access, and to spot leaks early.
  • Schedule annual professional inspection: A trained technician can catch problems you might miss — subtle signs of bladder degradation, pressure switch wear, or wiring issues that could cause an unexpected failure. Annual inspections are especially important for tanks over 8 years old.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size pressure tank do I need?

For most residential wells in the Birdland area, we recommend a minimum 30-gallon pressure tank. Homes with higher water demand or multiple bathrooms benefit from 50-85 gallon tanks. If your well also supplies irrigation, a larger tank or dual-tank setup prevents excessive pump cycling. Proper sizing reduces pump starts and extends pump life significantly.

How do I know if my pressure tank is failing?

The most common signs of a failing pressure tank include: pump short cycling (turning on and off every few seconds), a waterlogged tank (sounds solid when tapped instead of hollow near the top), fluctuating water pressure, air bursts from faucets, and visible rust or leaking from the tank body. If you notice any of these symptoms, call us for a diagnostic before the problem damages your pump.

How long do pressure tanks last?

Quality bladder-type pressure tanks (like Well-X-Trol) typically last 10 to 15 years with proper maintenance. Older diaphragm-style tanks may last less. In the San Diego climate, outdoor tanks exposed to sun and coastal air may need replacement sooner. Annual air charge checks and professional inspections can extend tank life and catch problems before they lead to pump damage.

Can I replace a pressure tank myself?

While it's physically possible for a handy homeowner to swap a pressure tank, we recommend professional installation for several reasons. Improper sizing is the most common DIY mistake — an undersized tank will destroy your pump over time. The air pre-charge must be set precisely, the pressure switch settings need to match, and the electrical connections must be safe and code-compliant. A professional installation also ensures the old tank is disposed of properly.
