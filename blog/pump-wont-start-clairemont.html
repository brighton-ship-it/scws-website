Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Common Reasons a Well Pump Won't Start

When a Clairemont well pump stays silent, the cause is usually one of these:

  • Tripped breaker or blown fuse — no power to the pump. The most frequent reason of all. A dedicated pump breaker can trip from a surge, a loose connection, or a failing motor.
  • Failed pressure switch or clogged sensing port. The pressure switch signals the pump to start. On older legacy wells the small sensing port can clog with scale or sediment, so the pump never gets the command to run.
  • Burned-out capacitor or control box fault. Many surface and submersible pumps use a start capacitor and relay. Capacitors weaken with age and are a leading no-start cause on decades-old Clairemont systems.
  • Failed or seized motor or pump. On legacy wells that have run for years, worn bearings or a shorted motor winding can finally lock the pump up.
  • Wiring and corrosion faults. Coastal-influenced air in the Bay Park and Mission Bay corridor accelerates corrosion at terminals, junction boxes, and splices, interrupting the circuit.
  • Stuck check valve. A check valve jammed closed can stall the pump or trip its overload.
  • Waterlogged or air-bound pressure tank. A tank that has lost its air charge short-cycles the pump and can trip protective controls until it appears dead.
  • Power problems. Even in an urban setting, an old service panel, an undersized circuit, or a backfed surge can starve a pump of the steady voltage it needs to start.

Safety-First Steps Before You Call

Warning: well pumps operate on 240 volts, which is more than enough to cause serious injury or death. Do not open a control box or touch wiring while the power is on. Follow these steps in order:

  1. Turn the pump breaker OFF at the main panel first. Always kill the power before inspecting anything.
  2. Examine the breaker. If it sits in the tripped middle position, reset it ONCE — fully off, then back on.
  3. If it trips again right away, stop. Leave it off and call a professional; repeated tripping means a short or failing motor and a fire risk.
  4. Read your pressure tank gauge. A gauge stuck at zero, or far above the normal cut-out pressure, is a useful clue to report.
  5. Listen at the pressure switch for a click when water should be demanded. No click suggests a switch or control failure.
  6. Inspect any accessible wiring and conduit for burns, melted insulation, corrosion, or a scorched smell — all reasons to keep the power off and call.
  7. Verify the rest of the house has power so you can tell a pump fault from a property-wide outage.

Electrical Causes Worth Understanding

Clairemont's legacy wells were often installed and wired decades ago, and electrical aging is one of the biggest no-start factors we see. A three-wire submersible pump keeps its start capacitor and relay in a surface control box, which is the easiest and least expensive place to find and fix a fault. A two-wire pump has those components built into the motor downhole, so a failure there means pulling the pump. A motor that hums but won't turn usually points to a weak capacitor or low voltage, not necessarily a dead motor — and confirming that with a meter prevents an unnecessary, costly pull. We measure incoming voltage, motor winding insulation resistance, and amperage on every no-start diagnostic, because in an older urban setting a corroded terminal or a tired capacitor is frequently the real culprit rather than the pump itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm in Clairemont — why would I even have a private well?

While most of Clairemont is on city water, a number of older and canyon-edge San Diego County parcels still have legacy private or shared wells. If your water comes from a pump rather than a meter, the same diagnostic steps apply when it won't start.

Why won't my pump start when the rest of the house has power?

The pump runs on its own dedicated circuit. A tripped pump breaker, a failed pressure switch, or a bad capacitor can keep the pump silent while every other circuit in the home works normally. Check the dedicated pump breaker first.

Can I reset the pump breaker myself?

Yes, once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us. Repeated tripping points to a short or a failing motor and forcing it risks an electrical fire.

Does coastal air really affect my well equipment?

It can. Salt-influenced air in the Bay Park and Mission Bay corridor accelerates corrosion at terminals, junction boxes, and splices, which can interrupt the circuit and prevent the pump from starting.
