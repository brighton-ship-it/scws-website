What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Sunset Cliffs Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Wells in Sunset Cliffs

Sunset Cliffs is a coastal neighborhood on the Point Loma peninsula in the City of San Diego, San Diego County — famous for the dramatic sandstone bluffs of Sunset Cliffs Natural Park, the sweeping Pacific views along Sunset Cliffs Boulevard, and its tight-knit residential streets above Ocean Beach. It is an established, fully urbanized, ocean-side community served entirely by the City of San Diego's municipal water supply. Because of that, we will lead with the candid truth: for the typical Sunset Cliffs homeowner, hydrofracturing is not a relevant service, because there is essentially no private well here to fracture.

Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation method for private wells finished in hard, fractured bedrock. If your home draws water from a city meter — as virtually every Point Loma property does — then a pressure or flow problem is a plumbing or municipal-system issue, not a well matter. We would rather tell you that honestly than sell a service that has nothing to do with your situation. Here is the full picture.

The Geology of the Point Loma Peninsula

The cliffs that give Sunset Cliffs its name are a geology lesson in plain sight. The bluffs above the surf are layered marine sedimentary rock — the Point Loma Formation, a soft sandstone and siltstone laid down on an ancient seafloor and later uplifted to form the peninsula. You can watch the ocean carving sea caves and arches into it, which tells you something important: this is relatively soft, layered rock, not the hard, brittle granite that fractures cleanly and stores water in a network of seams.

Hydrofracturing depends on that brittle, fractured hard rock. The high-pressure water has to have an existing fracture system to flush open and extend. In soft marine sandstone there is little of that to work with, and the groundwater picture is further complicated by the immediate proximity of the Pacific, where pumping near the coast can pull saltwater into a well. Between the municipal water service, the sedimentary geology, and the seawater-intrusion risk, Sunset Cliffs lines up as one of the least suitable settings for the technique that we cover — the near-opposite of an inland granite parcel.

What Hydrofracturing Does

Hydrofracturing (or hydrofracking) injects high-pressure clean water — no chemicals — into a private well to reopen and extend the natural fractures in the surrounding bedrock. In rural hard-rock country, a well produces through those fractures, and they gradually clog with sediment and mineral scale, cutting yield. Pressurizing the borehole at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI clears and extends the fractures, often dramatically improving a struggling well for a fraction of the cost of drilling a new one. The method only makes sense, though, when two things are present: a private well, and fractured rock around it.

Why Sunset Cliffs Is a Weak Fit

  • Municipal water service. Sunset Cliffs and all of Point Loma are on the City of San Diego water system. There are no private domestic wells to treat.
  • Coastal sedimentary geology. The peninsula is built of marine sedimentary rock — the soft Point Loma sandstone and related formations that form the cliffs — not the fractured granite hydrofracturing needs.
  • Seawater intrusion risk. Even if a coastal well existed, groundwater this close to the ocean is prone to saltwater intrusion, making it unsuitable for the kind of yield development fracturing provides.
  • Dense urban lots. The built-out, view-oriented neighborhood leaves no practical place or reason for private wells.

The technique is outstanding inland on granite. On a coastal sandstone peninsula on city water, it simply does not apply.

Where Hydrofracturing Truly Belongs

To put Sunset Cliffs in context, the technique earns its reputation about 40 miles inland, in the granitic foothills of East County — places like Ramona, Julian, Alpine, and the rural valleys where homes genuinely depend on private hard-rock wells. There, a clogged or marginal well can often be restored to two to five times its flow through fracturing, saving an owner the much larger cost of a new well. That is the right home for the service. We mention it so you understand we are not dismissing the method — only being straight about the fact that a coastal, city-served neighborhood like Sunset Cliffs is not where it applies.

On City Water? What to Do Instead

For a Sunset Cliffs household, water complaints almost always come down to these, none of which involve a well:

  • Low pressure at fixtures. Frequently a failing pressure-reducing valve, a partially closed shutoff, a clogged aerator, or aging supply lines — especially in older Point Loma homes. A licensed plumber is the right first call.
  • Neighborhood-wide pressure changes. Contact the City of San Diego; coastal areas sometimes see pressure-zone or main-related fluctuations.
  • Salt air, hardness, or taste. A water-treatment specialist can install filtration or softening. We provide treatment systems even where there is no well.

It would be unusual, but if you own a property near Sunset Cliffs with a genuine private well that has lost yield, we would still give you an honest assessment: a baseline flow test, a downhole camera survey to see whether the well is in rock or sediment, and a frank recommendation. Given the coastal sandstone and the seawater-intrusion risk, the realistic advice would more likely point toward treatment, a different water source, or connecting to the municipal supply than toward fracturing — and we would say so.

What to Check Before Assuming It Is a Well

A quick self-check saves time and money. Confirm your water source first: a meter at the property line and a City of San Diego water bill mean you are on the municipal system and have no well. Then test several fixtures — a single weak faucet is usually a clogged aerator or a corroded supply line, not a supply problem. If every fixture is weak, look for a pressure-reducing valve near your main shutoff, since these wear out over time and are a routine plumbing fix. Coastal Point Loma homes also tend to be older, so galvanized piping and aging fixtures are common culprits. And if neighbors notice the same change at once, it is a city-system matter. In none of these cases would hydrofracturing be the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there private wells in Sunset Cliffs?

Essentially none. Sunset Cliffs and Point Loma are served by City of San Diego municipal water, so homes do not rely on private wells.

My water pressure dropped — do I need hydrofracturing?

No. On city water, that is a plumbing or municipal issue. Check your home's pressure-reducing valve and shutoff valves, or contact the city. Hydrofracturing only applies to private wells.

Could the coastal geology ever support hydrofracturing?

Not well. The peninsula's soft marine sandstone lacks the hard fractures the technique relies on, and coastal groundwater faces seawater-intrusion problems.

Can you help with hard or salty-tasting water?

Yes. We install filtration and softening systems for homes whether or not they have a well.

What should I do first?

Confirm whether you are on city water or a well. City water issues go to a plumber or the city; the rare well issue comes to us.

Why tell me I do not need your main service?

Because honest guidance has built our reputation since 2020. We will not sell you something your property cannot use.
