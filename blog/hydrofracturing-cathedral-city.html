What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Coachella Valley Wells in Cathedral City

Cathedral City sits in the heart of the Coachella Valley, in eastern Riverside County, where private water wells draw from one of Southern California's most heavily relied-upon groundwater systems. Most of the valley is supplied by the Coachella Valley Groundwater Basin, often described as a tilted bathtub filled with sand, gravel, and clay. While the city itself is largely on district water, plenty of properties on the desert fringe, in the hills toward Cathedral Canyon, and on older parcels around the Whitewater drainage still depend on private and irrigation wells. When one of those wells starts producing less than it used to, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is frequently the most cost-effective way to restore flow before anyone considers the major expense of drilling a replacement well.

Unlike the deep alluvial wells on the valley floor, many Cathedral City wells that benefit most from hydrofracturing are those drilled into the harder, fractured rock along the toe of the surrounding mountains. In those formations, water moves through cracks and fractures rather than open sand. Over years of pumping, those fractures clog with fine sediment and mineral scale, and yield drops. Hydrofracturing reopens them.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a mechanical well-rehabilitation process that uses nothing but clean, high-pressure water. There are no chemicals involved, which matters a great deal in a basin where the U.S. Geological Survey has already flagged naturally occurring arsenic and fluoride in some domestic wells. The last thing a Coachella Valley well owner wants is to introduce a contaminant.

The process works like this:

  • Isolation with packers. We lower inflatable packers into the well casing and inflate them to seal off a specific section of the borehole. This concentrates all the pressure on one zone instead of letting it bleed off up and down the well.
  • High-pressure injection. Clean water is then pumped into the isolated zone at pressures typically ranging from 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure pushes into the existing fractures in the rock, flushing out the silt, sand, and mineral deposits that have been choking flow.
  • Opening and extending fractures. The pressure widens existing fractures and can connect the borehole to nearby water-bearing cracks that were previously isolated, giving groundwater new pathways to reach the well.
  • Development and testing. Once treatment is finished, we surge and pump the well to clear loosened material, then run a flow test to confirm the gain.

Why Cathedral City Wells Lose Yield

In this part of Riverside County, declining well performance usually comes down to a handful of local causes:

  • Mineral scaling. Desert groundwater is mineral-rich. Calcium and iron deposits slowly cement the fractures and perforations near the well intake.
  • Regional water-table decline. Decades of pumping across the valley have lowered groundwater levels in places, so a well that was once comfortably submerged now sits closer to the bottom of its water column.
  • Fine-sediment plugging. Wells in fractured bedrock at the valley margins gradually silt up as fine material migrates into the fracture network.
  • Seasonal demand spikes. Summer landscape and pool demand in the desert can outrun a marginal well's recovery rate, making a borderline well feel like it is failing in July even though it limps through winter.

Signs Your Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Before you call anyone, it helps to know what to look for. A well that is a good hydrofracturing candidate usually shows several of these signs:

  • Water production that has steadily dropped over the past few years, not just during one dry spell.
  • The well runs dry or sputters during peak summer use, then recovers overnight.
  • The pump short-cycles, kicking on and off more often than it used to.
  • Pressure at the house has fallen even though the pump and pressure tank test fine.
  • You have a relatively new well drilled into fractured rock that simply never produced the flow you expected.

It is worth ruling out simpler problems first. Low pressure caused by a failing pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a bad pressure switch is far cheaper to fix than a yield problem. A proper diagnostic separates a true low-yield well from a mechanical fault.

What to Check Before You Call

A little homework saves money. Before assuming your well needs hydrofracturing, walk through this short checklist:

  1. Track your usage. A new pool, expanded landscaping, or extra residents can outstrip a well that was always marginal. The well may not have changed, your demand did.
  2. Inspect the pressure tank. A waterlogged tank causes rapid pump cycling that mimics a low-yield well. Tap the tank; if it sounds solid all the way up, the air charge is gone.
  3. Listen to the pump. Frequent on-off cycling and air spitting from faucets often point to the pump drawing the water level down faster than the well recovers, a classic low-yield symptom.
  4. Note the timing. If the shortage only shows up in late summer and clears by winter, you likely have a recovery-rate problem that hydrofracturing addresses well.
  5. Check for sediment. Cloudy or gritty water can signal that fine material is migrating in the formation, the same material that clogs fractures over time.

If several of these point to a genuine yield problem rather than a mechanical fault, hydrofracturing is worth a serious look.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing is not a do-it-yourself job. It requires high-pressure equipment, packers sized to your casing, and the experience to read how a specific formation will respond. Call a licensed well contractor when your yield has clearly declined, when a flow test confirms the well is producing below what your household needs, or when you are weighing the cost of a new well against rehabilitating the one you have. A professional will also perform a video inspection of the casing first, because hydrofracturing is only appropriate when the casing is sound and the well is set in fractured rock rather than loose sand or gravel. In the Coachella Valley, where some wells are decades old and the desert heat is hard on equipment, that inspection step routinely catches casing corrosion or perforation damage that would otherwise be misdiagnosed as a formation problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil and gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure to clear and open existing fractures in shallow rock. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no deep shale formations involved. It is a long-established well-rehabilitation method, completely different from oil and gas extraction.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Cathedral City well?

It depends on your geology. Wells set in fractured rock at the valley margins respond well, often improving yield two to five times. Wells in loose sand or gravel on the valley floor are generally not good candidates. We inspect the casing and formation before recommending the treatment.

How long does the process take?

Most residential hydrofracturing jobs take a single day, with the actual high-pressure injection running roughly four to eight hours depending on how many zones we treat. We run a flow test afterward and usually confirm results within 24 to 48 hours.

Does hydrofracturing risk contaminating my water?

Because only clean water is used, the treatment itself introduces nothing harmful. In the Coachella Valley, where arsenic and fluoride can occur naturally, we recommend testing your water after any well work regardless of the cause, simply as good practice.

How much more water can I expect afterward?

In fractured-rock wells across the region, hydrofracturing typically improves yield somewhere between two and five times the pre-treatment flow. Results vary by well, which is why we measure your baseline yield before and after so you can see exactly what changed.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well in the Cathedral City area runs $18,000 to $42,000 or more. For most low-yield wells it is the smart first step.
