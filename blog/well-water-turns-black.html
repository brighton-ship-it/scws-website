Manganese: The Most Common Culprit

Manganese is a naturally occurring mineral found in many California aquifers. When dissolved in water, it's invisible. But when it oxidizes (contacts air), it turns black or dark brown. You might notice clear water that turns black after sitting, black staining in fixtures and laundry, or a gritty black sediment. Manganese is more common in deeper wells and areas with certain geological conditions. While small amounts are actually essential for health, the EPA recommends keeping levels below 0.05 mg/L for aesthetic reasons and 0.3 mg/L for health.

Iron Bacteria and Black Slime

Iron bacteria are organisms that feed on iron in groundwater, producing a dark, slimy residue. Unlike mineral manganese, iron bacteria create a gelatinous black or rusty slime in toilet tanks, pump screens, and pipes. You might notice a swampy or sulfur odor. Iron bacteria aren't typically harmful but can clog pipes and create an environment for more dangerous bacteria. They're introduced during drilling or pump service when equipment isn't properly sanitized. Treatment involves shock chlorinating the well and installing continuous disinfection.

Testing and Diagnosis

Before treating, get a comprehensive water test including: total iron, manganese, pH, hardness, and bacterial analysis. Note when discoloration occurs—immediately from tap (dissolved minerals) or after sitting (oxidizable minerals)? Is there an odor? Is slime present? Take photos for your water treatment professional. The test results guide treatment selection. Treating for the wrong contaminant wastes money and doesn't solve the problem. Expect to pay $100-200 for proper laboratory analysis.

Treatment Solutions

For dissolved manganese: an oxidizing filter (air injection, chlorine injection, or potassium permanganate feed) converts dissolved manganese to solid particles, then filtration removes them. Manganese greensand filters or catalytic carbon are effective media. For iron bacteria: shock chlorinate the well with 200 PPM chlorine solution, let sit 24 hours, flush thoroughly, then install continuous chlorination or UV disinfection to prevent recurrence. Some severe cases require a combination approach. Systems range from $1,500-4,000 installed depending on complexity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is black well water safe to drink?

Black water is usually caused by manganese or iron, which aren't immediately dangerous at typical well concentrations but aren't ideal for consumption either. The EPA secondary standard for manganese is 0.05 mg/L. High manganese may affect neurological health with long-term exposure. Get your water tested before drinking—the color could also indicate other contaminants. Use bottled water until you have test results.

What causes well water to suddenly turn black?

Sudden changes often occur after the pump disturbs sediment at the well bottom, during heavy pumping that draws from lower (more mineral-rich) zones, or after a power outage when settled minerals get stirred up. Changes in the aquifer chemistry, a failing well screen, or a dropped pump can also cause sudden discoloration. If the problem persists beyond a day of normal use, investigate further.

How do I remove black color from well water?

The treatment depends on the cause. For manganese: oxidation (air injection, chlorination, or ozone) followed by filtration through manganese greensand or birm media. For iron bacteria: shock chlorination of the well plus ongoing treatment. Water softeners don't effectively remove manganese. A proper water test identifying the specific cause is essential before investing in treatment equipment.
