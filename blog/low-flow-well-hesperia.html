Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Yield Wells in the High Desert: What Makes Hesperia Different

Hesperia sits in the Victor Valley, high on the southern rim of the Mojave Desert in San Bernardino County, and its groundwater story is very different from the mountain-front wells farther south. Here the water comes from the Mojave River Basin, an unconfined system made of two stacked aquifers: a shallow, highly productive floodplain aquifer near the river channel that can be up to about 200 feet thick, and a deeper regional aquifer beneath it that reaches as much as 1,000 feet. Most Hesperia domestic and small-farm wells are completed somewhere between roughly 200 and 600 feet, with solid casing running a few hundred feet down before the perforated section begins.

What ties every Hesperia well together is the desert itself. There is very little surface water up here, so groundwater does almost all the work — for homes, livestock, and irrigation alike. That heavy reliance, combined with long dry stretches and years of regional pumping, means water levels across the Victor Valley have declined in many areas over the decades. When your well starts delivering weak flow, the High Desert setting shapes both the likely cause and the smartest fix.

The Main Reasons Hesperia Wells Go Low

Weak flow at the tap is a symptom, not a diagnosis. On Hesperia properties the underlying reasons tend to fall into a handful of categories:

  • A declining regional water table. If your well draws from the deeper regional aquifer and the level has dropped below where your pump was originally set, the pump can no longer keep its intake submerged through a full day of use.
  • A pump set too high or now undersized. Many older Victor Valley wells were equipped when the water table was higher. As levels fell, pumps that were never re-set ended up too shallow, or too small for the extra lift now required.
  • Screen and perforation clogging. Mojave groundwater carries dissolved minerals, and over time scale plus iron-bacteria biofouling can seal the well screen, cutting off the flow even when water is present.
  • Sand and silt accumulation. The valley's sandy deposits migrate into a well over years, filling the bottom of the casing and burying the deepest, most productive perforations.
  • Drought-reduced recharge. With so little natural inflow in a desert basin, a few dry years noticeably lower the resource that feeds every well in the area.

Two Hesperia Situations We See Constantly

The older Oak Hills / Summit Valley homestead. A property that once ran comfortably on a 300-foot well starts losing pressure during the summer. Testing shows the static water level has fallen well below the pump intake as neighboring irrigation demand peaks. The well still has water — the pump just cannot reach it reliably anymore. Lowering the pump, or replacing it with a deeper-set unit, restores dependable flow.

The mineral-fouled ranch well. A small livestock operation on the east side of Hesperia reports flow that has slowly weakened over several seasons, along with reddish staining and a faint sulfur smell. Here the aquifer is fine; the screen is being choked by iron-bacteria slime and mineral scale. Mechanical brushing and a chemical treatment reopen the perforations and bring the yield back without touching the pump or drilling deeper.

How a Professional Yield Test Sorts It Out

Because "low flow" has so many possible causes, we never recommend a repair before measuring the well. Our evaluation follows a set sequence:

  1. Static water level. We measure the resting level to learn how much water the well is actually holding and how far it sits below the surface.
  2. Drawdown observation. With the pump running, we track how quickly the level drops. A steep drawdown points to a screen or aquifer limitation rather than a simple equipment fault.
  3. Recovery timing. After we stop pumping, how fast the well recovers tells us whether the formation can keep up with demand.
  4. Sustainable GPM. A proper yield test establishes the flow rate the well can hold indefinitely — the number that actually matters for sizing storage and pumps.
  5. Equipment check. We inspect pump depth and condition plus the pressure switch, tank, and control box, since any of those can imitate low yield.

Solutions Built for High-Desert Conditions

Once we know what the well is really doing, the fix follows the facts:

  • Reset or replace the pump. In a basin where water levels have dropped, setting the pump deeper — or installing a correctly sized submersible ($2,500-$5,500) — is often all it takes to restore steady flow.
  • Well rehabilitation. For a clogged screen, mechanical brushing followed by acid or chemical treatment reopens the perforations. On mineral-heavy Mojave water this is frequently the highest-value repair available.
  • Storage tank plus booster. The High Desert is ideal territory for a cistern strategy: a large tank fills slowly around the clock from a modest well, and a booster or constant-pressure system ($2,000-$4,500) delivers strong household pressure on demand. This turns a 2-3 GPM well into a reliable supply.
  • Deepen or drill new. When the well has silted in badly or the productive zone is exhausted, deepening or a turnkey new well ($18,000-$42,000) reaches fresh capacity. We reserve this for cases where rehabilitation and pump work cannot deliver enough.

Water quality gear often rides along with these repairs. Sediment filtration ($300-$900) protects a new pump from the valley's sand, a softener ($1,500-$3,500) tackles hardness, and where staining or odor is present, iron treatment keeps the system clean. A pressure switch ($150-$350) or pressure tank ($600-$1,500) replacement is a small cost that sometimes resolves an apparent flow problem outright.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are most wells in the Hesperia area?

Most domestic and small-farm wells in the Victor Valley are completed between roughly 200 and 600 feet, drawing from the Mojave River Basin's floodplain or deeper regional aquifer. Your original well log tells the exact depth, and we can pull the likely productive zones from it during an evaluation.

My pump runs but pressure keeps dropping — what does that mean?

That usually means the well is drawing down faster than it recovers, so the pump periodically loses its water supply. In the High Desert this often reflects a fallen regional water table combined with a pump set too shallow. A drawdown test confirms it, and lowering the pump or adding storage typically fixes it.

Why is my water suddenly rusty or smelly along with the weak flow?

Iron-bacteria biofouling produces both reddish staining and a sulfur odor while it slowly seals your well screen and chokes the flow. It is a common Mojave-water problem. Well rehabilitation clears the screen, and follow-up treatment keeps the staining and smell from returning.

Is a storage tank really better than deepening my well?

For a modest but steady producer, yes — a cistern and booster let a low-GPM well serve a full household without the cost of drilling. Deepening makes sense only when the well has silted in or the aquifer zone is genuinely exhausted, which our testing determines.

Could years of drought be the whole problem?

Drought lowers the water table across the Victor Valley, but it is rarely the only factor. Usually a dry stretch simply exposes a pump set too high or a partially clogged screen. We measure both the water level and the well's condition so the recommendation matches reality.
