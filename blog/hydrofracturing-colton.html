What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Colton Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Colton-Area Wells

Colton sits in the heart of the Inland Empire in San Bernardino County, wedged between the Santa Ana River, the Jurupa Hills, and the rising slopes of the San Bernardino Mountains to the north. The city itself is served largely by municipal water, but the surrounding area—Grand Terrace, Bloomington, the Reche Canyon corridor, and the rural parcels climbing toward the foothills—still relies on private and agricultural wells. Those wells draw from a mix of alluvial basin fill along the Santa Ana River and fractured bedrock where properties sit higher against the hills. When one of these wells starts losing yield, hydrofracturing (also spelled hydrofracking) is frequently the most economical way to bring it back without the cost of drilling a replacement.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates a well using clean, high-pressure water and nothing else. It is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking despite the shared name: there are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no additives—only water pressure applied where it is needed.

Our crew lowers inflatable packers into the well casing and inflates them to seal off a specific section of the borehole. With that interval isolated, we inject clean water at pressures typically between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. That pressure pushes water out into the fractures, joints, and water-bearing seams around the well, scouring out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have plugged them and widening the natural pathways so groundwater flows freely again. We treat one zone at a time, then run a flow test to confirm the yield improvement.

In hard-rock wells against the Colton hills, the technique reopens the fracture network the well depends on. In basin wells along the river, it clears the screen and gravel pack of the fine sediment and scale that accumulate over years of pumping. Either way, the goal is the same: restore the well's ability to pull water from the ground around it.

This dual nature is what makes local knowledge matter in Colton. A contractor who treats every well the same way will get inconsistent results, because a sediment-plugged basin well and a fracture-limited foothill well respond to pressure differently. We tailor the packer placement, pressure, and number of treatment zones to your specific well, which is why an accurate assessment up front is worth the time.

Why Colton-Area Wells Lose Yield

Across the Colton, Grand Terrace, and Reche Canyon area, declining wells usually share a handful of causes:

  • Mineral scale and incrustation. Inland Empire groundwater often carries dissolved calcium and carbonates that precipitate onto the screen and inside fractures, slowly choking flow.
  • Sediment plugging in basin wells. Wells screened in Santa Ana River alluvium draw in fine sand and silt that pack the screen and gravel envelope.
  • Clogged fractures in hard-rock wells. Foothill wells depend on a limited fracture network that can seal off with mineral deposits and fines.
  • Regional groundwater decline. Heavy demand and dry years have lowered water levels across parts of the basin, leaving some wells drawing near their limit in summer.
  • Old, never-serviced wells. Many area wells were completed decades ago and have never been cleaned or developed.

Hydrofracturing directly tackles scale, sediment, and clogged fractures. Where the underlying issue is a falling water table, we will tell you honestly and lay out deepening or replacement options rather than push a treatment that will not hold.

Signs Your Colton Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Keep an eye out for these warning signs, especially heading into the hot, dry Inland Empire summer:

  • The well produces noticeably less water than it did a few years ago.
  • It draws down quickly and the pump short-cycles or runs dry during heavy use.
  • Water pressure at the house or in irrigation lines has dropped off.
  • Recovery is slow—the well takes far longer to refill after pumping.
  • You are seeing more sand, grit, or cloudiness in the water.
  • A newly drilled well came in with disappointing flow.

What to Check Before You Call

A few details help us move faster. Note when the decline started and whether it follows the seasons. Watch for pump short-cycling. Locate any records of the well's depth, original yield, or completion report. And be ready to describe your demand—a household versus several irrigated acres are very different loads. The more context you can give us about how the well has behaved over the past few seasons, the more precisely we can target the treatment and the faster we can get your water flowing again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean, pressurized water. There are no chemicals, sand proppants, or additives. It is a safe, long-established method for rehabilitating water wells.

Will it work on a Colton-area well?

It depends on how your well is built and what formation it draws from. Hydrofracturing is effective both at reopening clogged fractures in foothill hard-rock wells and at clearing scale and sediment from basin wells. We run a flow test and assess construction first to confirm your well is a good candidate.

How much can my yield improve?

Results vary, but many wells see a meaningful increase in flow once plugged pathways are reopened. We document before-and-after yield with flow tests so you see the real improvement.

How long does the process take?

A typical job takes about four to eight hours on site, and we usually verify the improved yield with a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing typically costs $3,000–$8,000 versus $18,000–$42,000 for a new turnkey well, making it the smart first step before considering replacement.

My well struggles only in summer—can you help?

Often, yes. If your well draws down hard during peak summer demand, reopening the formation can improve both yield and recovery rate. If the cause is a falling regional water table, we will tell you honestly and discuss deepening.
