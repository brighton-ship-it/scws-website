What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Pinon Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Granite-Bedrock Wells in Pinon Hills

At more than 4,500 feet of elevation, Pinon Hills occupies the rugged shoulder where the San Gabriel Mountains climb out of the western Mojave. It is one of the better places in San Bernardino County to own a well — and one of the trickiest. Much of the community sits directly on granitic bedrock, so groundwater here does not soak through sand the way it does in a valley aquifer. Instead it travels through cracks, joints, and fracture seams in the rock. When those fractures clog with mineral scale and fine sediment, a well that used to flow freely can slow to a trickle. Hydrofracturing reopens that natural plumbing, and in Pinon Hills' fractured granite it is frequently the single most effective and economical fix available.

Local well records bear this out: wells around Pinon Hills range from roughly 290 feet to over 1,000 feet deep, averaging in the neighborhood of 600 feet, and the difference between a strong well and a weak one often comes down to how many water-bearing fractures the borehole happens to intersect. Hydrofracturing is a way to improve those odds after the fact.

Why Fractured Granite Makes Pinon Hills a Strong Candidate

The whole premise of water-well hydrofracturing is that the aquifer is a network of fractures rather than open pore space. That is precisely the setting under most of Pinon Hills. Decomposed granite near the surface gives way to harder crystalline rock at depth, and the productive water comes from the fracture system within that rock. High-pressure water injected into the borehole pushes into those fractures, scours out the calcium, silica, and iron deposits that have accumulated over the well's life, and can extend the fractures a little farther, connecting the borehole to water it could not previously reach.

Because so many Pinon Hills wells are sited in this kind of rock, success rates here tend to be high. That said, no honest contractor promises a guaranteed result. A few wells along the lower drainages tap shallower alluvial pockets near the washes; those behave more like sand-and-gravel aquifers and respond less reliably. We determine which type you have before recommending treatment.

The Hydrofracturing Procedure, Step by Step

  1. Baseline flow test. We measure exactly how much water your well produces and how quickly it recovers, so any improvement is measurable rather than anecdotal.
  2. Camera inspection. A downhole video survey shows casing condition and helps us locate the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Packer isolation. Inflatable packers seal off the section to be treated, focusing all the injected pressure where it will do the most good.
  4. High-pressure injection. Clean water is pumped at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, opening and flushing the fracture network in the granite.
  5. Development and retest. The well is surged to clear loosened debris, the pump is reset, and a post-treatment flow test confirms the gain — usually verifiable within 24 to 48 hours.

The whole job on a typical Pinon Hills well takes four to eight hours. No chemicals are used at any point; the only thing going down the well is water.

Signs Your Pinon Hills Well Could Benefit

  • Output that has dropped year over year, especially noticeable since the last few dry winters.
  • A newer well that came in weak — a common frustration when a borehole misses the best fracture seams.
  • The pump running longer and longer to fill the pressure tank, or short-cycling as the water level falls.
  • Sputtering, air-filled faucets late in the day during summer demand.
  • Sandy or cloudy water that hints at sediment movement in the borehole.

What to Check Around Your Property First

Before you call, it helps to gather a few facts. Find your well completion report if you can; it lists the total depth, the casing, and the formations the driller logged, all of which tell us whether you are in granite or alluvium. Note your pump's behavior — whether it cycles normally or struggles — and whether low pressure happens constantly or only under heavy use. Check the pressure tank's air charge and the pressure switch settings, since a tired tank or a misadjusted switch can mimic a yield problem that fracturing would not fix. If you irrigate, track roughly how long you can run water before the well draws down. These observations let us separate a true yield decline from a simpler equipment issue.

When It's Time to Bring in a Professional

Generating thousands of PSI safely inside a 600-foot borehole is specialized work. Done wrong, it can split casing or damage a marginal well. Call a licensed contractor when your production has clearly declined, when a new well underperformed, or whenever you are weighing fracturing against the much larger cost of drilling again. A professional assessment — flow test plus camera — is the only reliable way to know whether your Pinon Hills well is blocked or genuinely depleted.

Hydrofracturing Compared to Other Rehabilitation Options

Hydrofracturing is one tool among several, and the right choice depends on what is actually wrong with the well. Mechanical brushing and bailing physically scrub the casing and screen, which helps when the problem is surface buildup but does little for fractures deep in the rock. Acidizing dissolves carbonate scale chemically and is sometimes combined with fracturing, though many Pinon Hills owners prefer the chemical-free simplicity of water-only treatment. Surging and airlifting redevelop a well that has filled with fines but cannot create new flow paths. Where the limitation is the fracture network in the granite itself — by far the most common cause of declining yield in this community — hydrofracturing addresses the root problem rather than just the symptoms. During our evaluation we explain which method, or combination of methods, fits your specific well so you are not paying for treatment that won't move the needle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Pinon Hills well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

Most likely, yes — the granite bedrock under much of Pinon Hills is the ideal fractured-rock setting for the technique. We confirm with a camera inspection and your well log before recommending it.

How much more water will I get?

Suitable fractured-rock wells often improve two to five times over their pre-treatment flow, and we document the change with before-and-after testing. Results are never guaranteed, but the odds in Pinon Hills granite are favorable.

Does the treatment affect water quality?

No chemicals are introduced, so there is no contamination risk. Hydrofracturing actually flushes out accumulated sediment and scale, which often leaves the water cleaner than before. Routine sanitizing after the pump is reset is all that's required.

How deep are most wells here, and does depth matter?

Local wells commonly run 300 to 700-plus feet. Deeper wells cost a bit more to treat because of the additional packer setting and water volume, but depth alone does not prevent a successful job.

How long will the improvement last?

Many treated wells hold their gains for years. Mineral scale eventually rebuilds, so a small number of wells benefit from a second treatment down the line, but for most owners the result is durable.

What if hydrofracturing doesn't restore enough water?

If your well is genuinely depleted or structurally compromised, we'll be candid about it and discuss deepening or a replacement well. We would rather give you an honest answer than sell a treatment that won't help.
