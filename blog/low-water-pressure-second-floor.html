How Elevation Affects Pressure

Water pressure naturally decreases with height—it takes energy to push water uphill against gravity. Specifically, you lose 0.433 PSI for every vertical foot. In a two-story home, your second floor fixtures might be 12-20 feet above your pressure tank, meaning you're starting with 5-9 PSI less than the first floor before any other factors. This is physics, not a malfunction. But it means your system needs to be configured with enough starting pressure to overcome this elevation loss.

Pipe Sizing and Friction Losses

Beyond elevation, water loses pressure to friction as it travels through pipes. Undersized pipes (1/2" when you need 3/4"), too many fittings and elbows, or corroded galvanized pipes all increase friction losses. Older homes are often under-piped for modern fixtures. If replacing pipes isn't practical, at minimum ensure the main line to the second floor is 3/4" diameter. Also check for partially closed valves, clogged aerators, or showerheads with mineral buildup—these restrictions compound pressure problems.

Adjusting Your Pressure System

The easiest fix is adjusting your pressure switch to higher settings. Most systems come preset at 30/50 PSI (cut-in/cut-out), but well systems can typically handle 40/60 or even 50/70 with appropriate equipment. Before adjusting, verify your tank and pump are rated for higher pressures. Turn off power, drain some pressure, and use a wrench to adjust the large nut (raises both settings) and small nut (adjusts differential). Increase your tank's air pre-charge to match the new cut-in pressure minus 2 PSI.

When You Need System Upgrades

If adjusting pressure settings isn't enough—perhaps your pump can't produce higher pressure or your old pipes can't handle it—consider upgrades. A constant pressure system maintains steady pressure regardless of demand and can be set to 60-70 PSI. A dedicated booster pump for the second floor adds pressure just where needed. Re-piping with larger diameter or PEX reduces friction losses. For severe cases, a pressure tank relocation to a higher point in the home (like a utility closet) reduces the effective elevation difference.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my water pressure lower on the second floor?

Gravity is working against you. Water loses about 0.43 PSI for every foot of elevation. A second floor bathroom 15 feet above your pressure tank loses about 6.5 PSI before friction losses in the pipes. If your system starts at 50 PSI, you might only have 40 PSI upstairs after accounting for elevation and pipe friction—not enough for a good shower.

What PSI should my well pressure tank be set to for a two-story house?

For two-story homes, we recommend a cut-out pressure of 60-70 PSI at the tank. Your cut-in (when pump starts) should be about 20 PSI lower. So a 50/70 setting works well for most two-story homes. This ensures you still have 50+ PSI on the second floor. Check that your pressure tank and pump can handle these higher pressures.

Will a booster pump help with second floor pressure?

Yes, a booster pump can solve second floor pressure problems when adjusting your existing system isn't enough. Booster pumps add 20-50 PSI to your line pressure and can be installed specifically for the second floor. However, they add complexity and another component to maintain. First try adjusting your pressure switch to higher settings—it's free and often solves the problem.
