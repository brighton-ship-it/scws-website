Quarterly Well System Checkup: What to Inspect Every 3 Months

A 30-minute quarterly inspection catches problems before they become $3,000 emergencies. Here's exactly what to check every 3 months to keep your San Diego County well system running reliably for decades.

Why Quarterly Checks Matter

Most well system failures don't happen overnight. They develop gradually:

  • Pressure tank bladder slowly loses air charge → pump cycles more frequently → pump motor burns out
  • Well production declines slightly each season → one day you run dry mid-shower
  • Minor electrical issue causes intermittent problems → eventual complete failure
  • Water quality shift indicates aquifer changes or contamination

Catching these trends quarterly lets you schedule repairs on your terms—not during a holiday weekend when emergency rates apply.

The Quarterly Inspection Checklist

Time required: 20-30 minutes
Tools needed: Tire pressure gauge, 5-gallon bucket, stopwatch/phone timer, flashlight

1. Pressure System Check (5 minutes)

What to do:

  1. Find your pressure gauge (usually on the pressure tank or nearby piping)
  2. Run water until the pump kicks on—note the "cut-in" pressure
  3. Stop water flow and let tank fill—note the "cut-out" pressure
  4. Record both numbers and compare to previous quarters

What's normal:

  • Typical settings: 30/50 PSI or 40/60 PSI (cut-in/cut-out)
  • 20 PSI difference between cut-in and cut-out
  • Consistent readings quarter to quarter

Warning signs:

  • Narrowing pressure range – Tank losing air charge; check/add air
  • Lower overall pressure – Pump wear, clogged screen, or declining well
  • Pressure fluctuates wildly – Failing check valve or pressure switch
  • Pressure never reaches cut-out – Pump can't keep up (leak, pump failure, or demand too high)

2. Flow Rate Test (5 minutes)

What to do:

  1. Use outdoor hose bib closest to well/pressure tank
  2. Open valve fully
  3. Time how long to fill a 5-gallon bucket
  4. Calculate: GPM = 5 ÷ (seconds to fill ÷ 60)

Example: 30 seconds to fill = 5 ÷ 0.5 = 10 GPM

Record this number every quarter. A gradual decline indicates:

  • Pump wear (impeller erosion)
  • Clogged well screen
  • Declining aquifer level
  • Scale buildup in pipes

A sudden drop suggests an immediate problem—failing pump, cracked pipe, or major well issue.

3. Pressure Tank Inspection (5 minutes)

Visual check:

  • Look for rust, corrosion, or water stains indicating leaks
  • Check fittings for moisture or mineral deposits
  • Inspect for physical damage or dents

Air charge check (with tank empty):

  1. Turn off pump power
  2. Open a faucet to drain pressure
  3. Check air valve with tire gauge
  4. Should read 2 PSI below cut-in pressure (e.g., 28 PSI for a 30/50 system)
  5. Add air with bicycle pump or compressor if low

Waterlogged tank test:

  • Knock on tank from top to bottom
  • Should sound hollow (air) in top portion, solid (water) in bottom portion
  • If solid all the way up → waterlogged (bladder failed)
  • If completely hollow → no water in tank (inlet/bladder issue)

Cycling test: How often does the pump turn on during normal use?

  • Normal: Every 1-2 minutes during continuous demand
  • Rapid cycling (every 15-30 seconds): Waterlogged tank—needs replacement or repair

Rapid cycling is the #1 cause of premature pump failure. A $500 tank replacement prevents a $3,000 pump job.

4. Pump and Motor Check (5 minutes)

For jet pumps (above ground):

  • Listen for unusual sounds (grinding, squealing, humming)
  • Feel motor for excessive heat (warm is OK, too hot to touch is not)
  • Check for water leaks around pump body and connections
  • Look for oil leaks from motor bearings

For submersible pumps (in well):

  • You can't see the pump, but you can detect problems:
  • Listen at wellhead for grinding or vibration sounds
  • Check amp draw at control box (if you have a clamp meter)
  • Note if pump runs longer to rebuild pressure

Control box inspection:

  • Look for burnt marks, discoloration, or melted plastic
  • Check for ant nests or debris (common failure cause)
  • Ensure box is weatherproof and sealed

5. Electrical System Check (5 minutes)

Visual inspection:

  • Check wiring for damage, exposed conductors, or rodent chews
  • Inspect conduit for cracks or separation
  • Look at pressure switch contacts (remove cover carefully)
  • Check for burnt contacts or pitting

Warning signs:

  • Burning smell near electrical components
  • Breaker trips occasionally when pump runs
  • Lights dim when pump kicks on
  • Pump runs but pressure doesn't build

Safety note: If you see damaged wiring or suspect electrical problems, call a professional. Well pump electrical systems can be 240V and dangerous.

6. Water Quality Check (5 minutes)

Sensory evaluation:

  • Appearance: Clear? Cloudy? Any color tint?
  • Odor: Rotten eggs (sulfur)? Musty? Chlorine-like (rare in wells)?
  • Taste: Metallic? Salty? Bitter? Different from usual?

Physical checks:

  • Run water into white container—easier to see color changes
  • Let sit 30 minutes—sediment should settle, water should stay clear
  • Check toilet tanks and bowls for staining patterns
  • Look at faucet aerators for accumulated debris

What changes may indicate:

  • Sudden cloudiness: Sediment influx, pump pulling sand, or casing issue
  • New odor: Bacterial growth or aquifer contamination
  • Color change: Iron/manganese shift, surface water infiltration
  • Salty taste: Potential saltwater intrusion (coastal wells)

7. Wellhead and Surrounding Area (5 minutes)

Inspect the wellhead:

  • Well cap secure and watertight?
  • Any cracks or damage to casing above ground?
  • Concrete pad intact (no cracks for surface water entry)?
  • Grading slopes away from well (water shouldn't pool here)

Check surrounding area:

  • No new potential contamination sources (new septic, animal pens, chemical storage)
  • Vegetation cleared from wellhead area
  • No standing water or evidence of flooding

Recording Your Findings

Keep a simple log with these quarterly entries:

4/15/26 30 50 8.0 26 Clear, good Added air to tank

This simple log reveals trends that single inspections can't show. A slow flow rate decline over 2 years tells a different story than a sudden drop.

Seasonal Considerations

Spring Check (March-April)

  • Post-winter assessment—any freeze damage?
  • Baseline water quality after rainy season
  • Prepare irrigation systems if applicable
  • Test flow rate before dry season demand

Summer Check (June-July)

  • Monitor for dropping water table (longer pump run times)
  • Check for overheating components in hot weather
  • Assess flow rate during peak demand
  • Fire season prep—clear vegetation from wellhead

Fall Check (September-October)

  • Post-summer water level assessment
  • Prepare for any potential freezing (mountain areas)
  • Clean and store irrigation equipment
  • Schedule professional annual inspection

Winter Check (December-January)

  • Verify freeze protection (Julian, Palomar, etc.)
  • Check for condensation issues in cold weather
  • Monitor for rain-related contamination signs
  • Ensure access road to well is maintained

When to Call a Professional

Your quarterly checks are for monitoring—not diagnosing or repairing. Call us if you notice:

  • Rapid pressure cycling – Tank likely waterlogged, needs replacement
  • Flow rate drop of 20%+ – Pump wear or well production declining
  • Pressure won't reach cut-out – Pump failing, leak, or well issue
  • Any electrical burning smell – Fire hazard, needs immediate attention
  • Major water quality change – Potential contamination, needs testing
  • Strange pump sounds – Bearing failure, impeller damage, or sand in pump
  • No water at all – Don't wait—call immediately

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I inspect my well system?

Perform quarterly (every 3 month) DIY checkups plus an annual professional inspection. More frequent checks may be needed for older systems, during drought conditions, or if you notice any changes in water quality or pressure.

What's the most important thing to check on my well?

Pressure tank performance and cycling frequency. A waterlogged tank causes rapid pump cycling, which dramatically shortens pump life—often the most expensive component to replace at $2,000-$5,000+.

Can I do quarterly well checks myself?

Yes—most quarterly checks require no special tools. You're looking for changes from baseline: pressure variations, flow rate drops, unusual sounds, or water quality changes. Document readings each quarter to spot trends.

What should normal well pressure be?

Most residential systems run 30/50 or 40/60 PSI (cut-in/cut-out). Your system should have a consistent 20 PSI range. If the range narrows or overall pressure drops, something needs attention.

How do I know if my pressure tank is bad?

Signs include: pump cycles every 15-30 seconds (rapid cycling), no air sound when you tap the tank, water sprays from air valve when checked, or tank feels heavy/solid when empty. Any of these means tank needs service or replacement.
