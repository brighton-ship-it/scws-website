Rusty Water from Your Lake Gregory Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Lake Gregory well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Lake Gregory well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Well Water Around Lake Gregory: Causes and Cures for Crestline-Area Homes

If your tap has started running an orange or tea-colored rusty hue, staining fixtures and giving your water a metallic bite, iron is almost certainly the reason, and it is a problem we solve for Lake Gregory homeowners regularly.

How Snowmelt, Granite, and Seasonal Use Shape the Problem

Wells around Lake Gregory tap a hard-rock aquifer fed largely by winter snowmelt and seasonal rain percolating down through fractures in the granite. As that water moves slowly through the rock, it dissolves iron from the surrounding minerals. Because the recharge is seasonal, the iron concentration — and the staining — can shift through the year, sometimes worsening late in a dry season as the water table drops and the well draws from deeper, more mineralized zones. Meanwhile, the heavy share of part-time and short-term-rental homes in the Crestline area means a lot of wells sit idle between guests, and stagnant water is exactly what iron bacteria need to take hold. Put together, the geology and the rhythm of mountain living explain why rust shows up so often here.

Three Sources of Rust, One Color

To fix it, you have to know which source you are dealing with. Dissolved iron is the most frequent: invisible in the well, it turns the water orange only after it hits air, which is why a clear glass clouds and discolors as it sits. Iron bacteria announces itself with a rust-colored slime in tanks and toilets and a musty smell, and it thrives in lightly used vacation wells. Corrosion of an old steel casing or galvanized home plumbing drops actual rust particles into water that may otherwise be fine. Each demands its own remedy, and confusing them is the most common reason a do-it-yourself fix fails.

What You Can Check Before We Arrive

A few minutes of observation pays off. Draw a glass of cold water and watch it stand: clear water that yellows and then drops orange sediment over an hour signals dissolved iron. Rust confined to the hot tap points squarely at the water heater, an inexpensive fix unrelated to your well. Discoloration that spikes when you reopen a home after it has been closed up suggests stagnation, iron bacteria, or corroding pipe. A swampy or sulfur smell paired with the color warrants a lab test before drinking. These simple notes let us focus testing and avoid guesswork.

Treatment That Fits — and What It Costs

The right system follows directly from the diagnosis. For dissolved iron, an oxidizing or catalytic iron filter installed where water enters the house clears the discoloration; sediment and iron filtration around Lake Gregory typically costs $300 to $900. Where iron rides along with the hard, mineral-rich mountain water, a water softener — generally $1,500 to $3,500 installed — can tackle both, as long as the iron load is moderate. Iron bacteria requires shock chlorination and a proper well cleaning, since no filter outpaces a living colony. A corroded casing may need lining or replacement, and aging galvanized plumbing is best replaced with PEX or copper. We open every job with a $125 water test and diagnostic, credited toward the work, so the diagnosis becomes part of the solution rather than an added expense.

Knowing When to Pick Up the Phone

Call us promptly if the rusty water appeared abruptly along with weaker pressure or air sputtering at the faucet — that can flag a pump or drop-pipe issue rather than simple iron. Reach out if the stain persists after you have replaced the water heater and flushed your lines. And whenever odor accompanies the color, get a test before using the water for drinking or cooking. In a community where a fair number of homes are rentals, catching a well problem early also protects you from a bad guest experience or an emergency on a holiday weekend.

Lake Gregory Rusty Water: Your Questions

Is it safe to bathe in rusty well water?

Bathing in water with typical iron levels is generally fine, though it can stain tubs and, in some cases, lightly tint light-colored hair over time. Iron is an aesthetic concern rather than a health hazard at these concentrations, but a water test confirms there are no other issues riding along with it.

Why does my rusty water seem worse at the end of summer?

As the dry season drops the water table, your well may draw from deeper, more mineralized zones, raising the dissolved iron concentration. This seasonal swing is common in the snowmelt-fed wells around Lake Gregory and is one reason testing at the right time of year matters.

My place is a vacation rental — why is the water rusty when guests arrive?

Water that sits stagnant between stays lets iron bacteria grow and corrosion products settle in the pipes, so the first draw after a vacancy is often the most discolored. Shock chlorination and, where appropriate, a treatment system keep the water consistently clear for guests.

Can I remove iron with a whole-house filter from a big-box store?

A generic cartridge filter rarely keeps up with dissolved iron or iron bacteria, so staining usually comes back. A system sized to your measured iron level — whether an iron filter, a softener, or well cleaning — is what delivers lasting results.

What does it cost to solve rusty water near Lake Gregory?

Sediment and iron filtration typically runs $300 to $900, a softener $1,500 to $3,500, and well rehabilitation for iron bacteria is quoted after inspection. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward whatever solution you choose, so you pay once for the answer and the fix.

Does rusty water mean my well is failing?

Not usually. Iron staining is most often a water-quality issue, not a sign the well is dying. That said, if the color appears suddenly with falling pressure or sputtering air, we will check the pump and components, because that combination can indicate a mechanical problem.
