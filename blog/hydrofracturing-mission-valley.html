What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mission Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Mission Valley

Mission Valley is an urban district in the heart of the City of San Diego, in San Diego County, carved by the San Diego River as it cuts west toward the coast between the mesas of Mission Hills, Linda Vista, and Serra Mesa. Nearly every home, condo, and business here is connected to municipal water through the City of San Diego, so true private water wells are rare in the valley itself. The valley floor is deep alluvium, river-deposited sand, silt, and gravel many feet thick. If you actually operate a well in or right around Mission Valley, the first thing worth saying plainly is this: a well drilled into that river-bottom alluvium is generally a weak candidate for hydrofracturing, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Hydrofracturing only does its job in fractured rock. Where it shines is on the surrounding bedrock margins, the harder decomposed granite and crystalline rock that forms the mesas and canyon walls rimming the valley, not the soft fill on the valley floor. So the honest answer depends entirely on what your well bottoms out in.

What Hydrofracturing Does, and Where

The method is simple. We seal off a section of the open borehole with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI into that isolated zone. In hard, fractured rock, the pressure widens the natural fractures already in the stone, clears out the silt and mineral scale clogging them, and reconnects them to the borehole so more groundwater flows in. Only water is used, never chemicals, and the work usually takes four to eight hours. But that mechanism needs rock that holds a fracture. Pump high-pressure water into loose valley-floor sand and it simply migrates through the grains and the borehole settles back to its starting yield. That is the core reason Mission Valley's deep alluvial setting is not ideal.

Why a Mission Valley Well Might Be Underperforming

  • Alluvial fines clogging the screen — sand and silt migrating into the well screen choke flow over time. This is a development or screen-rehabilitation problem, not a fracturing one.
  • Pump or pressure-tank failure — what feels like a dying well is frequently a worn pump, a bad pressure switch, or a waterlogged tank.
  • Falling river-aquifer levels — the shallow San Diego River alluvial aquifer responds quickly to dry years, dropping the water available to a shallow well.
  • An old, partially collapsed casing — common in legacy wells, and a job for re-lining or replacement rather than hydrofracturing.

Honest Alternatives for Valley-Floor Wells

If your Mission Valley well draws from river alluvium and the flow is disappointing, hydrofracturing is rarely the answer. The solutions that actually move the needle here are: screen and well rehabilitation (surging, jetting, and re-development to clear the sand-clogged screen), deepening or drilling a new well sited where a thicker or cleaner aquifer zone exists, or simply leaning on the municipal connection that nearly every valley property already has. If the well is only used for irrigation, upgrading the pump and adding a constant-pressure system may give you everything you need without touching the aquifer. We would rather send you down one of these proven paths than charge for a fracturing job that will not deliver.

When Hydrofracturing Does Make Sense Nearby

The story changes on the bedrock margins above and around the valley. Properties up on the harder decomposed-granite mesas and in the surrounding canyon country of San Diego County frequently have wells set in fractured crystalline rock, and those are genuinely strong candidates. If your well is on one of those benches rather than the valley floor, the same treatment that fails in sand can multiply your yield in rock. The deciding factor is always the formation at the bottom of your well, which your driller's completion report will spell out.

A Practical Checklist Before You Call

  1. Locate the well log. Does the well end in sand and gravel or in rock? That answer decides whether hydrofracturing is even worth discussing.
  2. Check the cheap failure points. A pressure switch ($150-$350) or a waterlogged tank ($600-$1,500) often explains a sudden loss of pressure.
  3. Note the pattern. Sand in the water points to a screen problem; a gradual long-term decline in a rock well points to fracture scaling.
  4. Confirm your backup. Most Mission Valley properties have a municipal connection, which changes the urgency and the economics of any well repair.

Mission Valley Hydrofracturing FAQ

Can hydrofracturing help a well on the Mission Valley floor?

Usually not. The valley floor is deep river alluvium, and hydrofracturing needs fractured rock to work. Wells up on the surrounding bedrock mesas are a different and much better story.

I have city water. Is a well even worth repairing?

Often the municipal connection is the simpler answer, especially for a problem well. We will tell you honestly when a repair is not worth the money against the city supply you already have.

My water has sand in it. Is that a fracturing issue?

No. Sand usually means a screen or development problem in an alluvial well, which is fixed by rehabilitation, not by hydrofracturing.

How would I know if my well is in rock?

Your well completion report states the formation at the bottom. We can also confirm it with a camera survey during the diagnostic visit.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure. It does not contaminate groundwater the way oil-and-gas fracking is often assumed to.

What alternatives do you offer if my well cannot be fractured?

Screen rehabilitation, deepening, a new well, pump and pressure upgrades, or simply relying on your municipal connection, whichever fits your situation and budget best.
