What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Murrieta Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Murrieta's Granite Hills and Valley Wells

Murrieta sits at about 1,100 feet in the Temecula Valley of southwestern Riverside County, ringed by rolling hills and the basalt-capped Santa Rosa Plateau to the west, with Wildomar to the north and Temecula just south. As the area has grown, much of the city has moved onto municipal water, but rural parcels, hillside homes near the plateau, and properties on the valley's edges still rely on private wells. Those wells tap a mix of decomposed granite, fractured crystalline bedrock, and alluvial valley fill, and that mix determines whether hydrofracturing can help. The technique works on wells finished in competent fractured rock and offers little to wells set in loose alluvium or unconsolidated decomposed granite, so the honest first step on any Murrieta property is to determine which formation your well actually taps.

The Geology Beneath Murrieta

The hills around Murrieta and the Santa Rosa Plateau expose granitic and metamorphic rock that has weathered to decomposed granite near the surface and stays hard and fractured at depth. Where a well is finished in that competent fractured rock, water moves only along cracks and joints, so yield depends on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole intersects. Hydrofracturing improves that connection by injecting clean water at high pressure to widen and link the fractures, and the firm rock holds those openings. Where a well sits instead in loose valley alluvium or crumbly decomposed granite without a true fracture network, there is little to open and the benefit is limited. We assess this distinction carefully, because it is the difference between a treatment that transforms your supply and one that wastes your money.

Murrieta's long, dry summers concentrate demand exactly when the fracture system is at its lowest, after winter recharge has been spent. A hillside well that supports a home, landscaping, and perhaps a small vineyard or orchard comfortably in spring can fall behind by late summer. Raising the well's sustainable flow through fracturing gives those properties the headroom they need in the hardest months.

Signs a Murrieta Well Needs Attention

  • Declining production over several years from a well you know is in rock
  • Slow recovery after heavy household or irrigation use
  • Seasonal shortages that deepen in late summer and ease after winter
  • A newer bedrock well that never delivered the expected flow
  • A pump that short-cycles or draws the water level down to the intake

We always confirm the formation is the real limit and rule out equipment first. A worn pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch can mimic low yield, and these inexpensive fixes come before any major work.

How the Process Works

  1. Baseline flow test to record current yield as a documented starting point.
  2. Camera survey to check casing condition and locate the best fracture zones.
  3. Packer isolation to seal off a section of open borehole for focused treatment.
  4. High-pressure injection of clean water, typically 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, to clean and extend fractures.
  5. Development and re-testing to flush loosened material and confirm the gain.

Most Murrieta jobs finish in a single day. We use only clean water, never chemicals, so your drinking water stays safe throughout.

What to Check Before You Call

A little history helps us help you quickly. Try to learn whether your well is in competent rock or in valley alluvium and loose decomposed granite, since that largely determines whether fracturing applies; a well log usually says, and we can confirm with a camera. Note whether your shortage is seasonal, which points to a yield limit rather than equipment. Watch the pressure gauge for rapid cycling, a sign of a waterlogged tank, and listen for a pump that runs long then loses prime, a classic low-yield symptom. Sharing this history, along with your well's depth and how much you irrigate, lets us give you a fast, accurate answer.

Pairing Flow With Storage

Many hillside and rural properties around Murrieta get the most reliable supply by combining improved well flow with adequate storage. A storage tank lets a moderate well recharge during quiet overnight hours and then deliver a heavy draw for the household, landscaping, or a small vineyard during the day, while hydrofracturing raises the rate at which the well refills that storage. On parcels near the Santa Rosa Plateau where wildfire is a seasonal concern, on-site storage also supports defensible-space irrigation. We evaluate the whole system during your assessment, including pump sizing and storage, so the recommendation fits how your property actually uses water.

Realistic Results and Cost

Where a Murrieta well is genuinely set in fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, typically two to five times the prior flow. A new well in this area runs about $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, while fracturing a suitable existing well usually costs $3,000 to $8,000, with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the job. For a bedrock well that has lost yield, fracturing is the sensible first step before five-figure drilling. We document every result with before-and-after flow testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Murrieta well is a fracturing candidate?

It depends on the formation. Wells finished in competent fractured granite are often strong candidates, while wells in loose valley alluvium or crumbly decomposed granite are not. We confirm with your well log and a camera survey before recommending treatment, so you get an honest answer up front.

Does the process use chemicals?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure, unlike oil-and-gas fracking. There are no chemicals or additives, so it does not contaminate your groundwater; it simply reopens the natural fractures feeding your well.

Can it help a well that irrigates a small vineyard or orchard?

Yes, if the well is in fractured rock. Boosting sustainable flow is exactly what irrigation-heavy hillside properties need. We routinely treat wells where the goal is to support landscaping or a small planting without drilling an expensive new well.

How much more water can I expect?

On a qualifying Murrieta well, successful treatments commonly yield two to five times the prior flow, with a success rate around 70 to 80 percent. We measure your well before and after so you see the actual, documented gain rather than a promise.

How long does treatment take?

Most jobs take a single day, four to eight hours depending on the number of zones. We reinstall the pump and run a post-treatment flow test before leaving, and water is usually available again the same evening.

What if my well doesn't improve?

About one in five wells shows little gain, which is why we test before and after and assess candidacy honestly up front. If results disappoint, we discuss alternatives such as added storage, deepening the well, or, as a last resort, drilling a new one.
