How Your Well and Pressure System Work

If your property draws from a private well, the system rests on three parts working in concert. A submersible pump set in the well casing lifts water into a pressure tank, where a rubber bladder separates the water from a charge of compressed air. That compressed air is what pushes water to your fixtures at a usable pressure, typically 40 to 60 PSI. A pressure switch monitors the tank and tells the pump when to start (cut-in) and stop (cut-out). Chollas View's mesa-and-canyon topography means elevation differences can matter: a home above its wellhead loses pressure to vertical lift, so the pump has to be sized for the climb. When the pump weakens, the tank loses its air charge, the switch drifts, or the lines clog, you feel it as low or unsteady pressure at the tap.

Why Chollas View Wells Lose Pressure: Local Causes

The geology of southeastern San Diego is defined by flat-topped mesas cut by the Chollas Creek canyon system, underlain by the sedimentary San Diego Formation - sandstones and siltstones - over older bedrock. Private wells in this part of the county tend to be relatively shallow and draw from limited local aquifers that depend on seasonal rainfall for recharge, which makes them sensitive to drought. The most common causes of low pressure we encounter include:

  • Limited well yield in dry years: Shallow coastal-area aquifers recharge from rainfall, and after dry winters the water table can fall, reducing pump output and pressure.
  • Mineral and hard-water scaling: San Diego County groundwater often carries calcium and dissolved minerals that scale pump parts, narrow pipes, and foul pressure-tank bladders over time.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank: A failed bladder or lost air charge makes the pump short-cycle while pressure surges and sags.
  • Sediment from sandy formation soils: Fine sand from the San Diego Formation can slip past worn well screens, clogging filters, foot valves, and fixtures.
  • Elevation lift on canyon-edge lots: Homes on the mesa rims above their wells need pumps with adequate lift, or pressure suffers.
  • Aging buried lines: Older parcels in this long-established area can have decades-old underground lines prone to leaks that bleed pressure.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks help clarify the issue. Never open the well cap or touch electrical components if you are unsure.

  1. Watch the pressure gauge at your tank through a full cycle. Healthy systems move between roughly 40 and 60 PSI; note if it barely climbs or bounces.
  2. Listen for short-cycling - rapid on/off clicking signals a tank that has lost its air charge.
  3. Compare one fixture to the whole house. Weak flow at a single tap usually means a clogged aerator; weak flow everywhere points to the well system.
  4. Look for damp ground along the line route that could reveal a leak.
  5. Check your sediment filter. A clogged cartridge is a cheap, common cause of pressure loss on sandy formation soils.

Preventing Low Pressure: Maintenance Tips

Preventive care keeps Chollas View well systems healthy. Schedule an annual or bi-annual inspection of your pump, tank, and switch so we can catch scaling, a weakening air charge, or early pump wear. Check the tank's air pressure with a tire gauge and keep it about 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in setting. Flush outdoor spigots at full flow every few months to clear sediment, and monitor well yield during dry seasons to avoid over-drawing. A whole-house sediment filter protects your fixtures, and a softener fights hard-water scaling while extending the life of pumps and tanks.
