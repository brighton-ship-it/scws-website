Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Ocotillo well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Desert Well Service for Ocotillo Homes, Ranches, and Properties

Ocotillo is a small, unincorporated desert community in Imperial County, tucked into the far southwest corner of the county near the San Diego County line, about 26 miles west of El Centro along Interstate 8. Sitting at the western edge of the Imperial Valley near the Yuha Desert and the In-Ko-Pah and Jacumba mountains, Ocotillo is one of the driest places in all of California, often receiving under three inches of rain in a year. The community took shape as a desert retirement settlement after its post office opened in 1957, and today most homes, ranches, and outlying parcels here depend on private wells rather than a municipal system. When the temperature climbs past 110 degrees and the nearest water main is many miles away, a dependable well is not a convenience; it is survival infrastructure.

Let's be honest about the local picture. Ocotillo is not avocado country. The extreme desert heat, the high salinity of much of the groundwater, and the lack of cheap surface water make salt-sensitive crops like avocados impractical here. The famous irrigated agriculture of the surrounding Imperial Valley — alfalfa, hay, winter vegetables, dates, and citrus in places — runs almost entirely on Colorado River water delivered through canals. Out on the desert fringe in Ocotillo, there is no canal at your property line. What you have is your well. That well typically serves a home, hardy desert landscaping, a few date palms, livestock and horses, and the occasional small hobby planting that can tolerate brackish water. Our job is to keep that well producing clean, usable water in one of the harshest environments a pump can operate in.

How a Desert Well and Pressure System Works in Ocotillo

A complete private water system in the Ocotillo area is more than a hole in the ground. It is a chain of components, and a weakness anywhere in that chain shows up at your faucet. Desert wells in this part of Imperial County can be deep — depths vary widely by parcel and by which water-bearing zone a driller reaches, often a few hundred feet, sometimes more. Here is how the pieces fit together:

  • The well and submersible pump — A submersible pump sits down in the water column and pushes water to the surface. In deep desert wells the pump works against significant lift and runs hard during the long cooling season.
  • Pressure tank and pressure switch — The tank stores a cushion of pressurized water so the pump is not cycling on and off every time you open a tap. The pressure switch tells the pump when to start and stop. These are the most common failure points in any system.
  • Storage tank — Because desert wells can be low-yield, many Ocotillo properties run the well into a large storage tank, then use a booster pump to deliver household pressure. This buffers you against a slow-recovering well and gives you a reserve for heat waves and power outages.
  • Water treatment — Given the salinity and hardness of much local groundwater, treatment is often essential: sediment filtration, softening or iron and manganese removal, and in some cases reverse-osmosis or desalination-type treatment for drinking water.

When all of these work together, you get steady pressure and water you can actually use. When one link weakens — a worn switch, a waterlogged tank, scaled-up plumbing — the whole system suffers.

Common Well Problems We See Around Ocotillo

The desert environment around Ocotillo creates a predictable set of issues. Most service calls in this area trace back to one of the following causes:

  • High salinity and TDS — Much of the groundwater here carries elevated dissolved salts. Salty water is harsh on plumbing, hard on appliances, and unsuitable for salt-sensitive plants. It is the single biggest water-quality challenge in the area.
  • Hard water and mineral scale — Dissolved calcium and magnesium leave scale inside pipes, pressure tanks, water heaters, and pump components, slowly choking flow and shortening equipment life.
  • Heat-stressed pumps and controls — Relentless summer heat and long daily run times push pumps, motors, capacitors, and pressure switches to fail earlier than they would in a mild climate.
  • Sediment and dust — Fine desert sand and silt can enter the system, clog filters and fixtures, and abrade pump components.
  • Low yield and slow recovery — Some desert wells simply do not produce a lot of water per minute, which makes storage tanks and careful pump sizing critical.

What You Can Check Before You Call

A few safe checks can help you describe the problem — or sometimes resolve it — before a technician arrives:

  1. Confirm power. Check the breaker for the well and any booster pump. Desert power can flicker, and a tripped breaker is a frequent culprit.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge. Note whether pressure is low, zero, or swinging wildly. That single reading tells a technician a great deal.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking of the pressure switch or a pump that runs constantly without building pressure points to a tank or switch problem.
  4. Inspect for leaks. Walk the line from well to tank to house looking for wet spots, especially at fittings exposed to sun and heat cycling.
  5. Check your storage tank level. If you run a storage-and-booster setup, a near-empty tank suggests the well is not keeping up or the fill float has failed.

Never open the well cap, pull wiring, or attempt to lift a submersible pump yourself. Those are jobs for a licensed contractor with the right equipment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I grow avocados in Ocotillo using my well water?

Realistically, no. Avocados are highly sensitive to salt, and Ocotillo's intense heat plus the high salinity of much local groundwater make them impractical. Wells here are far better suited to homes, livestock, hardy desert landscaping, and date palms. The Imperial Valley's commercial crops rely on canal-delivered Colorado River water, which Ocotillo properties do not have.

How deep are wells in the Ocotillo area?

Depths vary widely by parcel and by which water-bearing zone a driller reaches. Many desert wells in this part of Imperial County run a few hundred feet, and some are deeper. We evaluate each site individually rather than assuming a fixed depth.

Why is my well water salty or hard?

Much of the groundwater around Ocotillo carries elevated dissolved salts (high TDS) and hardness minerals. That is a natural feature of this desert aquifer. The good news is that sediment filtration, softening, and reverse-osmosis or desalination-type treatment can make the water far more usable for your home.
