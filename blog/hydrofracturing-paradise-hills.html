What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Paradise Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Paradise Hills

In Paradise Hills, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Paradise Hills wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing and Paradise Hills: A Truthful Local Take

Paradise Hills is a hilly, established residential neighborhood in southeastern San Diego, set on the rolling high ground between National City, the Skyline-Paradise Hills mesa, and the Sweetwater River valley to the south. It is a community of mid-century homes and cul-de-sacs with long views toward the South Bay, named for the literal hills it climbs. Like the rest of urban San Diego, it is served by municipal water — here through the City of San Diego Public Utilities Department, with the Sweetwater Authority serving adjacent areas just south. There are no private domestic wells in Paradise Hills, so in plain terms, there is nothing here to hydrofracture.

We say that openly because you deserve a straight answer rather than a pitch. Hydrofracturing is a real, useful technique — for wells drilled into bedrock, in places where people rely on their own groundwater. A built-out hillside neighborhood on city water is not that. Below we explain why, describe what hydrofracturing genuinely does, and point you to where it pays off in San Diego County.

Why Paradise Hills Is Not a Hydrofracturing Candidate

Two clear reasons. First, the supply: every home in Paradise Hills is connected to the municipal distribution system — a blend of imported Colorado River and State Water Project water with treated local supply. No one in this neighborhood pumps from a private well, so there is no borehole to inject into.

Second, the ground. Paradise Hills rises over the uplifted coastal mesas and the slopes above the Sweetwater valley — sedimentary terrace deposits, sandstone, and the soft formations typical of San Diego's urbanized hills. That is not the hard, fractured crystalline bedrock hydrofracturing relies on. There are no rock seams here to clean and widen, so high-pressure injection would have nothing to work on even if a well somehow existed.

What Hydrofracturing Actually Does

So the contrast is clear, here is the real process. A crew lowers an inflatable packer into a bedrock well and seals off a section of the borehole. Clean water — no chemicals — is then injected into that isolated zone at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes silt and mineral scale out of the rock's natural fractures and widens those seams so groundwater flows into the well more freely. On a good fractured-rock well, yield often rises two to five times. It is a precise, established rehabilitation method — for rock wells, which Paradise Hills does not have.

Where Hydrofracturing Helps in San Diego County

Our hydrofracturing work happens out in the county backcountry, where homes run on private groundwater and the rock is granite. Communities such as Jamul, Dulzura, Alpine, Ramona, Julian, and Descanso sit on the San Diego batholith — the fractured crystalline rock that forms the inland mountains. Notably, some of that bedrock country lies just east and southeast of Paradise Hills, up in the hills toward Jamul and the Sweetwater foothills. Those wells depend entirely on the fractures they intersect, and when their yield fades, hydrofracturing is frequently the most economical rescue. If you own such a property, you have found the right company — you are simply searching under an urban neighborhood name.

Common Causes of Low Yield on Backcountry Wells

  • Fracture fouling — silt, clay, and mineral scale seal the cracks feeding a granite well.
  • Drought drawdown — dry winters lower the regional water table and shallow seams go dry.
  • A marginal borehole — some wells never tapped a rich fracture network and underperformed from day one.
  • Decomposed-granite infill — loose “DG” sloughs into the hole and chokes the producing section.

What to Check (If You Do Have a Well)

  • Confirm you are on a private well — within Paradise Hills you are on city water.
  • For a rural parcel in the hills, pull the well completion report: depth, static level, and reported yield show whether it is a hydrofrac candidate.
  • Check the pressure switch and the pressure tank's air charge — a $150–$350 switch or a waterlogged tank often imitates a weak well.
  • Note whether the decline was gradual (rehabilitation territory) or sudden (likely a pump or controls fault).

The Skyline-Paradise Hills Landscape

Part of what gives Paradise Hills its character — the climbing streets, the ridge-top views toward the bay and the Sweetwater valley — is exactly what tells a well professional this is not bedrock-well country. The neighborhood sits on uplifted mesa and terrace ground, the same kind of soft, layered marine sediment that underlies much of urbanized San Diego. It makes for buildable, view-rich lots, but it is geologically unlike the dense, jointed granite of the inland mountains. The water that hydrofracturing chases — held in cracks within hard crystalline rock — simply is not present in this formation, and the neighborhood's reliance on imported municipal supply reflects that reality. The hills here are scenic, not aquifer-rich in the fractured-rock sense.

A trustworthy contractor reads the landscape before quoting work. Anyone offering to hydrofracture a Paradise Hills lot is either mistaken about where they are or not being candid. The map of San Diego County is consistent on this point: the fracturable rock is inland and uphill in the batholith, while the coastal and mesa neighborhoods like Paradise Hills run on the city system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there water wells in Paradise Hills?

No. Paradise Hills is a built-out urban neighborhood served by City of San Diego municipal water. There are effectively no private domestic wells here to hydrofracture.

Then why does this page exist?

Many people researching hydrofracturing in southeastern San Diego actually own a well in the nearby backcountry. This page explains why the technique does not apply on an urban hillside lot and where it does help.

Can hydrofracturing improve my Paradise Hills water pressure?

No. On city water, pressure is a plumbing or regulator matter, not a well one. Hydrofracturing affects only bedrock wells. We can refer you to the right plumbing or treatment solution instead.

Where would hydrofracturing actually help me?

On a fractured-rock well in the hills east and southeast of here — Jamul, Dulzura, and similar areas — where it can multiply a fading well's yield far more cheaply than a new well.

What does hydrofracturing cost?

Typically $3,000 to $8,000 for a qualifying bedrock well, versus $18,000 to $42,000 for a new well. It applies only where there is a rock well to treat.

Is hydrofracturing safe?

Yes — clean water only, no chemicals, and no casing damage when a licensed C-57 contractor performs it. It is simply not applicable to a municipally served neighborhood like Paradise Hills.
