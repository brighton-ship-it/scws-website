Sand in Your Calimesa Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Calimesa well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Calimesa well.

Why Calimesa Wells Produce Sand

Calimesa sits at the southern edge of the Yucaipa groundwater subbasin and draws into the wider San Timoteo Canyon and Beaumont (San Gorgonio Pass) groundwater area. The aquifer here is not solid rock. It is made up of alluvial sand-and-gravel deposits that washed down from the surrounding hills over hundreds of thousands of years, carried and sorted by San Timoteo Creek and its tributaries. Layered into and beneath those deposits is the soft, poorly cemented San Timoteo Formation, a sedimentary unit that crumbles and sheds fine sand far more readily than the coarse fan material and bedrock found on higher ground.

What this means in practical terms is that the water-bearing zones supplying most Calimesa wells are loose, granular, and full of fine to medium sand grains. A properly built and developed well holds those grains back; when something in the system changes, the sand starts to move. It is worth being precise about this, because some well companies mistakenly describe Inland Empire aquifers as decomposed granite. In Calimesa proper, the primary producing aquifer is alluvial and sedimentary sand and gravel, not granite, and that distinction drives every decision about screens, pump placement, and filtration.

The most common reasons a Calimesa well begins producing sand include:

  • A worn or failing pump whose impellers and wear rings have eroded, letting the unit pull sand it once held back.
  • An oversized pump that over-draws the well, yanking water through the surrounding formation hard enough to mobilize fine grains.
  • A failed or corroded well screen where slots have widened or steel has rusted through, removing the barrier that keeps sand in the aquifer.
  • The wrong screen slot size for the fine San Timoteo sand, so grains that should be filtered out pass straight into the casing.
  • Excessive drawdown, where the water level is pulled down so far during pumping that the well draws from looser, sandier zones.
  • A new, undeveloped well that has not yet been properly developed and surged to settle the formation and gravel pack.
  • The inherently sandy aquifer itself, where the loose alluvial material simply migrates over time as the well ages.

In our experience across Calimesa, Cherry Valley, and the Beaumont side of the Pass, the cause is usually a combination: an aging well in a naturally sandy formation, paired with a pump that is set too deep or pumping too aggressively. Identifying which factors are in play is the whole point of a proper diagnosis, because the fix for a torn screen is very different from the fix for an oversized pump.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is abrasive, and it does not pass through your water system harmlessly. Every grain that moves through your pump acts like liquid sandpaper. The single most expensive consequence of ignoring sandy water is a destroyed submersible pump: the sand grinds away the impellers and bearings, the pump loses efficiency, the motor works harder and hotter, and eventually it seizes or burns out years before it should have. We have pulled pumps from Calimesa wells that were chewed to scrap after only a couple of seasons of running sand.

Downstream, the damage spreads. Sand collects in the bottom of your pressure tank and waterlogs it, shortens the life of pressure switches, and scores the seats of valves so they never seal again. It packs into water heaters, reducing their capacity and efficiency. It clogs the screens and solenoids in dishwashers, washing machines, irrigation valves, and reverse-osmosis systems. Faucet aerators plug up, and fine sediment can scratch fixtures and stain laundry. Most seriously of all, persistent sand is often the first visible symptom of a structural problem in the well, such as a breached casing or a collapsing screen, that will only worsen if left alone. Catching it early almost always saves money.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Calimesa

Guessing is expensive, so we diagnose before we recommend anything. When we come out to a Calimesa property, our process is methodical:

  • Jar and settling test. We capture water straight from the wellhead in a clear container and let the sediment settle, so we can see the grain size, color, and quantity, and whether it is true aquifer sand or rust and scale.
  • Sand concentration measurement. We measure how much sand the well is producing relative to flow, which tells us whether this is a nuisance level or a destructive one.
  • Amp draw and pump depth check. We read the motor's amperage and confirm how deep the pump is set. An abnormal amp draw and a pump sitting low in a sandy zone are telltale signs.
  • Pump and screen pull and inspection. When the evidence points downhole, we pull the pump and inspect it along with the screen and casing for wear, corrosion, and slot damage.
  • Downhole camera survey. A camera lowered into the casing shows us holes, splits, encrustation, and exactly where sand is entering the well.
  • Flow and drawdown testing. We measure yield and how far the water level drops under load to judge whether the pump is over-drawing the formation.

Our full diagnostic visit is $125, and we credit that amount toward any repair we perform. That way you get a clear, professional answer about what your well is actually doing, and you are not paying twice for the trip.

How We Fix It

Because the causes vary, so do the fixes. Once we know the source, we match the solution to the problem rather than selling a one-size-fits-all package:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters. For wells producing modest amounts of sand, a centrifugal separator spins the grains out before they ever reach your pressure tank and plumbing, protecting everything downstream.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration. A spin-down filter plus staged sediment cartridges polishes the water and catches the finer San Timoteo sand that a separator may pass.
  • Pump resizing and repositioning. If an oversized or too-deep pump is the culprit, we right-size the pump to the well's true yield and reset it at the correct depth, above the sediment and the sandiest zones.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment. For older or undeveloped wells, we surge, swab, and redevelop the well to stabilize the formation and gravel pack so the aquifer holds its sand back again.
  • Screen repair and relining. Where the screen or casing has failed, we repair, reline, or install a new screen with the correct slot size for Calimesa's fine alluvial sand.

In many cases the right answer is a combination, for instance redeveloping the well and resetting a properly sized pump, then adding a separator as insurance. We will always tell you honestly which steps are necessary and which are optional.

Preventing Sand Problems

Some sand is unavoidable in a naturally sandy aquifer, but most destructive sand problems are preventable. Have your pump's depth and the well's drawdown checked periodically so it is never set to draw from the sandiest zones or pumped harder than the formation can sustain. Avoid the temptation to install a bigger pump for more pressure; over-pumping is one of the leading causes of sand in the Pass area. New wells should always be fully developed before they are put into service. An annual or biennial inspection that includes a jar test and an amp-draw reading catches rising sand levels long before they ruin a pump, and matching the screen slot size to the local sand at the time of construction or repair pays dividends for the life of the well.

When to Call a Professional

A little sediment right after the pump cycles on may be harmless, but you should call a professional if sand appears suddenly or steadily increases, if you see grit at multiple fixtures, if your pressure tank needs frequent draining, if pressure is dropping, or if your pump is short-cycling or drawing high amps. These are signs the well or pump has changed, and running the system in that condition only multiplies the damage. Because diagnosing sand correctly requires pulling equipment and reading downhole conditions, it is not a do-it-yourself job. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Calimesa well water normal?

A trace of fine sediment can be normal in a brand-new well or briefly when a pump first kicks on, because Calimesa's aquifer is naturally sandy alluvial material. But sand that is steady, increasing, or showing up at several fixtures is not normal and signals a pump, screen, or casing issue that should be inspected.

Will sand ruin my well pump?

Yes, given time. Sand is abrasive and grinds down a submersible pump's impellers and bearings, causing it to lose efficiency and fail years early. Removing the sand source or filtering it out is far cheaper than repeatedly replacing burned-out pumps.

Why is my Calimesa well sandy when my neighbor's is not?

Local geology changes over short distances here. One property may draw from looser San Timoteo Formation sand while a neighbor taps a coarser, better-cemented zone. Differences in well depth, screen design, pump size, and age all matter too, which is why each well needs its own diagnosis.

Can I just install a sediment filter and be done with it?

A filter or separator protects your plumbing and appliances, and for many wells it is the right call. But if the sand is coming from a failing screen or a pump pulling from the wrong zone, a filter only treats the symptom. We check the source first so you do not keep replacing clogged cartridges over a hidden problem.

How long does a typical sand repair take?

Installing a separator or sediment filtration is often a same-day job. Pulling and resizing a pump usually takes a day, and a full well rehabilitation or screen repair can take one to several days depending on depth and conditions. We give you a realistic timeline with your estimate.
