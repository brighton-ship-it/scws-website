Rusty Water from Your Potrero Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Potrero well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Potrero well.

Rusty, Orange, and Iron-Stained Well Water in Potrero

Potrero is one of those San Diego County backcountry communities that most people drive past on the way to somewhere else. Tucked into the oak-dotted valleys of the Cleveland National Forest, between Tecate and Campo and just a few miles north of the Mexican border, it is ranching country in the truest sense — the name itself is Spanish for pasture or grazing land. Out here along Highway 94, past Potrero County Park and the broad meadows of Round Potrero, there is no municipal water main waiting at the property line. Nearly every home, ranch, and small farm draws its water from a private well drilled into fractured granite, and that single fact shapes almost everything about the water that comes out of your tap.

So when a Potrero homeowner turns on the faucet and sees rusty, orange, brown, or red-tinted water, it is rarely a mystery to anyone who works on wells in this part of San Diego County. The granitic mountain bedrock that holds the water is also full of iron and manganese, and the deep, slow-recovery wells that are typical here create exactly the conditions where that iron shows up as rust. The good news is that discolored water is almost always a solvable problem once you understand which of several possible sources is actually behind it.

What Causes Rusty or Orange Well Water?

"Rust" is a catch-all word for several different problems that happen to look similar in the glass. Pinning down the real cause is the whole game, because the right fix for one is the wrong fix for another.

Dissolved (Ferrous) Iron Oxidizing to Rust

The most common culprit in Potrero wells is dissolved ferrous iron. Deep in the granitic aquifer, with little oxygen present, iron stays dissolved and invisible — the water often looks perfectly clear at the wellhead. The moment it meets air at your faucet, in your toilet tank, or sitting in a glass on the counter, it oxidizes into ferric iron, the reddish-orange particles we recognize as rust. The tell-tale sign is water that pours clear but turns orange after a few minutes, leaving rusty rings in toilets and orange stains on white laundry and porcelain.

Manganese — Black and Purple Staining

Manganese travels with iron through the same granitic rock, and it stains too — but darker. Where iron leaves orange marks, manganese leaves black, brown, or purplish staining in toilets, dishwashers, and on fixtures, and it can give water a faintly bitter taste. Many Potrero wells carry both minerals at once, which is why a sample sometimes stains both orange and black.

Iron Bacteria — Rusty Slime

The deep, low-yield wells common around Potrero recover slowly and can run warm, and that is exactly the environment iron bacteria love. These naturally occurring organisms feed on iron and leave behind a rusty, reddish-brown slime that you may notice in the toilet tank, around fixtures, or clinging inside the pressure tank. Iron bacteria often come with a swampy or oily smell and a slimy texture that distinguishes them from simple mineral iron.

Corroding Steel Casing

Many older wells in the San Diego County backcountry were finished with steel casing. After decades in the ground, that steel casing corrodes, and flakes of rust wash directly into the water you pump. When casing is the source, the discoloration tends to be more gritty and particulate, and it may worsen over the years as the steel continues to break down.

Galvanized Plumbing in the Home

Sometimes the well is innocent. Older Potrero homes plumbed with galvanized steel pipe develop rust on the inside of the pipe walls. As that zinc coating fails, the exposed steel rusts and sheds into your water — often showing up worst at the first faucet you open in the morning after water has sat in the pipes overnight.

A Rusting Water-Heater Anode or Tank

If the rusty water shows up only on the hot side, the well is almost certainly not to blame. The sacrificial anode rod inside your water heater is designed to corrode so the tank does not — but once it is spent, the tank itself begins to rust and tint the hot water orange. This is one of the easiest causes to confirm and one of the cheapest to fix.

Is Rusty Well Water Safe to Drink?

For most Potrero households, the honest answer is reassuring with a couple of caveats.

  • Iron is primarily an aesthetic problem. At the concentrations typical of San Diego County well water, it is not considered a health hazard — it simply tastes metallic, stains everything, and clogs plumbing over time. The U.S. EPA classifies iron as a secondary (aesthetic) contaminant for that reason.
  • Manganese deserves more respect. While low levels are harmless, higher concentrations have a health advisory attached, particularly for infants and for water used to mix formula. If your water stains black and you have young children, manganese is worth testing for specifically.
  • Iron bacteria are not generally a disease threat themselves, but the slime they create can harbor other organisms and is a sign your well environment needs attention. Their presence is also a reason to test for coliform bacteria at the same time.

The only way to know what you are actually dealing with — and at what concentration — is a water test. We strongly recommend testing before spending money on any treatment system, because the test result is what tells us which system will actually work.

Localizing the Source: Simple Checks Before You Spend a Dime

Before any equipment gets recommended, a few minutes of observation at home narrows the field dramatically. These are the same questions our technicians ask on the phone.

  • Hot water only, or cold too? If rust appears only on the hot side, suspect the water heater. If both hot and cold run rusty, the source is upstream — the well, the casing, or the main plumbing.
  • One fixture or the whole house? Rust at a single faucet points to that fixture or the pipe feeding it. Rust at every tap points to the well or the main line.
  • First draw or constant? Discoloration that is worst first thing in the morning and clears after running the tap suggests corroding pipes where water sat overnight. Rust that is constant whenever the pump runs points back toward the well and aquifer.
  • Sudden or gradual? A gradual worsening over months or years fits corroding casing or galvanized plumbing. A sudden change can follow a drop in the water table, heavy pumping, or a disturbance in the well — common during Southern California's dry stretches when low-yield wells are stressed.
  • Slime or grit? A slimy, reddish coating in the toilet tank points to iron bacteria; loose gritty particles point to mineral iron or casing flakes.

What to Do Right Now

  1. Run an outside hose bib for several minutes and catch a sample in a clear glass. Watch whether it is cloudy at the tap or only turns orange after sitting.
  2. Compare hot and cold at the same sink to isolate the water heater.
  3. Check the toilet tank for orange staining versus black staining versus rusty slime.
  4. Avoid bleaching rust-stained laundry — chlorine bleach sets iron stains permanently. Use an iron-specific rust remover instead.
  5. Schedule a water test so treatment is matched to the real cause rather than a guess.

Treatments Matched to the Cause

There is no single "rust filter" that fixes every situation. The correct solution depends entirely on what your test and inspection reveal.

Oxidizing Iron Filter (Greensand or Air-Injection)

For moderate to heavy dissolved iron and manganese — the most common Potrero scenario — an oxidizing filter is the workhorse. Greensand and air-injection systems introduce oxygen, convert the dissolved iron to solid particles, trap them in a filter bed, and then flush the bed clean on a regular backwash cycle. These also handle manganese well.

Water Softener for Low Iron

When iron is present only at low levels and the water is also hard, a water softener can remove that small amount of iron along with the hardness minerals. Softeners are not the right tool for heavy iron or for iron bacteria, but for the right water chemistry they are an efficient, dual-purpose fix.

Chemical (Chlorine) Injection with Retention and Filtration

For very high iron, high manganese, or stubborn combinations, a chemical injection system doses chlorine ahead of a retention tank, then filters out the oxidized particles before the water reaches the house. It is the most thorough approach and sits at the higher end of the cost range, but for difficult backcountry water it is sometimes the only thing that genuinely works.

Shock Chlorination and Well Rehabilitation for Iron Bacteria

If iron bacteria are the problem, filtration alone will not solve it — you have to address the colony in the well. Shock chlorination disinfects the well, and a full well rehabilitation cleans the casing, screen, and surrounding gravel pack to knock back the slime and restore flow. In persistent cases an ongoing low-level chlorination routine keeps the bacteria from returning.

Casing Liner or Replacement

When corroding steel casing is shedding rust into the water, the answer is structural. Depending on the condition of the well, we may install a liner inside the existing casing or, in severe cases, replace the casing outright to stop the contamination at its source.

Plumbing Replacement

If the rust is coming from old galvanized pipe inside the home, no well treatment will help. Replacing the failing galvanized lines with modern PEX or copper eliminates that household source of rust for good.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Potrero well water clear at the tap but turns orange in the glass?

That is the classic signature of dissolved ferrous iron. Underground in the granitic aquifer there is little oxygen, so the iron stays dissolved and invisible. Once the water hits the air, it oxidizes into rust-colored ferric particles. An oxidizing iron filter is usually the right fix.

Is the rusty water from my well safe to drink?

Iron is mainly an aesthetic issue and is not considered a health hazard at typical well concentrations — it tastes metallic and stains. Manganese, which often appears alongside it and stains black, carries a health advisory at higher levels, especially for infants. A water test tells you exactly what you have.

How do I know if it is iron bacteria versus mineral iron?

Iron bacteria leave a slimy, reddish-brown coating — in the toilet tank, around fixtures, or inside the pressure tank — often with a swampy or oily smell. Mineral iron produces gritty particles and orange staining without the slime. Iron bacteria require shock chlorination and well rehabilitation rather than filtration alone.

Only my hot water is rusty. What does that mean?

When only the hot side runs orange, the well is almost certainly fine. The most likely cause is a spent anode rod and a rusting water-heater tank. That is one of the simplest and least expensive problems to confirm and fix.

Could my old steel casing or galvanized pipes be the cause?

Yes. Many older San Diego County backcountry wells have steel casing that corrodes and sheds rust into the water, and older homes often have galvanized plumbing that rusts from the inside. We can determine whether the source is the well, the casing, or the household plumbing — each has a different solution.

What does it cost to fix rusty well water in Potrero?

It depends on the cause. A water softener or iron filter typically runs about $1,500 to $3,500 installed, sediment filtration about $300 to $900, and a well rehabilitation from a few hundred dollars into the low thousands. We start with a roughly $125 diagnostic that we credit toward the work.
