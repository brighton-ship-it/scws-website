Understanding Aquifers and Water Tables

Before diving into Southern California's specific conditions, let's establish the fundamentals of how groundwater works.

What Is an Aquifer?

An aquifer is an underground layer of rock, sand, gravel, or other geological material that contains and transmits water in recoverable quantities. Think of it as an underground reservoir—but instead of an open lake, the water fills the tiny spaces (pores) between rock particles or flows through cracks (fractures) in solid rock.

Not all underground rock is an aquifer. To qualify as an aquifer, the geological formation must have two key properties:

  • Porosity: The rock must have spaces that can hold water—like sand, gravel, or fractured bedrock
  • Permeability: The pores or fractures must be connected so water can flow through them toward a well

Clay, for example, is highly porous (full of tiny spaces) but has low permeability (the spaces aren't well-connected), so it makes a poor aquifer. Gravel, on the other hand, has both high porosity and high permeability, making it an excellent aquifer material.

What Is the Water Table?

The water table is the upper boundary of the saturated zone—the depth below ground where all the available spaces in the soil and rock are filled with water. Above the water table is the unsaturated zone (or vadose zone), where the pores contain both air and water.

When rain falls, it percolates downward through the soil and rock. Some water is absorbed by plant roots, some evaporates, and the rest continues downward until it reaches the saturated zone. This process is called recharge.

The water table isn't flat like a lake's surface—it generally follows the contours of the land surface above it, but more gently. It's higher under hills and lower under valleys. It rises during wet periods when recharge exceeds extraction, and falls during dry periods or when pumping is heavy.

📊 Visualizing the Water Table

Imagine a cross-section of the ground beneath your property:

  • 0-100 ft: Unsaturated zone—soil and rock with air in the pores
  • 100 ft: Water table—the level where pores become fully saturated
  • 100-400 ft: Saturated zone (aquifer)—all pore spaces filled with water
  • 400+ ft: Impermeable bedrock or confining layer (in some cases)

Your well casing extends through the unsaturated zone, and your pump sits in the saturated zone below the water table. As you pump water out, the water table around your well drops slightly, creating a "cone of depression" that recovers when pumping stops.

Recharge Zones and Water Movement

Groundwater isn't static—it's constantly moving, though very slowly (sometimes just inches per year in rock, or several feet per day in gravel). Water enters the aquifer in recharge zones, typically in higher elevations where rain and snowmelt percolate downward, then flows laterally through the aquifer until it's extracted by wells or emerges as springs and streams in discharge zones.

In Southern California, natural recharge happens primarily during winter and spring when rainfall is highest. Urban development, pavement, and altered drainage patterns have significantly reduced natural recharge in many areas. Some water districts now operate managed aquifer recharge (MAR) projects, where they intentionally divert stormwater or imported water into spreading basins to recharge depleted aquifers.

Types of Aquifers: Confined vs Unconfined

Southern California has both confined and unconfined aquifers, and understanding which type you're drawing from affects well design, water availability, and vulnerability to contamination.

Unconfined Aquifers (Water Table Aquifers)

Characteristics: Unconfined aquifers have no impermeable layer above them—they're directly connected to the surface through permeable materials. The water table forms the upper boundary of the saturated zone.

Recharge: Direct and relatively fast—rainfall can percolate down through overlying soils and reach the aquifer within days to months.

Water table behavior: Fluctuates seasonally and responds quickly to rainfall and pumping. Water levels can vary 20-50+ feet between wet and dry seasons.

Vulnerability: More susceptible to surface contamination because there's no protective confining layer. Nitrates from septic systems, fertilizers, and animal waste can reach unconfined aquifers.

Well design: Wells must be drilled through the unsaturated zone to below the water table. Pumps are set well below the lowest anticipated water table level to avoid running dry during droughts.

Common in: Alluvial valleys, coastal plains, and areas with sedimentary deposits like San Diego's inland valleys (Ramona, Valley Center, San Pasqual Valley).

Confined Aquifers (Artesian Aquifers)

Characteristics: Confined aquifers are sandwiched between impermeable layers (called aquitards or confining layers) above and below. The water in a confined aquifer is under pressure from the weight of the overlying rock.

Recharge: Indirect and slow—confined aquifers can only recharge where the confining layer is absent or broken, often many miles away at higher elevations. Water may take decades or even centuries to travel through the aquifer.

Water level behavior: The water level in a well drilled into a confined aquifer rises above the top of the aquifer due to artesian pressure. This level is called the potentiometric surface. If the potentiometric surface is above ground level, you have a flowing artesian well that flows naturally without a pump. More commonly, the potentiometric surface is below ground but above the aquifer depth—this is a non-flowing artesian well.

Vulnerability: Less vulnerable to surface contamination because the confining layer protects the aquifer from surface pollutants. However, once contaminated, confined aquifers are very difficult to clean because recharge and water movement are so slow.

Well design: Wells must penetrate the confining layer to reach the aquifer. Water will rise in the well casing due to artesian pressure. The pump can be set at the static water level (potentiometric surface) rather than at the aquifer depth.

Common in: Deeper sedimentary basins in Southern California, some coastal areas where clay layers confine deeper aquifer zones, and certain geological formations with interbedded permeable and impermeable layers.

Perched Aquifers

A perched aquifer is a special case—a small, local saturated zone sitting above the main water table, separated by an impermeable layer. Perched aquifers form when water percolating downward encounters a clay lens or other impermeable layer and accumulates above it. They're typically small, unreliable, and can dry up seasonally. Some shallow wells accidentally tap perched aquifers instead of the main aquifer and experience seasonal water shortages.

Southern California Aquifer Systems

Southern California's geology creates diverse aquifer systems, from coastal alluvial basins to fractured mountain bedrock. Understanding which system you're tapping is crucial for well design and water management.

San Diego Formation Aquifer

The San Diego Formation is a Tertiary-age sedimentary formation (approximately 50 million years old) that underlies much of central and inland San Diego County. It consists of weakly consolidated to moderately consolidated sandstone, siltstone, shale, and conglomerate layers.

Geographic extent: Widespread from Escondido and Valley Center through Ramona, Poway, and parts of Alpine and East County

Aquifer type: Primarily unconfined, though local confining layers create semi-confined conditions in some areas

Water table depth: Typically 100-400 feet below surface, varying with topography and season

Well depth range: Most productive wells are 200-600 feet deep

Typical well yield: 5-50 GPM (gallons per minute), with better yields in the more permeable sandstone layers

Water quality: Generally good, though hardness is common (10-30 grains per gallon). Some areas have elevated TDS (total dissolved solids) or nitrates from septic systems and agriculture

Challenges: Variable permeability—wells may encounter tight siltstone layers with poor yield. Drought has significantly depleted this aquifer in some areas, dropping water tables 50-100+ feet since 2012.

Alluvial Basin Aquifers

Alluvial aquifers consist of unconsolidated sand, gravel, silt, and clay deposited by streams and rivers in valley bottoms. These are typically the most productive aquifers but also the most vulnerable to over-pumping and contamination.

Major alluvial basins in San Diego County:

  • San Pasqual Valley: Deep alluvium (up to 400+ feet) with high productivity; heavily used for agriculture
  • Santa Maria Valley: Ramona area; significant agricultural pumping has depleted water levels
  • Lower Sweetwater Valley: Smaller alluvial deposits in East County
  • San Luis Rey Valley: Coastal to inland valley with variable alluvium thickness
  • Temecula Valley: Extends into Riverside County; heavily developed

Aquifer type: Unconfined

Water table depth: 50-200 feet in valley centers, deeper near valley edges

Well depth range: 100-400 feet

Typical well yield: 10-100+ GPM—alluvial aquifers typically have the highest yields

Challenges: High vulnerability to contamination from agriculture (nitrates, pesticides), septic systems, and industrial activities. Many alluvial basins are overdrafted, with pumping exceeding recharge. SGMA groundwater sustainability plans may regulate pumping in some basins.

Fractured Rock Aquifers

In the mountains and eastern portions of San Diego, Riverside, and San Bernardino counties, water occurs primarily in fractured crystalline bedrock—granitic and metamorphic rocks where groundwater flows through cracks, fractures, and joints rather than through pore spaces.

Geographic extent: Julian, Palomar Mountain, Cuyamaca Mountains, Laguna Mountains, San Bernardino Mountains (Big Bear, Lake Arrowhead, Crestline, Running Springs)

Aquifer type: Unconfined, though weathered zones may create localized semi-confined conditions

Water table depth: Highly variable—200-800+ feet, depending on topography, fracture patterns, and elevation

Well depth range: 300-1,000+ feet—often drilled until adequate yield is found or depth limit is reached

Typical well yield: 1-20 GPM—highly unpredictable. Some wells hit productive fracture zones and yield 20+ GPM; others find minimal water even at great depths. Yield depends entirely on encountering water-bearing fractures.

Water quality: Generally excellent—low mineralization and minimal contamination risk due to remote locations. Some areas have naturally low pH or elevated radon.

Challenges: Unpredictable well yield; expensive drilling due to hard rock and deep wells; limited storage capacity means wells can't support high continuous pumping rates. See our guide to well drilling costs for fractured rock drilling pricing.

Other Geological Formations

  • Mission Valley Formation: Coastal areas of San Diego; provides limited groundwater, mostly extracted by municipal water districts
  • Otay Formation: South Bay and Otay areas; interbedded sandstone and claystone with variable water-bearing characteristics
  • Lindavista Formation: Terrace deposits in coastal areas; shallow and often perched
  • Pauba Formation: Temecula/Murrieta area in Riverside County; sedimentary formation similar to San Diego Formation

⚠️ Mountain Well Reality Check

Mountain wells in fractured rock are inherently unpredictable. Two wells 100 feet apart can have completely different yields and water table depths depending on which fractures they intersect. Always get a professional hydrogeological assessment before purchasing mountain property without an existing well.

San Diego County Groundwater Basins

The California Department of Water Resources (DWR) has designated several groundwater basins in San Diego County under the SGMA framework. Understanding which basin you're in helps you access monitoring data and understand management priorities.

Major Groundwater Basins

San Pasqual Valley Groundwater Basin (Basin 9-6)

Location: Northeast of Escondido, extending from Lake Hodges to near Ramona

Area: Approximately 13,900 acres

Aquifer: Alluvial deposits up to 400+ feet thick

Status: High priority basin under SGMA; Groundwater Sustainability Plan (GSP) required

Challenges: Heavy agricultural pumping has caused overdraft conditions. Water levels dropped significantly during 2012-2016 drought and recovery has been slow.

Warner Valley Groundwater Basin (Basin 9-10)

Location: Near Warner Springs in northern San Diego County

Area: Approximately 6,600 acres

Aquifer: Alluvial and volcanic deposits

Status: Low priority; not subject to SGMA requirements at this time

Borrego Valley Groundwater Basin (Basin 7-11)

Location: Anza-Borrego Desert, eastern San Diego County

Area: Approximately 71,000 acres

Aquifer: Deep alluvial deposits (up to 3,000+ feet)

Status: High priority, critically overdrafted; GSP being implemented

Challenges: Severe overdraft from agricultural pumping exceeds recharge by 3-5 times. Water levels have dropped 100-150+ feet in some areas. SGMA regulations are reducing pumping allocations.

Lower Sweetwater Valley Groundwater Basin (Basin 9-5)

Location: East of National City/Bonita

Area: Approximately 3,400 acres

Aquifer: Alluvial deposits

Status: Very low priority; limited groundwater use

Note: Much of San Diego County's groundwater is outside designated basins, occurring in non-basin areas with fractured rock or sedimentary formations like the San Diego Formation. These areas aren't subject to SGMA regulations unless they're later designated as basins.

Riverside County Water Table Conditions

Riverside County encompasses diverse landscapes from valleys to mountains, with correspondingly varied water table conditions.

Temecula Valley / Wine Country

Typical water table depth: 50-250 feet in valley floor; 200-400+ feet on hillsides

Aquifer: Pauba Formation (sedimentary), alluvial deposits in valley bottoms

Groundwater basin: Temecula Valley Groundwater Basin—high priority under SGMA

Well yields: 10-50 GPM typical; some high-capacity wells for vineyards and agriculture

Issues: Rapid development and agricultural expansion have stressed the basin. Water levels have declined 20-50 feet in some areas. SGMA implementation may impose pumping restrictions.

Hemet / San Jacinto Valley

Typical water table depth: 100-300 feet in valley areas

Aquifer: San Jacinto Groundwater Basin—alluvial deposits

Groundwater basin: San Jacinto Groundwater Basin (high priority under SGMA)

Well yields: Highly variable; 5-50+ GPM

Issues: Historical overdraft; water levels have declined significantly. Managed recharge programs are being implemented. Some areas have water quality issues (TDS, arsenic).

Anza / Aguanga / Sage

Typical water table depth: 200-500+ feet

Aquifer: Mixed—fractured bedrock and sedimentary formations

Well yields: 3-20 GPM typical; unpredictable

Characteristics: Rural, mountainous transition zone. Wells often encounter fractured rock at depth. Water table highly variable depending on local geology and topography.

Murrieta / Wildomar

Typical water table depth: 100-300 feet

Aquifer: Alluvial and sedimentary formations

Groundwater basin: Part of Murrieta/Temecula area basins

Notes: Much of this area now has municipal water service, but outlying properties still rely on wells. Groundwater sustainability is a concern with rapid urbanization.

San Bernardino Mountain Communities

The San Bernardino Mountains present unique groundwater challenges due to high elevation, fractured bedrock geology, and seasonal population fluctuations.

Big Bear Lake / Big Bear City

Typical water table depth: 300-700+ feet

Aquifer: Fractured granite and metamorphic rocks

Well depths: 400-1,000+ feet common

Well yields: 1-15 GPM typical; some wells produce more if they hit productive fracture zones

Water source alternatives: Many properties use Big Bear Municipal Water District service where available

Challenges: Expensive drilling in hard rock; unpredictable yields; seasonal demand from vacation homes. Wells may struggle during summer peak usage periods.

Lake Arrowhead / Rim Forest

Typical water table depth: 200-600 feet

Aquifer: Fractured bedrock

Well depths: 300-800 feet typical

Well yields: 3-20 GPM

Notes: Arrowhead Lake Company provides water to most Lake Arrowhead properties. Outlying areas rely on individual wells. Hard rock drilling is expensive ($25-$50+ per foot).

Crestline / Lake Gregory

Typical water table depth: 150-500 feet

Aquifer: Mixed—some alluvial deposits, fractured bedrock

Well depths: 200-600 feet

Well yields: 5-25 GPM typical

Notes: Crestline has a water district providing service to much of the area. Outlying properties use private wells.

Running Springs / Arrowbear

Typical water table depth: 200-600+ feet

Aquifer: Fractured bedrock

Well depths: 300-800 feet

Characteristics: Running Springs Water District serves many properties. Private wells are common in Arrowbear and surrounding mountain areas. Water table varies significantly with topography.

🏔️ Mountain Well Economics

Drilling wells in mountain communities is significantly more expensive than valley wells due to hard rock geology, deeper water tables, and access challenges. Budget $15,000-$40,000+ for a fractured bedrock well. Always verify water availability before purchasing mountain property without an existing well or water district connection.

Seasonal Fluctuations and Drought Impact

Water tables aren't static—they rise and fall with the seasons, and can drop dramatically during multi-year droughts.

Normal Seasonal Variation

In Southern California's Mediterranean climate, groundwater recharge occurs primarily during the winter rainy season (November-March). Water tables typically:

  • Rise in late winter/spring as rainfall percolates down to the saturated zone
  • Peak in late spring (April-May) after the rainy season ends but before heavy summer pumping
  • Decline through summer and fall as wells pump water out faster than natural recharge occurs
  • Reach their lowest point in September-October before winter rains begin

Typical seasonal fluctuation: 10-30 feet in unconfined alluvial aquifers; 5-15 feet in sedimentary formations; minimal fluctuation (2-5 feet) in deep confined aquifers.

Drought Impact: The 2012-2016 California Drought

The 2012-2016 drought was one of the most severe in California's recorded history, causing unprecedented water table declines throughout Southern California:

📉 Documented Water Table Declines (2012-2016)

  • Ramona/Valley Center: 50-100+ feet in many monitoring wells
  • San Pasqual Valley: 40-80 feet
  • Borrego Valley: 80-120+ feet (continuing long-term decline)
  • Temecula Valley: 30-60 feet
  • Hemet/San Jacinto: 40-70 feet

Impacts on well owners:

  • Thousands of wells went dry or experienced reduced flow
  • Emergency well deepening and pump lowering services were backlogged for months
  • Some wells that had operated reliably for decades suddenly failed
  • Well drilling costs skyrocketed due to high demand
  • Rural communities faced water hauling and emergency water deliveries

Recovery and Current Conditions (2017-2026)

While the drought officially ended in 2017 with above-average rainfall, groundwater recovery has been slow and incomplete:

  • Partial recovery: Most basins recovered 30-60% of drought losses during 2017-2019 wet years
  • Ongoing deficits: Many areas remain 20-50 feet below pre-drought levels
  • New drought cycles: 2020-2022 saw renewed dry conditions, halting or reversing recovery
  • SGMA implementation: New groundwater management may prevent full recovery in some basins as sustainable pumping limits are enforced

Long-Term Trends and Climate Change

Climate projections for Southern California suggest:

  • More frequent and severe droughts
  • Greater year-to-year precipitation variability
  • Reduced snowpack in mountains, affecting recharge timing
  • Increased water demand from higher temperatures
  • Potential for permanent water table decline in overdrafted basins

For well owners, this means groundwater monitoring and conservation are more critical than ever. See our drought and dry well solutions guide for strategies to protect your water supply.

How to Monitor Your Well's Water Level

Regular water level monitoring helps you detect problems early, track seasonal patterns, and make informed decisions about well management.

Understanding Static vs. Pumping Water Level

Static Water Level (SWL): The depth to water when the well isn't pumping and has had time to recover (usually 2-4 hours after pumping stops). This represents the water table elevation at your well.

Pumping Water Level: The depth to water while the pump is running. This is always deeper than the static level because pumping creates a cone of depression around the well.

Important: Always measure and record the static water level for meaningful comparisons over time. Pumping levels vary with pump size and usage patterns.

DIY Water Level Measurement Methods

1. Water Level Sounder (Electronic)

How it works: A probe on a marked cable is lowered down the well casing. When the probe touches water, it completes an electrical circuit and triggers a light or beeper.

Cost: $150-$400 for a basic model

Pros: Accurate, easy to use, works in any well depth

Cons: Initial cost; requires access to well casing

Best for: Well owners who want to monitor regularly and accurately

2. Weighted Tape Measure

How it works: A steel measuring tape with a weight (chalked cylinder) on the end. Lower it down the well until the weight makes a splash sound or you see a wet mark on the chalk when you pull it up.

Cost: $20-$100

Pros: Very inexpensive; no batteries or electronics

Cons: Harder to use; splash sound can be difficult to hear in deep wells

Best for: Budget-conscious well owners; shallow wells

3. Pressure Transducer (Continuous Monitoring)

How it works: An electronic sensor installed in the well continuously measures water level based on pressure. Can log data or transmit wirelessly to a monitoring system.

Cost: $300-$1,500+ installed

Pros: Continuous monitoring; automatic data logging; can set up alerts for low water levels

Cons: Higher cost; requires professional installation; needs power source

Best for: High-value properties; commercial wells; properties with history of water level problems; remote properties you can monitor online

When and How to Measure

📅 Recommended Monitoring Schedule

  • Annually minimum: Measure at the same time each year (e.g., late summer/early fall when levels are lowest) to track long-term trends
  • Seasonally: Measure spring (high water table) and fall (low water table) to understand seasonal range
  • After pump work: Measure before and after any pump repair or lowering
  • During drought: Measure monthly or quarterly to monitor declining levels
  • When problems occur: Measure if you notice reduced flow, air spitting, or other performance issues

Recording and Interpreting Measurements

Create a water level log with the following information:

  • Date and time of measurement
  • Depth to water (feet below top of casing or measuring point)
  • How long pump was off before measurement (2+ hours for static level)
  • Recent rainfall (past week)
  • Any changes in well performance or problems noted
  • Changes in neighborhood (new wells drilled nearby, etc.)

What to look for:

  • Long-term declining trend: Water table dropping year after year indicates overdraft or extended drought
  • Normal seasonal pattern: Higher in spring, lower in fall—typically 10-30 feet variation
  • Sudden drop: Could indicate new nearby pumping, aquifer damage, or equipment problems
  • Approaching pump depth: If water level is getting close to your pump intake depth, plan to lower the pump before it runs dry

DWR Groundwater Monitoring Resources

California's Department of Water Resources maintains extensive groundwater monitoring programs and data resources available to the public.

CASGEM: California Statewide Groundwater Elevation Monitoring

What it is: A statewide program that collects and publishes groundwater level data from monitoring wells across California.

How to access: Visit the SGMA Data Viewer to view groundwater level data for monitoring wells near your property.

What you'll find: Historical water level measurements from monitoring wells, showing seasonal patterns and long-term trends. You can see how your local aquifer has responded to drought, wet years, and pumping patterns.

Limitations: Monitoring wells may not be right in your neighborhood. Use nearby well data as a general indicator of basin conditions, but your specific well may behave differently based on local geology.

SGMA Portal and Groundwater Sustainability Plans

What it is: The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) requires high and medium priority groundwater basins to develop and implement Groundwater Sustainability Plans (GSPs).

How to access: Visit the SGMA Portal to find your basin's GSP and monitoring data.

What you'll find: Detailed basin studies, historical water level data, pumping estimates, projected sustainable yield, and proposed management actions.

Why it matters: If you're in a SGMA-regulated basin, the GSP may eventually restrict pumping, require metering, or impose extraction fees. Review your basin's GSP to understand future regulations that could affect your well.

Well Completion Reports

What it is: Every well drilled in California requires a Well Completion Report (WCR) filed with DWR. These reports include the static water level when the well was drilled.

How to access: Search the DWR Well Completion Report Database using your address or APN (Assessor's Parcel Number).

What you'll find: Your well's original static water level, total depth, perforated intervals, estimated yield, and other construction details. You can compare current water levels to original drilling levels to see long-term changes.

Tip: Look up neighboring wells too—you can see what water table depths and yields others found in your immediate area.

Impact of Neighbor Wells on Your Water Table

Groundwater doesn't respect property lines. When your neighbor pumps their well, they're drawing from the same aquifer you are—and that can affect your water table.

The Cone of Depression

When a well pumps, it creates a cone of depression—a funnel-shaped drawdown in the water table centered on the pumping well. The size and depth of this cone depend on:

  • Pumping rate: Higher GPM = larger, deeper cone
  • Pumping duration: Continuous pumping expands the cone over time
  • Aquifer properties: In low-permeability aquifers, cones extend farther because water moves slowly toward the well
  • Aquifer type: Unconfined aquifers have localized cones; confined aquifers can transmit pressure effects for miles

How Far Can a Well Affect Neighbors?

Well Type Pumping Rate Radius of Influence Impact on Neighbors
Domestic well 5-15 GPM 100-300 feet Minimal impact beyond property in most cases
Agricultural well 50-500+ GPM 1,000-5,000 feet Can affect neighbors up to a mile away
Municipal well 500-2,000+ GPM 1-5+ miles Basin-wide impacts possible
Commercial well (bottling, industry) 100-1,000+ GPM 0.5-3 miles Significant impact on surrounding area

Signs a Neighbor's Well Is Affecting Yours

  • Your water level drops significantly after a neighbor drills a new well or expands pumping
  • You notice reduced water pressure or pump running longer, coinciding with neighbor's irrigation schedule
  • Your well performs fine in winter/spring but struggles in summer when neighbor's agricultural pumping is heavy
  • Multiple neighbors are experiencing similar declining water levels at the same time

California Groundwater Rights and Remedies

California follows the "reasonable use" doctrine for groundwater. This means:

  • Overlying landowners (those on top of the aquifer) have correlative rights—equal rights to reasonable use
  • During shortages, users must share the available supply proportionally
  • Unreasonable use that harms neighbors can be challenged legally
  • In adjudicated basins, pumping rights are formally determined and enforced

If you believe a neighbor's pumping is harming your well:

  1. Document your water levels before and after the neighbor's well activity
  2. Document any changes in well performance
  3. Check if you're in a SGMA-regulated basin with pumping restrictions
  4. Attempt to resolve the issue directly with the neighbor if possible
  5. Contact your county environmental health or water resources department
  6. Consult a water rights attorney if the problem is severe and negotiation fails

Reality check: Groundwater rights litigation is expensive and complex. In most cases, the practical solution is to deepen your well, lower your pump, or reduce your own water use rather than pursue legal action. However, if a large commercial operation is causing widespread impacts, collective action with other affected neighbors may be warranted.

Signs Your Water Table Is Dropping

Recognizing early warning signs of a declining water table can help you take action before you lose water completely.

Performance-Based Warning Signs

⚠️ Pump Running Longer or More Frequently

Your pressure tank should fill in a predictable time. If the pump is suddenly running 2-3 times longer to fill the tank, the water level may have dropped and the pump has less capacity.

⚠️ Reduced Water Pressure

Lower pressure at fixtures, especially during peak use times, can indicate the well isn't keeping up with demand as water levels drop.

⚠️ Air Sputtering from Faucets

If the water table drops below your pump intake, the pump will draw air along with water, causing sputtering, coughing, and air bursts from faucets.

⚠️ Pump Short Cycling

The pump turns on and off rapidly because it can't deliver enough water to maintain system pressure. This can damage the pump motor.

⚠️ Pump Running Dry and Shutting Off

If the water table drops completely below the pump, it will run dry and shut off on thermal overload protection. This requires immediate attention to prevent pump damage.

⚠️ Sandy or Gritty Water

As water levels drop, the pump may draw from the very bottom of the well where sediment accumulates, causing cloudy or sandy water.

Environmental and Regional Indicators

  • Neighbors reporting well problems: If multiple neighbors are experiencing issues, it's a basin-wide problem, not just your well
  • Extended drought conditions: Multi-year droughts deplete aquifers faster than they can recharge
  • New wells or increased pumping nearby: Agricultural expansion or new development can strain local aquifers
  • Springs and creeks drying up: Surface water features fed by groundwater will dry up as the water table drops
  • Declining levels in monitoring wells: Check DWR CASGEM data for regional trends

When to Take Action

Don't wait until your well completely fails. Take action if:

  • Your static water level has dropped 20+ feet below the original level when the well was drilled
  • The water table is within 50 feet of your pump intake depth
  • You're experiencing any of the performance warning signs above
  • Your area is in official drought status with no rain forecasted
  • Neighbors are experiencing well failures

Proactive action is always cheaper than emergency service when your well is completely dry. See our guide on what to do when you have no water from your well.

What to Do If Water Table Drops Below Your Pump

If your water table has dropped to or below your pump intake, you need to act quickly to restore water service and prevent pump damage.

Immediate Steps

  1. Turn off the pump: If your pump is running dry and sputtering air, shut it off immediately at the breaker to prevent motor burnout. Don't repeatedly try to run a dry pump.
  2. Measure your static water level: Determine exactly how deep the water table is currently. You need this information to evaluate your options.
  3. Review your well log: Check your Well Completion Report to see your total well depth, current pump setting depth, and original static water level.
  4. Calculate available depth: Determine how much well depth remains below your current pump for potential pump lowering.

Solution 1: Lower the Pump

When it works: If you have adequate well depth below your current pump setting and the well has sufficient yield at greater depth

The process: A well technician pulls the pump, extends the drop pipe and wiring (or replaces with longer sections), and reinstalls the pump 50-100+ feet deeper where water is available

Cost: $800-$2,500 depending on depth, pump size, and whether new pipe/wire is needed

Pros: Usually the most cost-effective solution; can be done quickly (1 day); preserves existing well investment

Cons: Only works if you have available depth; if the entire aquifer is depleted, lowering won't help

Considerations: Leave at least 20-30 feet of casing below the new pump setting for sediment storage. Don't set the pump right at the bottom of the well.

Solution 2: Deepen the Existing Well

When it works: If you've reached the bottom of your well but geological conditions below are likely to produce water

The process: A drill rig re-enters your existing well and drills deeper, extending the casing and perforations into new water-bearing zones

Cost: $3,000-$10,000+ depending on depth added (typically $15-$40/foot plus mobilization)

Pros: Expands your well's capacity; may access deeper, more reliable aquifer zones

Cons: More expensive than pump lowering; no guarantee of finding adequate water; may not be feasible if existing casing is damaged

Considerations: Get a professional evaluation first—some geological formations don't get better with depth. In fractured rock, deeper doesn't always mean more water.

Solution 3: Drill a New Well

When it's necessary: If your existing well can't be deepened, if deepening won't help due to geological constraints, or if the well casing is damaged

The process: Drill a completely new well in a different location or to a different aquifer zone. May require county permits.

Cost: $15,000-$40,000+ for a complete new well system (drilling, casing, pump, pressure tank, electrical)

Pros: Fresh start with a new, properly-designed well; can target deeper, more reliable aquifer zones

Cons: Most expensive option; permitting may be required; no guarantee the new well will perform better

Considerations: In some counties, new well permits are restricted or require proof that existing well can't be rehabilitated. Check with your county environmental health department. See our well drilling cost guide for detailed pricing.

Solution 4: Water Conservation and Storage

When it works: As a supplemental solution or if your well can provide adequate water with reduced demand

Strategies:

  • Install a large storage tank (500-2,500 gallons) to pump water slowly when the well can produce it
  • Implement aggressive water conservation (low-flow fixtures, drought-tolerant landscaping, shorter showers)
  • Limit high-demand activities (no lawn watering, minimal laundry)
  • Reduce occupancy if a vacation home

Cost: $500-$5,000 for storage tank system plus conservation measures

Limitations: Only works if your well can still produce water, just not enough to meet peak demand. Doesn't solve a completely dry well.

Choosing the Right Solution

The best solution depends on your specific situation. A professional well evaluation should assess:

  • Current static water level vs. original level
  • Available well depth below current pump
  • Well yield at current vs. deeper depths (via pumping test if needed)
  • Geological formation and likelihood of finding water deeper
  • Condition of existing well casing
  • Regional water table trends (is this temporary or permanent decline?)
  • Cost-benefit of repair vs. replacement

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep is the water table in Southern California?

Water table depth in Southern California varies dramatically by location and geology. Coastal San Diego areas typically have water tables 50-200 feet deep, while inland valleys like Ramona and Valley Center range from 100-400 feet. Mountain communities like Julian and Palomar Mountain often see water tables 200-600+ feet deep. Desert and high-desert areas can have water tables 300-800+ feet below the surface. Seasonal fluctuations of 10-50 feet are common, with levels dropping during drought and rising during wet years. Urban areas with extensive groundwater pumping may have much deeper water tables than undeveloped areas.

What is the difference between confined and unconfined aquifers?

Unconfined aquifers (also called water table aquifers) have no impermeable layer above them—water percolates down from the surface until it reaches the saturated zone where all spaces in the rock or soil are filled with water. The top of this saturated zone is the water table, which rises and falls with precipitation and pumping. Confined aquifers are sandwiched between impermeable layers (aquitards) above and below. Water in confined aquifers is under pressure because the weight of overlying rock compresses it. When you drill into a confined aquifer, water may rise above the aquifer level (artesian pressure) or even flow naturally at the surface (flowing artesian well). Confined aquifers recharge only where the confining layer is absent or fractured, often many miles away from the well.

How do I find my water table depth?

To find your water table depth, you can: (1) Measure the static water level in your well using a water level sounder or weighted tape—drop it down the well casing until you hear a splash or see the measurement change, then read the depth. (2) Check your well driller's report (required in California)—it shows the static water level when the well was drilled. (3) Review DWR CASGEM data online for nearby monitoring wells. (4) Contact your local water district or county environmental health department for groundwater basin data. (5) Hire a well professional to measure and assess your well's water level. For active wells, measure when the pump has been off at least 2-4 hours to get an accurate static water level, not the pumping level.

What causes water table levels to drop?

Water table levels drop when groundwater extraction (pumping) exceeds natural recharge. Primary causes include: (1) Drought—reduced rainfall means less water percolating down to recharge aquifers. (2) Excessive pumping—too many wells extracting water faster than it can be replenished. (3) Increased water demand—population growth, agriculture expansion, or landscape irrigation. (4) Reduced recharge areas—urban development and pavement prevent rainwater from soaking into the ground. (5) Climate change—shifting precipitation patterns and reduced snowpack. (6) Neighbor wells—nearby wells drawing from the same aquifer create a cone of depression that lowers water levels. In Southern California, the 2012-2016 drought dropped water tables 50-100+ feet in many areas, and some basins still haven't fully recovered.

How does SGMA affect my well?

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), enacted in 2014, requires critically overdrafted and high-priority groundwater basins to develop sustainability plans to prevent depletion. If your well is in a basin covered by SGMA, the local Groundwater Sustainability Agency (GSA) may implement pumping allocations, well metering requirements, or extraction fees to balance pumping with recharge. Some GSAs are already requiring groundwater extraction reports and may limit new well permits. However, most rural residential wells pumping less than 2 acre-feet per year are exempt from pumping restrictions. SGMA primarily affects agricultural and municipal users. Check if your property is in a SGMA-regulated basin using the DWR SGMA Portal—if so, monitor your GSA's sustainability plan for potential future impacts on domestic wells.

Will my neighbor's well affect my water table?

Yes, nearby wells can affect your water table, especially if they pump heavily. When a well pumps, it creates a cone of depression—a funnel-shaped drop in the water table centered on the pumping well. The size and depth of this cone depends on pumping rate, duration, and aquifer characteristics. A heavily-pumped agricultural or commercial well can lower the water table hundreds of feet away. Residential wells typically have minimal impact beyond 100-300 feet. In unconfined aquifers, the effect is more localized; in confined aquifers, the pressure drop can extend for miles. If a neighbor drills a deeper well or increases pumping, you may notice your well's static water level dropping or your pump cycling more frequently. California's reasonable use doctrine governs groundwater rights, but enforcement is complex. If you suspect interference, document your water levels before and after the neighbor's well activity.

What are signs my water table is dropping?

Signs of a dropping water table include: (1) Well pump running longer or more frequently to fill the pressure tank. (2) Reduced water pressure at fixtures. (3) Air sputtering from faucets (pump drawing air if water level drops below pump intake). (4) Pump short cycling—turning on and off rapidly. (5) Pump running dry and shutting off on thermal protection. (6) Neighbors reporting similar well problems. (7) Nearby springs or creeks drying up. (8) Documented decline in static water level measurements over time. (9) Well that previously never had issues now struggling during dry season. (10) Sandy or gritty water (pump pulling sediment from bottom of well). If you notice these signs, measure your static water level and compare it to your well log. If the water level has dropped significantly, you may need to lower the pump, deepen the well, or reduce water usage.

What should I do if my water table drops below my pump?

If your water table drops below your pump intake, you have several options: (1) Lower the pump—if you have adequate well depth below the current pump setting, a well professional can lower the pump deeper where water is available. This is the most common and cost-effective solution ($800-$2,000). (2) Deepen the well—drill deeper to access water below the current well bottom ($3,000-$8,000 depending on depth and geology). (3) Drill a new well—if deepening isn't feasible, drill a new well in a different location or to a deeper aquifer ($15,000-$40,000+). (4) Install a storage tank—pump during peak water table periods and store water for use during low periods (requires well recovery testing). (5) Reduce water usage—conservation can help your well keep up with reduced water availability. Before choosing, have a well professional measure your static water level, assess remaining well depth, and evaluate aquifer productivity through a pumping test.

How can I monitor my well's water level?

Monitor your well's water level using these methods: (1) Water level sounder—electronic devices that beep or light up when the probe touches water. Drop it down the well casing and read the depth on the marked tape ($150-$400). (2) Weighted tape—a simple tape measure with a weight that makes a splash sound when it hits water ($20-$100). (3) Pressure transducer—electronic sensors that continuously measure water level and can log data or transmit wirelessly ($300-$1,500 installed). (4) Annual professional inspection—have a well service company measure and document water levels yearly ($100-$200). Measure when the pump has been off for at least 2-4 hours to get static water level, not pumping level. Record measurements with dates and compare over time. Measure at the same time each year (e.g., late summer when levels are lowest) for consistent trending. Keep a log with dates, measurements, and any changes in well performance.

What are the main aquifer systems in San Diego County?

San Diego County has several major aquifer systems: (1) San Diego Formation—a Tertiary-age sedimentary formation widespread in central San Diego County, consisting of sandstone, siltstone, and conglomerate. This is the most productive aquifer for domestic wells in areas like Ramona, Valley Center, and Escondido, typically yielding 5-50 GPM at depths of 100-600 feet. (2) Alluvial basin aquifers—unconsolidated sand, gravel, and clay deposits in valley bottoms like San Pasqual Valley, Santa Maria Valley, and Lower Sweetwater Valley. These shallow aquifers (50-200 feet) can be highly productive but are vulnerable to contamination and overdraft. (3) Fractured crystalline rock aquifers—granitic and metamorphic rocks in the mountains and eastern county. Water flows through fractures and joints rather than pore spaces. Well yields are unpredictable (1-20 GPM) and depths range from 200-800+ feet. (4) Mission Valley Formation—provides some groundwater in coastal areas. (5) Quaternary alluvium—recent deposits along major drainages, often shallow and seasonally variable.
