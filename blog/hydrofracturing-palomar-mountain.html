What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Palomar Mountain Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing on Palomar Mountain: Why a Wet Mountain Can Still Have a Thirsty Well

Palomar Mountain sits among the highest communities in San Diego County, a forested ridge climbing to roughly 5,500 feet above the inland valleys. It is a place defined by tall conifers, cool air, winter snow, and the famous white dome of Palomar Observatory looking out over the night sky. For the families who live in cabins and on the large wooded parcels scattered across the mountain and around Palomar Mountain State Park, that elevation and forest cover come with a quiet daily reality: almost every home depends on a private well drilled into granite. When that well slows down, there is no municipal main to fall back on. There is only the rock, the rain, and how well the two are connected.

What surprises many mountain residents is that good precipitation does not guarantee a strong well. Palomar Mountain receives far more rain and snow than the dry country below it, yet wells here still run thin. The reason is geology. At Southern California Well Service, we have, since 2020, working in exactly this kind of formation, and hydrofracturing is one of the most effective tools we have for waking up an underperforming mountain well.

The High-Elevation Paradox: Plenty of Rain, Not Enough Flow

Down in the valleys, low well yield is easy to blame on drought. On Palomar Mountain the explanation is less obvious, because the mountain genuinely gets water. Snow lingers in winter, the forest floor stays damp, and seasonal creeks run. So why does a cabin well sometimes sputter in late summer or recover painfully slowly after a shower or a load of laundry?

The answer lies in the bedrock. Palomar Mountain is built largely of granitic and crystalline rock. Water does not sit in this rock the way it sits in a sponge of sand and gravel; instead, it travels through a network of thin cracks and fractures. Rain and snowmelt that land on the surface can only reach your well if there is a connected fracture pathway to carry the water down and across to your borehole. When those fractures are few, narrow, or clogged, the recharge simply cannot keep up with the household, no matter how much precipitation falls on the mountain above.

This is the high-elevation paradox in plain terms: the mountain is wet, but your well is fracture-limited. A neighbor a few hundred feet away can have a healthy well while yours struggles, purely because their borehole happened to intersect a more generous set of cracks. Hydrofracturing is designed to address exactly this bottleneck, not the amount of water on the mountain, but your well's ability to draw on it.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing, sometimes spelled hydrofracking, is a well rehabilitation method that uses nothing but clean, high-pressure water to reopen and clean the natural fractures around your borehole. It has nothing in common with oil and gas fracking; there are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no deep drilling. It is simply water, applied with precision.

The process starts by lowering an inflatable device called a packer into your well casing. We position it below the casing in the open bedrock and inflate it to seal off a target section of the borehole. With that zone isolated, we pump clean water into it at pressures that typically range from about 1,500 to over 3,000 PSI. That pressure does two useful things at once. It pushes open existing hairline fractures that have tightened or silted closed over the years, and it flushes out the fine mineral scale, clay, and rock flour that accumulate on fracture faces and choke off flow.

By moving the packer and repeating the treatment at different depths, we can work several zones in a single borehole. The goal is to reconnect your well with the broader fracture network in the surrounding granite, opening fresh pathways for groundwater to migrate toward the borehole. A full treatment on a mountain well usually takes somewhere between four and eight hours on site, depending on depth and how many zones we treat.

Why Palomar Mountain Wells Underperform Over Time

Even a well that was strong when it was first drilled can fade. On Palomar Mountain we see a recurring set of causes, and most of them respond well to hydrofracturing.

  • Fracture closure and silting. Over years of pumping, the tiny cracks that feed a granite well gradually pack with mineral precipitate and fine sediment. The opening narrows, flow drops, and recovery after each pumping cycle stretches longer.
  • Fracture-limited recharge. Some wells were never well connected to begin with. They sit in tight rock with only a handful of productive cracks, so even abundant rainfall cannot reach them quickly enough.
  • Seasonal swings. Mountain water tables rise with winter snow and fall through the dry late summer and autumn. A marginal well that copes in March can go uncomfortably low by September, when the fracture network is at its lowest charge.

The common thread is that the problem is almost always in the connection between the rock and the well, not a shortage of water on the mountain as a whole. That is precisely the situation hydrofracturing was built for.

Good Candidates, Poor Candidates, and What to Check First

Hydrofracturing is powerful, but it is not a fix for every well, and honest assessment matters. The single biggest factor is the formation your well sits in. Wells completed in hard, fractured granite or crystalline rock, which describes the vast majority of Palomar Mountain wells, are excellent candidates. The high-pressure water has solid rock to work against, so opening and cleaning fractures produces lasting results. By contrast, wells finished in loose sand or gravel respond poorly, because there are no firm fractures to reopen and the pressure simply dissipates into the loose material.

Before we treat anything, we run a careful evaluation so you know what to expect:

  • Pre-treatment flow test. We measure your current yield and recovery rate to establish a clear baseline. Without a baseline, there is no honest way to prove improvement.
  • Downhole video inspection. A camera survey shows us the condition of your casing and lets us locate the fracture zones worth targeting. It also rules out problems that hydrofracturing would not solve, such as a collapsed casing or a failing pump.
  • Treatment. The high-pressure injection itself, zone by zone, with the packer isolating each target section.
  • Post-treatment flow test. A day or two after treatment we re-measure yield and recovery and compare it against your baseline, so the result is documented rather than guessed at.

In formations like the granite under Palomar Mountain, hydrofracturing improves yield in a large majority of wells, and successful treatments commonly multiply the pre-treatment flow rate several times over. We will tell you plainly if your well is one of the few that is unlikely to benefit.

When to Call a Professional

A few patterns are worth acting on rather than waiting out. If your well produces noticeably less than it did a year or two ago, if it runs dry during ordinary peak use such as filling a tub while a dishwasher runs, if recovery after pumping has grown sluggish, or if your pump short-cycles on and off as it chases water that is not there, those are classic signs of a fracture-limited or silted-in well. On a mountain property, where the next option is an expensive new borehole, it makes sense to have the existing well evaluated before assuming it is finished. High-pressure work and downhole equipment belong in the hands of a licensed contractor; this is not a do-it-yourself project on a 300-foot granite borehole.

Realistic Costs, and Why the Math Favors the Mountain Homeowner

Cost is where hydrofracturing makes its strongest case on Palomar Mountain. A hydrofracturing treatment generally runs between $3,000 and $8,000. Drilling a brand-new well, by comparison, typically falls between $18,000 and $42,000, and that range can climb sharply up here.

The mountain itself is the reason. Drilling a new well means getting a heavy rig up winding mountain roads, onto steep and often heavily forested parcels where access is tight and staging space is limited. Site preparation, longer travel, and difficult terrain all add cost and time that a flat valley lot never incurs. When you also consider that a replacement pump for a deep mountain well can run $2,500 to $5,500 on its own, the financial logic of first trying to rehabilitate the well you already have becomes hard to argue with. Hydrofracturing protects an existing, paid-for asset and very often restores it for a fraction of what a new well on this terrain would demand.
