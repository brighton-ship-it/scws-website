Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Bernardino County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Upland well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well Service for Upland Homes, Foothill Estates, and Citrus Properties

Upland sits at the northern edge of the San Bernardino Valley, tucked against the foot of the San Gabriel Mountains in San Bernardino County. Look north from almost anywhere in town and you see Mount Baldy (Mount San Antonio) rising past 10,000 feet, with San Antonio Heights climbing into the canyon below it. The ground here is the classic Inland Empire alluvial fan: fairly flat through the older neighborhoods, then sloping up toward the mountains where the soils turn to coarse gravel and cobble washed down over thousands of years. Neighbors like Rancho Cucamonga, Alta Loma, Claremont, and Ontario share the same geology and the same groundwater basins.

Upland earned the nickname "Lemon Capital" for a reason. This was citrus country long before it was suburbs, and that heritage still shapes the landscape. You will not find many true avocado groves up here the way you do in San Diego County or coastal Ventura, because the foothill frosts and the inland heat are tough on avocados. What you do find are lemon and orange trees, mature ornamental landscapes, rural residential parcels in the higher elevations, and homeowners who depend on a private well to keep all of it green. How a Residential or Agricultural Well System Works

If your water comes from a private well rather than the city main, you are running a small water utility on your own property. Understanding the parts makes troubleshooting far less mysterious, and it helps you describe a problem accurately when you call.

The Borehole and Casing

Your well is a drilled shaft lined with steel or PVC casing to keep the hole from collapsing and to seal out surface contamination. In the Upland foothills, wells commonly draw from the alluvial gravels and deeper groundwater basins beneath the valley floor. Depths vary widely with location and elevation, roughly 200 to 600-plus feet is typical for Inland Empire foothill properties. Slotted screen near the bottom lets groundwater seep in while keeping out the coarsest sand and gravel.

The Submersible Pump

Most modern wells use a submersible pump that hangs deep in the water column on the end of the drop pipe. It pushes water up to the surface. Because it lives underwater and runs on a motor, the pump is usually the most expensive single component and the one most likely to eventually wear out, especially in mineral-rich foothill water.

The Pressure Tank and Pressure Switch

Water from the pump fills a pressure tank, which stores a cushion of pressurized water so the pump does not have to switch on every time you open a faucet. A pressure switch senses the tank pressure and tells the pump when to start and stop, typically cycling between a low and high set point such as 40 and 60 PSI. When this small switch fails, the symptoms can look dramatic even though the fix is inexpensive.

Filtration and Treatment

Inland Empire groundwater is generally hard and often carries iron, manganese, or fine sediment. A sediment filter protects fixtures and irrigation emitters from grit, while an iron or manganese filter, or a water softener, addresses staining, odor, and scale. On citrus and landscape properties, clean water also means drip lines and micro-sprinklers that do not clog.

Watering Scenarios Around Upland

Different properties lean on their wells in different ways, and the right system reflects how you actually use water.

  • Citrus and orchard remnants: Lemon and orange trees on the old grove parcels need steady, even water through the dry summer. Consistent pressure and clog-free emitters matter more than raw volume.
  • Mature landscapes and large lots: Many Upland and Alta Loma properties carry established trees, lawns, and slopes that need to run several irrigation zones. A correctly sized pump and pressure tank keep coverage uniform without short-cycling.
  • Foothill and rural residential: Up toward San Antonio Heights, parcels are larger, terrain is steeper, and a single well may serve the house, a barn or shop, and the landscape. Booster or constant-pressure systems help maintain pressure as elevation changes across the property.

Common Well Problems We See in the Upland Area

Since 2020 of work across San Bernardino, Riverside, and San Diego Counties, certain issues come up again and again on foothill wells like those around Upland.

  • Hard-water scale and mineral buildup that shortens pump life and clogs filters, emitters, and fixtures.
  • Iron and manganese staining that leaves rusty or black marks on concrete, sinks, and stucco, often with a metallic taste or musty smell.
  • Sediment and sand in the water after heavy use or as a screen ages, fouling drip irrigation and aerators.
  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tanks that cause the pump to cycle on and off rapidly, a pattern that quickly burns out a pump motor.
  • Declining yield during drought years as the regional water table drops and a well that once kept up begins to run short on hot afternoons.
  • Aging submersible pumps that trip breakers, lose pressure, or simply stop after many years of service.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick checks at home can save you a service visit, or at least help us arrive ready with the right parts. Work through these in order before you pick up the phone.

  1. Check your breaker and any well disconnect. A tripped breaker or a switched-off disconnect at the pump panel is one of the most common causes of sudden "no water." Reset it once; if it trips again immediately, stop and call.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge. If you have a gauge on the tank, note the reading. Zero pressure points one direction; pressure stuck high or bouncing erratically points another.
  3. Listen to the pressure tank and tap it. A tank that sounds completely full and water-logged when tapped near the top often needs air recharge or replacement.
  4. Watch the pump cycle. If the pump switches on and off every few seconds (short-cycling), shut the system down to protect the motor and call us.
  5. Check for obvious leaks. A running toilet, a broken irrigation line, or a leaking hose bib can mimic a well problem by constantly draining pressure.
  6. Note water quality changes. New color, odor, grit, or taste is useful diagnostic information, so write down when it started and whether it is constant or intermittent.

When to Call a Professional

Some situations should go straight to a licensed contractor. Anything involving the submersible pump itself, the wiring in the well, pulling drop pipe, or diagnosing a yield problem requires specialized equipment and experience, plus it is electrical work around water. If you have no water at all, repeated breaker trips, rapid short-cycling, a suspected pump failure, or a sudden change in water quality, it is time to call. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really grow avocados on my Upland well, or should I stick with citrus?

Upland is foothill citrus country, not avocado country. The combination of foothill frost and intense inland summer heat makes avocados difficult here, which is why you see far more lemon and orange trees than groves of Hass. Most of our Upland customers run wells for citrus, ornamental landscaping, and household use. If you do try a few avocado trees, the limiting factor is usually climate and water quality, not well capacity, and we are happy to test your water before you plant.

How deep are wells in the Upland foothills?

It varies a lot with location and elevation. Foothill properties along the San Gabriel front commonly tap the alluvial gravels and deeper groundwater basins beneath the San Bernardino Valley, and depths often run anywhere from about 200 to 600 feet or more. Wells higher up toward San Antonio Heights can be deeper. We can pull your well records or sound the well to confirm your specific depth.

Why does my Upland well water leave rusty or white stains?

Inland Empire groundwater is typically hard and frequently carries iron and manganese. Hard water leaves white scale on fixtures and inside pipes, while iron and manganese cause rusty orange or blackish staining on concrete, stucco, and sinks. A water softener or a dedicated iron/manganese filter, generally 1,500 to 3,500 dollars, solves the problem and protects your irrigation lines too.

My pump keeps turning on and off rapidly. What is wrong?

Rapid short-cycling usually means a waterlogged or failed pressure tank, or sometimes a bad pressure switch. The tank has lost its air cushion, so the pump fires constantly to keep up. This wears out a pump motor fast, so shut the system down and call us. A pressure switch runs 150 to 350 dollars and a tank 600 to 1,500 dollars, far cheaper than a new pump.
