How Your Pressure Tank Keeps Water Flowing

A pressure tank exists so your well pump doesn't have to start every single time you open a faucet. It stores water under pressure using a cushion of compressed air. In the bladder tanks we install on Oak Hills wells, a heavy rubber diaphragm keeps the water separated from that air cushion. Incoming water compresses the air; when you draw water, the air pushes it back out to the house.

The pressure switch controls a cut-in and cut-out point — usually 30/50 or 40/60 PSI — and the pump only runs between those two numbers. The usable water delivered in that window is the drawdown, and it is much smaller than the tank's rated capacity. The air pre-charge, set about 2 PSI below the cut-in pressure, is what makes the drawdown work. Get it right and the pump runs in long, healthy cycles. Lose the air and the pump begins to hammer itself to death.

Why High Desert Wells Are Hard on Tanks

Oak Hills wells are typically deep — often several hundred feet — which means powerful pumps and higher operating pressures that stress every component, including the tank bladder. The region's hard, mineral-rich water leaves scale that accelerates wear, and wide day-to-night temperature swings in the desert can nudge air pre-charge out of spec over time. All of this makes Oak Hills tanks especially prone to the most common failure of all: waterlogging.

Waterlogged Tanks and Torn Bladders

A waterlogged tank has lost its air cushion — either the pre-charge slowly bled away or the bladder tore. Water then fills nearly the whole tank, drawdown collapses to almost nothing, and the pump is forced to short cycle, flipping on and off every few seconds during any water use. Short cycling is the fastest known way to burn out a well pump, because each restart hits the motor with a heavy inrush of current. Catching it early keeps a $600–$1,500 tank job from becoming a $2,500–$5,500 pump replacement.

Signs Your Oak Hills Tank Needs Attention

  • Rapid pump cycling during normal water use
  • Water pressure that surges and then fades in the shower
  • Water spraying from the Schrader air valve — a ruptured bladder
  • No hollow air space when you tap the top of the tank
  • Corrosion, rust streaks, or weeping at seams and fittings
  • Water hammer — pipes banging when taps shut off

Diagnosing the Problem Correctly

We don't guess. On an Oak Hills service call we shut down the pump, open a fixture to bleed the system to zero, and measure the air pre-charge at the Schrader valve with an accurate gauge. Water at the valve confirms a failed bladder and a tank that must be replaced. A low pre-charge on an intact bladder can often be restored with a compressor. We also confirm the pressure switch cut-in and cut-out and inspect its contacts, because a waterlogged tank and a pitted switch frequently fail together. The diagnostic fee is $125 and we credit it toward the repair, so a thorough inspection never costs extra.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe observations help. Watch the pressure gauge during a single fixture use — more than one pump cycle suggests waterlogging. Tap the tank high and low to feel for an air pocket at the top. Never add air with the system pressurized or the pump running. If the pump won't build pressure or you see water at the air valve, that's the moment to call a professional rather than risk a bigger repair.

Sizing a Replacement Tank for Oak Hills Properties

Oak Hills homes on their large desert lots often run irrigation, livestock water, and household demand off the same well, so tank sizing has to account for real peak use. We size to the pump's actual flow rate and simultaneous demand, targeting pump run cycles of one to two minutes. A small 1-2 bathroom home may need only a 20-32 gallon tank; a 3-4 bathroom home typically calls for 44-86 gallons; and ranch-style parcels with heavy irrigation often need 86-120 gallons or a paired-tank arrangement. With deep, high-pressure desert wells, correct sizing and precise pre-charge are what keep the pump alive.

Repair or Replace? Making the Right Call

A new tank isn't always the answer, and out in Oak Hills — where a service call means a drive into the High Desert — it pays to diagnose thoroughly the first time. If your tank has only lost some air pre-charge but the bladder is still sound, re-charging it may restore normal operation for the price of a service visit. Sometimes the trouble is really a tired pressure switch or a fouled valve rather than the tank at all. But when the bladder has torn — the usual cause of waterlogging — the tank cannot be repaired and must be replaced. Given the deep, high-pressure wells and hard water typical of the area, a tank already past ten years that has waterlogged is generally worth replacing rather than repeatedly patching. We quote both options plainly so you can make the call that fits your budget and your well.

Extending Tank Life in the High Desert

Desert conditions are tough on equipment, so preventive care matters here. Once a year, with the pump off and the system bled down, verify the air pre-charge and set it about 2 PSI below cut-in. Track how often the pump cycles during ordinary use — a slow creep upward signals pre-charge loss before it becomes a full waterlog. Because Oak Hills groundwater is mineral-heavy, sediment filtration ahead of the tank helps limit the scale and grit that wear bladders out early. And any time the system is already open for a pump or switch job, it's worth inspecting the tank while you're there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my well pump keep cycling on and off in Oak Hills?

Rapid on/off cycling almost always means a waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air cushion. It can occasionally be a leaking check valve or a bad pressure switch. Because short cycling wears out pumps quickly — especially the deep, high-pressure pumps common in the High Desert — get it diagnosed right away.

How can I tell if my pressure tank bladder is ruptured?

With the pump off and the system bled to zero pressure, depress the Schrader air valve on top of the tank. Water coming out instead of air means the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacement. A total lack of air pressure indicates the same.

What PSI should my Oak Hills pressure tank be pre-charged to?

Set the air pre-charge roughly 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in pressure with the tank empty of water — about 28 PSI for a 30/50 switch, or 38 PSI for a 40/60 switch. On deep desert wells with higher operating pressures, getting this right is critical to drawdown and pump life.

How much does a pressure tank cost to replace in the High Desert?

A bladder pressure tank replacement typically runs $600–$1,500 depending on size and fittings; a pressure switch is $150–$350. We give upfront quotes and credit the $125 diagnostic toward the repair.

Does hard desert water shorten pressure tank life?

It can. Oak Hills' mineral-heavy groundwater and large temperature swings accelerate wear, so tanks here may reach the lower end of the usual 10-to-15-year lifespan. Correct pre-charge, sediment filtration, and periodic inspection help extend it.
