What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Logan Heights Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Well Owners Searching From Logan Heights

Hydrofracturing, also called hydrofracking, injects high-pressure water into a low-yield bedrock well to reopen the natural fractures it draws from. On the granite that underlies inland San Diego County, it restores lost yield in the majority of wells we treat, at a small fraction of the cost of drilling new.

How the Process Restores a Well

A hard-rock well produces from cracks, not from a sandy reservoir. Groundwater travels through fracture seams in the granite and crystalline rock to reach the borehole, and the well only yields as much as those seams deliver. Years of pumping pack the nearest fractures with silt and mineral scale, throttling the flow. Hydrofracturing clears that bottleneck: an inflatable packer isolates a section of the well, and clean water is injected at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI, scouring the clogged fractures and prying open tight ones close by. The borehole reconnects to water it had lost access to. No chemicals are used, and the on-site work is usually finished in four to eight hours.

The Rock Under Your Property Decides Everything

Success hinges on geology. The inland reaches of San Diego County sit on the granitic and metamorphic basement of the Peninsular Ranges, decomposed granite near surface, harder rock below, and wells in that material are the ideal candidates. Wells finished in deep alluvial valley fill, the loose sand and gravel along drainages, are weak candidates, because there are no rock fractures for pressure to open. We review your well log and run a camera as needed to confirm you are in rock before recommending treatment, so we never sell a job that physically cannot work.

Warning Signs of a Fading Well

  • Declining yield compared with prior years.
  • Air or sputtering at fixtures during heavy demand.
  • Slow recovery after the pump shuts off.
  • Pump short-cycling from a borehole that cannot keep up.
  • A new well that never met expectations despite hitting rock.

Realistic Outcomes

In San Diego County's decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in about 70 to 80 percent of treated bedrock wells, with successful jobs commonly raising flow two to five times above baseline. These are honest ranges; underground rock is never perfectly predictable. We protect you with measurement, baselining your flow before treatment and re-testing afterward, so the result is documented rather than promised.

When a New Well Is the Right Answer

We will not run a hydrofracture that cannot help. If the casing has failed, the borehole is collapsing, or the well never reached a producing fracture, deepening or redrilling is the honest path forward, and a shallow alluvial well simply has nothing for the process to open. Telling you that before you spend money is part of the job.

Understanding Recovery Rate, Not Just Gallons Per Minute

When owners describe a struggling well, they usually focus on gallons per minute, but recovery rate often matters more for daily life. A well can show a respectable instantaneous flow yet still run a household short if it draws down quickly and takes hours to rebound. Because hydrofracturing widens the fracture network feeding the borehole, it tends to improve both the peak flow and, just as importantly, how fast the well recovers between demands. For a back-country home running irrigation, laundry, and household use on one well, that faster recovery is frequently what turns an unreliable well into a comfortable one.

The Value of a Camera Inspection First

We rarely recommend hydrofracturing without first looking inside the well. A downhole camera tells us whether the casing is sound, whether the borehole is silting in, and where the productive fracture zones sit, all of which shape the treatment and its odds of success. Skipping that step is how careless operators end up treating the wrong interval or pressurizing a compromised casing. Spending a little time on inspection protects your well and gives the hydrofracture the best chance of a lasting result, which is why it is a standard part of how we work.

Long-Term Well Stewardship in the Back-Country

A private well is a long-term asset, and a single hydrofracture, when the well is a good candidate, can reset its productive life for years. Pairing that with sensible stewardship, not overdrawing during peak summer demand, keeping the pump correctly sized, and inspecting the system periodically, keeps a rural San Diego County property self-reliant. We are happy to advise on that bigger picture, not just the treatment, because a customer whose well keeps working is the best reference we can earn.

Why Seasonal Timing Affects Your Results

Groundwater levels in the inland San Diego County back-country swing with the seasons, dropping through the dry late-summer and early-fall months and rebounding after winter rains. That matters for hydrofracturing in a practical way: a well treated when the water table is seasonally low can look even better once levels recover, while a well that struggles only at the peak of summer demand may benefit most from the improved recovery rate a treatment provides. We take the timing of your symptoms into account when we assess the well, because understanding when and how your well falls short helps us target the treatment and set realistic expectations for the gain you will see year-round.
