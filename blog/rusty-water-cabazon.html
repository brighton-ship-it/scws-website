What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in Riverside County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Cabazon well.

Rusty Well Water in Cabazon: Why It Happens and How to Fix It

If the water coming out of your tap in Cabazon has turned a rusty orange, leaves brown rings in the toilet bowl, or stains your white laundry a dingy yellow, you are dealing with one of the most common complaints among well owners in the San Gorgonio Pass. Cabazon sits in western Riverside County in the wind-swept gap between the San Bernardino Mountains and the San Jacinto range, where groundwater filters down through alluvial fans washed out of granite peaks. That same geology that gives the Pass its abundant wind also delivers iron and manganese into the aquifer, and those minerals are the usual reason a Cabazon well starts pumping discolored water.

The encouraging part is that rusty water is almost always a treatable problem, not a sign your well is finished. With the right diagnosis it can usually be cleared up for a fraction of the cost of a new well. The key is figuring out exactly where the iron is coming from before you spend money on equipment.

How Iron and Rust Get Into Cabazon Well Water

Iron is one of the most abundant elements in the earth's crust, and the decomposed granite and alluvial sediments that make up the San Gorgonio Pass are full of it. As rain and snowmelt off Mount San Jacinto percolate down into the groundwater, slightly acidic water dissolves iron out of the rock and carries it into the saturated zone your pump draws from. While that water is deep underground and starved of oxygen, the iron stays dissolved and invisible. It is only when it reaches the surface, hits the air, and oxidizes that it turns that telltale rusty color, the same chemistry that rusts a nail left out in the rain.

Treatment works by speeding up and controlling that oxidation. An iron filter introduces oxygen or an oxidizing media so the dissolved iron converts to solid particles, then traps those particles in a bed of filter media that backwashes itself clean on a schedule. For lower iron levels, or where hardness is also a problem, a water softener can pull modest amounts of iron out through ion exchange. Where iron bacteria are involved, the well itself has to be disinfected, because no downstream filter can keep up with a living, self-replenishing colony in the casing.

Common Causes We See on Cabazon Properties

Not every rusty-water call has the same root cause. On Cabazon and Morongo-area properties, these are the scenarios our technicians run into most often:

  • Dissolved natural iron. The classic case. Water is clear at the wellhead but turns orange after sitting in a glass or bucket for a few minutes as the iron oxidizes.
  • Iron bacteria. These harmless but messy organisms feed on iron and leave a reddish, slimy sludge in toilet tanks, on screens, and inside pipes. They often produce a swampy or oily smell along with the color.
  • Aging steel casing. Many older Pass-area wells were finished with steel casing that slowly corrodes from the inside, shedding rust flakes into the water column. The discoloration here is often a heavier, sediment-like rust rather than a fine tint.
  • Corroding household plumbing. Galvanized pipe installed decades ago rusts from the inside out. If only the hot water is rusty, suspect the water heater; if it is worst first thing in the morning, suspect the home's pipes rather than the well.
  • A failing water heater. A spent anode rod or a rusting tank can color the hot water all on its own, independent of well quality.

What to Check Before You Call

A few simple observations will help you, and us, zero in on the source:

  • Hot, cold, or both? Rust in only the hot line points to the water heater. Rust in both usually points to the well or the main plumbing.
  • Clear then orange, or orange right away? Water that starts clear and discolors in a glass means dissolved iron oxidizing. Water that is cloudy and orange from the tap suggests rust particles or sediment already in suspension.
  • Any slime or smell? Reddish slime in the toilet tank or a musty odor is a strong sign of iron bacteria.
  • When did it start? A sudden change after a power outage, a dry spell, or heavy pump cycling can mean your pump is drawing from a lower, more mineral-rich part of the aquifer.
  • Run an outside hose. If an outdoor spigot ahead of any treatment runs rusty, the problem is upstream in the well; if it runs clear, the problem is inside the house.

When to Call a Professional

You can swap a sediment cartridge or flush a water heater yourself, but rusty water that persists, comes with slime or odor, or shows up alongside dropping pressure deserves a professional look. A licensed well contractor can pull a water sample, measure iron and manganese in parts per million, check for iron bacteria, and inspect the casing and pump. That diagnosis is what prevents the common and expensive mistake of installing a softener when the real culprit is a corroding casing or a bacterial colony that simply needs to be chlorinated out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rusty water from my Cabazon well safe to drink?

Iron at typical well concentrations is not a health hazard; it is classified as an aesthetic problem. That said, rusty water tastes metallic, stains everything it touches, and can signal other issues, so we recommend testing to confirm what is actually in your water before deciding it is fine.

Why is my water clear at the tap but turns orange in a glass?

That is the signature of dissolved iron. Underground it stays invisible because there is no oxygen; once it hits air it oxidizes and turns rusty. An oxidizing iron filter is the standard fix for this pattern.

Will a water softener remove the rust from my well water?

A softener can handle modest amounts of dissolved iron, but it is not designed for heavy iron loads, rust particles, or iron bacteria. For those, a dedicated iron filter or well disinfection is the right tool. Testing tells us which.

What is the reddish slime in my toilet tank?

That is almost certainly iron bacteria. It is not a health threat but it is persistent, and the fix is shock chlorination of the well rather than a filter, since the colony lives in the casing.

Could the rust be coming from my house and not the well?

Yes. Old galvanized plumbing and aging water heaters both shed rust. If only the hot water is affected, or an outdoor spigot ahead of the house runs clear, the source is likely inside your home, not in the well.
