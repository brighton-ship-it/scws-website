What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Anza Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation method, not a drilling method. The goal is to restore the flow your well had when it was new by clearing and widening the cracks in the surrounding bedrock that groundwater travels through. The process is straightforward and uses nothing but clean water under pressure.

First, we lower an inflatable packer into the well casing and inflate it to seal off a specific zone of the borehole below it. This isolates the section we want to treat so all of the pressure is concentrated where it will do the most good. Then we inject clean water into that isolated zone at roughly 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. That pressure pushes into the existing fractures in the granite, clearing out the silt, mineral scale, and fine sediment that have plugged them over the years, and it extends those fractures so they connect with more water-bearing cracks in the rock.

Because Anza wells are set in hard, fractured granitic bedrock, the network of natural fractures is already there; hydrofracturing simply cleans and opens it back up. There are no chemicals involved at any point, which is the key difference between water-well hydrofracturing and the oil-and-gas fracking people hear about in the news. On a typical Anza well, the on-site high-pressure work takes about 4 to 8 hours, often treating more than one zone in a deeper well.

Signs an Anza Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Wells in the Anza Valley tend to show their age and the limits of the local water table in a few recognizable ways. Watch for these signs:

  • Declining yield over the years — your well used to keep up with the house, the livestock, and the irrigation, and now it cannot.
  • Slow recovery after pumping — the well draws down quickly and takes a long time to refill, so you run out during back-to-back showers or filling troughs.
  • Running low in dry or drought months — at this elevation the water table drops in late summer and through dry winters, and a marginal well goes from "tight" to "empty."
  • A new well that under-produces — you drilled into fractured granite expecting good flow but the yield came in disappointingly low.

All four of these point to the same underlying issue: not enough fracture connectivity feeding the borehole. In the fractured-granite conditions around Anza, Cahuilla, and Aguanga, that is precisely the problem hydrofracturing is designed to fix.

Why Local Geology Matters

The single biggest factor in whether hydrofracturing will help is the type of rock your well is drilled into, and that is where Anza has a real advantage. The Anza Valley sits within the Peninsular Ranges, built from decomposed granite at the surface over fractured granitic bedrock below. Water in this kind of formation does not sit in a big sandy aquifer; it travels through a network of cracks and joints in the rock. When those cracks get clogged or simply do not connect well to your borehole, the well underperforms.

Fractured rock is the ideal candidate for hydrofracturing. The high-pressure water has solid rock to push against, so it cleans and extends real fractures and links your well to more of them. By contrast, wells set in loose sand or gravel are not good candidates, because there are no rigid fractures to open; the pressure just dissipates into the unconsolidated material. Anza's hard, fractured granite is squarely on the favorable side of that line, which is a big part of why the technique works so reliably up here.

Safety First: What to Check Before Hydrofracturing

Before you spend money on hydrofracturing, it is worth confirming that the well itself is the problem and not the equipment attached to it. A surprising number of "my well is going dry" calls in Anza turn out to be something simpler and cheaper to fix. We always rule these out first:

  • The pump — a worn or failing submersible pump produces less water and can mimic a low-yielding well.
  • The pressure tank — a waterlogged or failed bladder tank causes pressure swings and short cycling that feel like a water shortage.
  • The pressure switch — a miscalibrated or sticking switch can shut the system down early or run the pump erratically.
  • Leaks — a leak in the line between the well and the house wastes water and drops your pressure.

The most reliable way to separate a true low-yield well from an equipment problem is a flow test. We measure how much water the well actually produces and how quickly it recovers, which tells us definitively whether hydrofracturing is the right call. Our diagnostic flow test is $125, and we credit it toward the cost of the work if you decide to move forward. That way you are never paying for a treatment your well does not need.
