The Most Common Well Emergencies We See in Bloomington

Bloomington's older estate properties and animal-keeping parcels often run wells and pump systems that have been in service for decades. That longevity is a testament to good equipment, but age, hard local groundwater, and the heat of the Inland Empire summer all take a toll. These are the emergencies our crews respond to most often:

  • Sudden loss of water at every fixture. When nothing comes out of any tap at once, the cause is almost always the well system itself rather than household plumbing.
  • Submersible pump failure or motor burnout. A pump that has run for fifteen or twenty years will eventually wear out its bearings or burn out its motor, especially when it is forced to cycle constantly.
  • Pressure tank failure. A waterlogged or ruptured bladder tank makes the pump turn on and off rapidly, which quickly destroys the motor if it is not corrected.
  • A well that has gone dry or is producing sand. Drawdown during dry years or a dropping water table can leave a pump pulling air, mud, or grit instead of clean water.
  • Electrical and control box failures. Capacitors, relays, and control boxes fail without warning and are a frequent culprit when a pump simply will not start.
  • Burst pipes and leaks. Failed fittings, cracked drop pipe, or a leaking pitless adapter can drain pressure and waste hundreds of gallons.
  • No water after a power outage. When the grid blips, control boxes and pressure switches do not always come back cleanly, and a tripped breaker or fried relay can leave you dry.

What to Check First Before You Call

A few quick, safe checks can sometimes restore water in minutes, and when they do not, the information helps our technician arrive prepared. Please do not open the well cap, touch wiring while standing in water, or attempt to pull a pump yourself.

  1. Check the breaker. Look in your main electrical panel for a tripped breaker labeled for the well or pump. Flip it fully off, then back on. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us, because that points to an electrical fault.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Find the gauge near your pressure tank. A healthy system usually reads between 40 and 60 psi. A reading of zero means the pump is not building pressure.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. Knock on the tank from top to bottom. A hollow sound up high and a solid sound low down is normal; a tank that sounds solid all the way up is waterlogged.
  4. Listen at the pressure switch. The small box on the pipe near the tank should click as the system calls for water. No click can mean a failed switch or no power reaching the pump.
  5. Confirm the grid. Make sure the outage is not affecting the whole property and that any sub-panel or disconnect serving the well is energized.

What to Do While You Wait for Us

Once you have called, a little preparation keeps your household comfortable and protects your equipment. Turn off the well pump breaker if it keeps tripping, so the motor is not damaged by repeated attempts to start. Conserve whatever water remains in your pressure tank and water heater for drinking and essential needs. If you keep horses or livestock, fill troughs and buckets from any neighbor or stored supply you can reach, since animals cannot wait long in the Inland Empire heat. Clear a path to your well head, pressure tank, and electrical panel so our technician can work efficiently the moment we arrive.
