Why Well Casing Matters

Well casing is the pipe lining that protects your well from collapse and prevents contamination from surface water or shallow groundwater. It's a critical component that must last decades. The casing extends from above ground into the aquifer zone, with different materials offering distinct advantages for various conditions.

Steel Casing Characteristics

Steel casing—either galvanized or stainless—provides exceptional strength and durability. It handles deep installations and difficult soil conditions where PVC might crack. Steel resists damage during installation and can last 50+ years. However, standard steel can corrode in acidic water or certain soil conditions. Cost runs $15-$30+ per foot.

PVC Casing Characteristics

PVC (Schedule 40 or 80) casing is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and less expensive—typically $8-$15 per foot. It's ideal for moderate depths (under 400 feet) and non-aggressive soil conditions. PVC won't rust or corrode in acidic water. Installation is easier and faster. However, PVC has depth limitations and can crack under extreme pressure or shifting soils.

Which Material Is Right for Your Well?

Choose steel for deep wells (400+ feet), unstable soils, or where maximum longevity is critical. Choose PVC for moderate-depth wells in stable conditions where corrosion resistance matters. Some wells use both—steel for the upper portion and PVC below. Your well driller should recommend materials based on your specific site conditions and local code requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does steel well casing last?

Quality steel casing typically lasts 50-80+ years in favorable conditions. Galvanized steel may show corrosion after 40-50 years in acidic soils. Stainless steel lasts longest but costs significantly more.

Is PVC casing safe for drinking water?

Yes. NSF-certified PVC casing is approved for potable water wells and doesn't leach harmful chemicals. It's widely used in residential and commercial wells across the country.

Can you replace well casing without drilling a new well?

Replacing casing is possible but often impractical and expensive. A liner can sometimes be installed inside deteriorating casing. In many cases, drilling a new well is more cost-effective than major casing rehabilitation.
