Cloudy or Milky Well Water in Redlands

Noticing cloudy, milky, or white-colored water from your Redlands well? This issue has several possible causes—some harmless, others requiring attention.

Causes of Cloudy Well Water

  • Air Bubbles: Tiny air bubbles (harmless, clears in minutes)
  • Hard Water: High mineral content causing cloudiness
  • Methane Gas: Natural gas dissolved in groundwater
  • Bacteria: Bacterial growth in well or pipes
  • TSS (Total Suspended Solids): Fine particles in water

The Glass Test

Fill a clear glass with water and let it sit:

  • Clears from bottom up: Likely air bubbles (harmless)
  • Clears from top down: Sediment settling (filtration needed)
  • Stays cloudy: Dissolved minerals or bacteria (testing needed)

Treatment Options

  • Aeration System: Removes dissolved gases
  • Sediment Filtration: Removes suspended particles
  • Water Softener: Reduces mineral hardness
  • UV Disinfection: Kills bacteria without chemicals
  • Shock Chlorination: One-time bacteria treatment

When to Be Concerned

While air bubbles are harmless, persistent cloudiness warrants investigation. Bacterial contamination or methane require professional attention for your family's safety.

Cloudy or Milky Well Water in Redlands: A Local Guide

That geology is why so many Redlands wells produce excellent water, and also why cloudy, milky, or white-tinted water shows up from time to time. Once you understand what is happening underground in San Bernardino County, the milky glass at your sink stops being a mystery and becomes a problem you can fix.

Start With the Glass Test

Before you call anyone, pour a glass of cold water from a frequently used tap and set it where you can watch it. Cloudy well water almost always sorts into one of three patterns within a few minutes, and which one you see tells you a great deal about the cause.

  • It clears from the bottom up. If the cloudiness lifts and the water turns clear starting at the bottom of the glass and rising toward the top, you are almost certainly looking at trapped or dissolved air. This is harmless.
  • Particles settle to the bottom. If you see a layer of fine grit, sand, or silt collecting at the bottom while the water above clears, you have sediment or turbidity. This needs filtration.
  • It stays uniformly cloudy. If the glass looks the same after 15 or 20 minutes, with no settling and no clearing, the cloudiness is most likely dissolved minerals or, less commonly, bacteria. This calls for water testing before you assume anything.

That one observation narrows the field. Here is what each cause looks like in a Redlands well.

Trapped and Dissolved Air (Harmless)

The most common reason for milky Redlands water is simply air. Pressurized water holds dissolved air, and when it leaves your faucet and returns to normal pressure, that air comes out of solution as millions of tiny bubbles that make the glass look white. Because the bubbles rise, the water clears from the bottom up. This is harmless and usually appears in cooler months, after pump service, or when a pressure tank is low on air charge. No treatment is needed, though if it is sudden and persistent it can point to a minor air leak worth checking.

Fine Sediment, Silt, and Clay Turbidity

Redlands wells draw from sand-and-gravel alluvium, and fine silt or clay can migrate into the water, especially in older wells or after heavy mountain runoff recharges the Santa Ana River basin. This is turbidity, and in the glass test the particles settle to the bottom. Sediment is more than cosmetic: it wears out pump impellers, clogs aerators and appliances, and shields bacteria from disinfection. It is one of the most common and most fixable cloudy-water problems we see in San Bernardino County.

Methane Gas

Naturally occurring methane dissolved in groundwater can also make water look cloudy or fizzy as it escapes at the tap. Methane is less common in the shallow alluvial aquifers around Redlands than elsewhere, but it does occur. Because it is flammable when it accumulates in an enclosed space, suspected methane should be confirmed by a professional and addressed with a vented aeration system, never ignored.

Precipitating Minerals Like Calcium

Mountain-fed Inland Empire groundwater is naturally hard, carrying dissolved calcium and magnesium. When that water warms or its pressure changes, minerals can precipitate into fine white particles that cloud the water and scale fixtures. If your cloudy water comes mostly from hot taps, or you see white crusting on faucets and glassware, mineral precipitation is the likely culprit. This is a water-chemistry issue, not a well-mechanical one, and it responds well to softening or conditioning.

A Stirred-Up Well: Failing Pump, Low Water Level, or Overpumping

Sometimes cloudy water signals trouble down the well itself. If the pump is set too low, the water level has dropped during a dry stretch, or the household is overpumping faster than the aquifer can recharge, the pump can begin pulling water from near the bottom of the well where sediment collects. A failing or worn pump can also stir up the well column and draw in sand. When cloudy water arrives suddenly alongside sputtering faucets, dropping pressure, or a pump that runs longer than it used to, the well, not the water chemistry, is usually the real story.

What to Check Yourself

You can gather useful clues before a technician arrives. Run the glass test and note the pattern. Check whether the cloudiness affects all taps or just hot water, and whether it is constant or comes and goes. Watch your pressure tank gauge for unusual cycling, and listen for a pump that short-cycles or runs continuously. Note recent changes such as new pump work, falling pressure, or a dry season. None of this requires tools, but it often shortens the diagnosis.

Fixes That Actually Solve the Problem

Sediment and Turbidity Filtration

When the cause is silt, sand, or clay, the answer is staged filtration. We typically start with a spin-down separator to catch the coarse material, then step down through cartridge or Big Blue housings in decreasing micron ratings so each stage removes finer particles without clogging instantly. Properly sized sediment and turbidity filtration generally runs between $300 and $1,500 depending on flow rate, particle load, and how many stages your water needs. Done correctly it protects every downstream appliance in the house.

Well Rehabilitation

If the well itself is producing sediment because the screen or casing has degraded, filtration alone treats the symptom. Well rehabilitation goes after the cause. We can run a downhole camera inspection to see exactly what is happening, then use surging and redevelopment to clean the screen and surrounding gravel pack, and repair casing or screen damage where needed. Well rehabilitation typically falls in the $1,500 to $5,000 range depending on depth and condition, and it can restore both water clarity and well yield.

Pump Depth, Air Leaks, and the Pressure Tank

When the diagnosis points to the equipment, the fix is mechanical: resetting pump depth so it draws from clear water, sealing air leaks that introduce bubbles, or repairing a waterlogged pressure tank that is letting the pump short-cycle. Pump service and related repairs generally run from $800 to $2,500 depending on well depth and the components involved.

Water Testing and Disinfection

When water stays uniformly cloudy, testing comes first. A certified test tells us whether we are dealing with minerals, bacteria, or something else, so we treat the actual problem. Mineral issues respond to softening or conditioning; confirmed bacterial contamination is addressed with shock chlorination and, where appropriate, UV disinfection.

When to Call a Pro

Air bubbles that clear from the bottom up are nothing to worry about. Call a professional, though, if cloudiness persists more than a day or two, if water stays uniformly cloudy, if sediment appears with falling pressure, if there is any odor, or if you suspect methane. Cloudy water that arrives suddenly with other symptoms is the well telling you something. Frequently Asked Questions

Is cloudy well water in Redlands safe to drink?

It depends on the cause. If the water clears from the bottom up within a few minutes, it is trapped air and completely safe. Sediment is usually not a health hazard but should be filtered. Water that stays uniformly cloudy should be tested before you drink it, since both minerals and, rarely, bacteria can cause persistent cloudiness.

Why does my Redlands water look milky in winter but clear in summer?

Cold water holds more dissolved air than warm water, so in cooler months more air comes out of solution at the tap and makes the water look milky. As the water warms it holds less air and the effect fades. This seasonal, harmless pattern is the most common cloudy-water complaint we hear in San Bernardino County.

What does it mean if sediment settles at the bottom of the glass?

Settling particles mean turbidity, fine silt, sand, or clay from the alluvial aquifer. It points either to sediment filtration or, if the well itself is shedding material, to well rehabilitation. A camera inspection tells us which.

How much does it cost to fix cloudy well water?

It varies by cause. Sediment and turbidity filtration runs $300 to $1,500, pump service runs $800 to $2,500, and well rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any repair we perform, so you only pay the diagnostic if you decline the work.

Could cloudy water mean my well pump is failing?

It can. A pump set too low, a dropping water level, or a worn pump can pull sediment from the bottom of the well and cloud the water. If cloudiness shows up suddenly with sputtering taps or falling pressure, the pump or water level is a likely cause and worth inspecting promptly.
