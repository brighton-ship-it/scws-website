The Most Common Well Emergencies in Cedar Glen

Mountain wells fail in fairly predictable ways, and after work since 2020 calls between Crestline, Lake Arrowhead, and Running Springs we have seen all of them. Knowing what tends to break helps you describe the problem accurately when you call, which in turn helps us load the right parts before we leave the shop.

  • Sudden loss of water with no warning. One minute the taps work and the next they sputter and die. This is the most common emergency call we take and it can point to several underlying failures.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout. Submersible pumps sitting hundreds of feet down a Cedar Glen borehole run for years, but motors do eventually burn out, especially after a power surge or a season of short-cycling.
  • Pressure tank failure. A waterlogged or ruptured bladder tank causes the pump to cycle rapidly, which both kills water pressure and shortens the life of the pump.
  • A well that has gone dry or low. After a dry winter the static water level can drop below the pump intake, producing air, sputtering, and muddy water.
  • Electrical or control box failure. The control box and pressure switch are the brains of the system, and frozen, corroded, or storm-damaged components are common up here.
  • Burst pipe or major leak. Freezing nights at 5,400 feet split exposed pipe and crack fittings, draining pressure and flooding pump houses.
  • No water after a power outage. Cedar Glen loses power often in winter storms, and pumps frequently fail to restart cleanly when the grid comes back, tripping breakers or overloads.

What to Check First

Before you assume the worst, there are a handful of safe checks any homeowner can run. Often the fix is simpler and cheaper than a pump pull, and walking through these steps on the phone with us saves everyone time.

  1. Check your breaker. Find the breaker labeled for the well or pump in your main panel. If it has tripped, switch it fully off and then back on once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us; do not keep resetting it.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Locate the gauge on your pressure tank or pump assembly. A healthy system usually reads somewhere between 40 and 60 psi. A reading near zero points to a pump or supply problem; a stuck high reading points to a switch problem.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. Knock on the tank low and high. A hollow sound up top and a solid, dull sound near the bottom is normal. A tank that sounds full and heavy all the way up is likely waterlogged.
  4. Look at the pressure switch. The small box with two metal contacts often shows burned or pitted points. Do not touch the contacts with power on, but note what you see for the technician.
  5. Confirm the grid. After a mountain storm, verify you actually have power to the well circuit and that no neighborhood outage is in progress.

What to Do While You Wait

Once you have called us and help is on the way, a little preparation makes the visit faster. Switch off the well pump breaker if it keeps tripping, so the motor is not straining against a fault. Conserve the water already in your lines and water heater for drinking and essential use, and avoid running appliances that need water. If temperatures are below freezing, open a cabinet door or two to keep the remaining plumbing from freezing solid, and clear a path to your pump house or well head if snow has piled up. Finally, keep your phone handy; our technician will often call as they head up the mountain to confirm access and ask a few diagnostic questions.

Do you work on wells during snow and winter conditions?

Yes. We service the San Bernardino Mountains year-round and are equipped for cold-weather and snow conditions. Clearing a path to your well head or pump house before we arrive helps us work faster.

My well water suddenly turned muddy or sputtery. What does that mean?

Sputtering air and cloudy water often mean the water level has dropped near the pump intake, which can happen after a dry winter, or that the pump is failing. Either way it is worth a prompt diagnostic before the pump runs dry and burns out.

Is the diagnostic fee really credited toward the repair?

Yes. The $125 emergency diagnostic is applied to the cost of the repair when you choose to have us complete the work, so you are not paying twice.

The power just came back after an outage and I still have no water. Why?

Cedar Glen pumps sometimes fail to restart cleanly after an outage, often tripping a breaker or overload. Try resetting the breaker once; if it trips again or water does not return, call us rather than repeatedly resetting it.

Do you replace pressure tanks and control boxes, or only pumps?

We repair and replace the entire system, including pressure tanks, pressure switches, control boxes, wiring, and submersible pumps. Many emergencies turn out to be a tank or control issue rather than the pump itself.
