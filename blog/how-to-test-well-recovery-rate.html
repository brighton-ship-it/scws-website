Understanding Well Recovery Basics

Your well connects to an underground aquifer—water-bearing rock or sand formation that stores and transmits groundwater. Recovery rate describes how quickly water flows from the aquifer into your well. This depends on aquifer characteristics (permeability, saturation), well construction (screen size, placement), and regional groundwater conditions. A well's depth doesn't guarantee good yield—a 400-foot well in tight rock may produce less than a 100-foot well in porous gravel. Recovery rate represents your actual sustainable water supply and is one of the most important numbers for well owners to know. Southern California's diverse geology means well yields vary dramatically even between neighboring properties.

Professional Pump Test Procedure

The most accurate recovery measurement is a professional pump test. The procedure involves pumping your well at a sustained rate while measuring water level drawdown over time, then monitoring how quickly water level rises after pumping stops. SCWS uses calibrated flow meters and electronic water level indicators for precise data. A typical test runs the pump for 4-8 hours at maximum rate, documenting gallons pumped and water level every 15-30 minutes. After pumping stops, recovery is tracked until static level returns. This data reveals your well's maximum sustainable yield, optimal pump sizing, and whether capacity matches your household needs. Professional testing is especially valuable before purchasing property or planning major water use additions.

DIY Recovery Indicators

While precise measurement requires professional equipment, homeowners can gather useful indicators. Monitor your pressure tank cycling during heavy use: if the pump runs continuously or nearly so, you're exceeding recovery rate. Time how long your pump runs versus rests during normal use—healthy systems rest more than they run. Note if morning pressure (after overnight rest) differs significantly from evening pressure (after daily use)—large differences suggest the well draws down during the day. Keep a log of pump run times during different seasons; dramatically longer runs in summer may indicate seasonal yield reduction. These observations, while not precise GPM measurements, reveal whether your well comfortably meets your demands.

Factors Affecting Recovery Rate

Recovery rate is influenced by factors you can and cannot control. Aquifer characteristics are fixed—the geology determines baseline yield. However, well maintenance significantly impacts actual performance. Clogged well screens restrict water entry; periodic cleaning restores flow. Biofouling (bacterial buildup) can reduce yield by 50% or more and is treatable. Pump efficiency matters too—an oversized pump can actually reduce effective yield by drawing down faster than the aquifer supplies, causing air entrainment and cycling problems. Proper pump sizing matches pumping rate to sustainable recovery. Southern California's ongoing aquifer stress from regional development means recovery rates may decline over time in some areas—another reason to establish baseline measurements and monitor regularly.

Solutions for Low Recovery Wells

If testing reveals inadequate recovery for your needs, several solutions exist depending on severity and budget. Storage tank systems—typically 1,000-5,000 gallons—accumulate water continuously then supply on demand, effectively multiplying available flow. Well rehabilitation through mechanical cleaning, chemical treatment, or hydrofracturing sometimes dramatically improves yield, especially in older wells. Deepening the existing well may reach better-producing zones. In some cases, drilling a new well at a different location taps more productive aquifer areas. SCWS provides comprehensive low-yield well assessment, evaluating all options with honest cost-benefit analysis. Sometimes a $1,500 storage system solves the problem; other times, significant investment is the only path to adequate water supply.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is well recovery rate and why should I know mine?

Recovery rate (also called recharge rate or yield) measures how fast your well replenishes water after pumping—expressed in gallons per minute (GPM). This determines your well's sustainable water supply. A well might be 200 feet deep, but if it only recovers at 2 GPM, you have roughly 120 gallons per hour available. Knowing your recovery rate helps you understand actual water availability, plan usage during dry periods, and make informed decisions about household expansion, irrigation systems, or adding a pool.

How do I perform a basic recovery test at home?

For a simple test: Run water at a high rate (multiple faucets) until you notice pressure dropping significantly or the pump cycles rapidly—indicating you've drawn down the well. Note the time. Stop all water use and time how long until pressure returns to normal cut-off point. This gives rough recovery indication. For more accuracy, you need to measure water level drawdown and recovery, which typically requires professional equipment. DIY tests provide general guidance; professional tests yield precise GPM measurements.

What is a good recovery rate for a residential well?

Most residential needs require 3-5 GPM minimum, with 5-10 GPM being comfortable for typical households. At 5 GPM, you get 300 gallons per hour—adequate for normal use including showers, laundry, and dishwashing without stress. Homes with irrigation needs, multiple bathrooms, or high water use may need 10+ GPM. Some Southern California wells produce 20+ GPM, while others struggle at 1-2 GPM. Low-yield wells can work with proper storage systems and water management but require careful planning.

Can my well's recovery rate change over time?

Yes, recovery rates can change due to several factors: seasonal aquifer fluctuations (lower in dry months), long-term aquifer depletion from regional pumping, well screen clogging from minerals or bacteria, and aging pump efficiency. Some changes are natural and cyclical; others indicate problems. If you notice recovery seeming slower—longer pump run times, more frequent cycling, pressure struggling to build—test again and compare to previous measurements. Declining recovery may warrant professional evaluation.

What can I do if my well has poor recovery?

Options for low-yield wells include: installing a storage tank system that accumulates water slowly then delivers on demand, reducing water usage through conservation fixtures and practices, well rehabilitation (cleaning screens, acidizing) which sometimes restores flow, hydrofracking to open new fissures in rock aquifers, or ultimately drilling a new or deeper well. SCWS can assess your specific situation and recommend cost-effective solutions. Sometimes simple well cleaning doubles recovery rate; other times, significant investment is needed.
