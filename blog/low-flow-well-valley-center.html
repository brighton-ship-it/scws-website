Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Flow Well Solutions for Valley Center Properties

When a private well in Valley Center stops keeping up — weak pressure with two taps open, a storage tank that never quite recovers, or a slow decline through the dry years — you are dealing with a low-yield well. It is one of the most common calls we field from this part of San Diego County, and in the great majority of cases it can be fixed without abandoning the well. The first step is understanding why your well runs low, and that always comes back to the ground beneath your feet.

Valley Center spreads across the hills and small valleys of north-central San Diego County, a rural community of groves, ranches, and large-lot homes where private wells are a way of life. The geology is classic Peninsular Ranges: pockets of alluvium in the valleys and extensive fractured granite and decomposed granite across the hills. With significant agricultural water use and that hard-rock terrain, low-yield wells are a regular part of the picture.

Understanding Well Yield and GPM Testing

Well yield is the sustained rate, in gallons per minute (GPM), that your well can deliver without running dry — a different thing from the pressure you feel at the faucet, which a small pump can fake for a few minutes. We measure true yield with a flow test paired with drawdown and recovery monitoring: we pump at a steady rate, watch how far the water level falls, and time how quickly it rebounds once the pump shuts off. For a Valley Center home, 5 to 10 GPM is comfortable; granite hillside wells producing 1 to 5 GPM are common and manageable with storage, while grove parcels require considerably more.

A well that draws down sharply and recovers slowly is limited by the aquifer or the fractures feeding it. One that holds its level but still delivers little is usually choked by a fouled screen or hampered by an undersized or failing pump. Knowing which it is keeps you from paying for the wrong fix.

What Causes Low Flow in Valley Center Wells

The culprits we see most often around Valley Center tie directly to the local geology and climate:

  • Fractured-granite storage limits. Hillside wells rely on bedrock cracks; sparse or disconnected fractures mean low yield at any depth.
  • Agricultural demand from groves and landscaping straining a modest well.
  • Drought and a falling water table reducing regional groundwater levels.
  • Mineral scale and biofouling choking the screen over time.
  • A pump set too high above the current water column or productive fractures.

Proven Solutions for a Low-Yield Valley Center Well

Well rehabilitation and redevelopment. Mechanical surging, brushing, and chemical treatment strip mineral scale and biofouling from the screen and gravel pack, often restoring much of the original yield. This is usually the first and least expensive step.

Lowering the pump. If the water table has fallen or the pump sits above the productive zone, repositioning it deeper in the casing can recover flow immediately, at a fraction of the cost of drilling.

Hydrofracturing. The fractured granite under much of Valley Center is well suited to hydrofracturing. By injecting water at high pressure to reopen and link fractures, we frequently double or triple the yield of a hillside well.

Deepening or drilling a new well. When the existing borehole bottomed out in tight rock, deepening it can reach a better-producing zone. When a well is truly spent, a new well sited with local knowledge is the long-term answer.

Storage tank plus booster or constant-pressure pump. The most practical fix for a modest-but-steady well. A 2,500 to 5,000 gallon tank banks your well's 24-hour output, and a booster or constant-pressure system delivers strong, even household pressure on demand — letting a 1 to 3 GPM well comfortably serve a busy home.

When to Call a Professional

Reach out if your flow has noticeably dropped, you are pulling air or sand, pressure collapses under normal use, or you are about to add bathrooms, livestock, or irrigation the well cannot support. A proper flow and drawdown test removes the guesswork and points to the most economical fix before a marginal well becomes a dry one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What well yield is normal in Valley Center?

For a household, 5 to 10 GPM is comfortable. Granite hillside wells producing 1 to 5 GPM are common and workable with storage, while grove parcels need much higher flow.

Why has my Valley Center well lost flow?

Hillside wells in fractured granite have limited storage, and agricultural demand strains them further. Drought, a dropping water table, mineral scale, or a pump set too high are the usual reasons flow declines.

Does hydrofracturing work in Valley Center?

Yes. The fractured granite here is ideal for hydrofracturing, which reopens and connects fractures and often doubles or triples a hillside well's yield.

Is a storage tank a good fix for my Valley Center well?

For most properties, yes. A 2,500 to 5,000 gallon tank banks a slow well's 24-hour output while a booster or constant-pressure pump delivers strong household pressure on demand.

How deep are wells in Valley Center?

Valley wells often reach water at 150 to 350 feet, while hillside properties may run 400 to 600 feet through decomposed and fractured granite. Productive fractures matter more than total depth.

What does it cost to fix a low-yield Valley Center well?

Rehabilitation typically runs $1,500 to $6,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, and storage-plus-booster systems $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey well ranges from $18,000 to $42,000. The diagnostic is $125, credited toward approved work.
