Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your San Marcos well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well Services for San Marcos Avocado Growers

San Marcos has changed a great deal as the city has grown around Cal State San Marcos and the Highway 78 corridor, but climb into the hills toward Twin Oaks Valley, Coronado Hills, or the ridges above Discovery Lake and you are back in working grove country. These granitic slopes in northern San Diego County have grown avocados for decades, and the properties that still farm them depend on private wells rather than district water. Southern California Well Service has worked this terrain since 2020, and we have learned that a San Marcos ridge well behaves nothing like a flatland ag well — the water lives in fractures in the rock, and tapping it reliably takes local knowledge.

What Avocados Ask of Your Water System

The arithmetic of avocado irrigation is demanding. A mature tree in San Marcos can use 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and a planted acre needs roughly four to six acre-feet a year, including the extra water you must apply to leach salts below the shallow roots. Avocados will not forgive a missed irrigation during fruit set, yet they rot if they sit wet. So the system has to be both steady and precise, and on the sloped ridges typical of San Marcos that precision depends heavily on holding even pressure from the top of the grove to the bottom.

How These Systems Are Put Together

A typical San Marcos grove pumps from the well into storage, then distributes through micro-sprinklers or drip under regulated pressure. The pieces that matter are the submersible pump set down in the fractured rock, an atmospheric storage tank that lets a modest well accumulate volume overnight, a constant-pressure or booster system that holds PSI as zones cycle, sediment filtration, and pressure-regulating valves across the blocks. Because hillside groves here can have substantial elevation change within a single property, we pay particular attention to pressure regulation — it is usually the cheapest way to fix uneven canopy stress that growers assume is a fertilizer or pest problem.

Problems Rooted in Local Geology

San Marcos groves bring us a predictable set of issues. Ridge wells in weathered granite recharge slowly, so during dry years their summer yield drops, sometimes to the point where the grove outruns the well by August. Mineral hardness scales pump components and gradually clogs emitters, starving the trees farthest down the line. Sediment becomes a problem when a dropping water level lets a well draw fines through the screen. And because avocados are extremely sensitive to chloride, elevated salts in the water can cause leaf tip burn that growers chase for months before realizing the irrigation source is the culprit. We also see plenty of waterlogged pressure tanks and worn switches causing pumps to short-cycle and fail early.

Checks You Can Make Yourself

A little observation goes a long way. If the whole grove is stressed evenly, suspect the well or main pump; if only the uphill or far rows wilt, suspect flow and pressure. Watch the gauge through a full irrigation set — a steady decline signals the well cannot meet demand, a rapid bounce signals a tank or switch fault. Inspect a handful of emitters for mineral crust. Confirm whether your storage tank empties by the end of a long set, which tells you whether supply is keeping up. And note any new cloudiness or grit in the water. Jot these observations down; they shorten our diagnosis and your bill.

When the Job Needs a Pro

Routine maintenance — filters, breakers, a quick emitter cleaning — is yours to handle. But pulling a pump, testing water chemistry, diagnosing a yield decline, or designing a system for new plantings is professional work, and on a deep granite well it is not a job for a general handyman. We hold a C-57 well drilling license and bring the right equipment for down-hole work. If your output is falling year over year, your canopy shows salt symptoms, or you are adding acreage, get an assessment before the trees pay the price.

Water Testing for San Marcos Ridge Wells

Ridge wells in San Marcos draw from weathered and fractured granite, and the water chemistry on these slopes deserves real attention. We test for chloride and total dissolved salts first, because avocados are among the most chloride-intolerant crops grown in the county and tip burn is often the only outward sign until damage is well underway. We also evaluate hardness and iron, which together govern how fast your pump scales up and how quickly your drip emitters narrow and clog. On a sloped grove fed by a single well, a small water-quality problem compounds: the salts that stress the trees and the minerals that plug the emitters tend to hit the far, high rows hardest, exactly where coverage is already most marginal.

A water profile turns guesswork into a plan. If chloride is elevated, we look at leaching strategy, blending with another source where one exists, and salt-tolerant rootstock for replants. If hardness or iron is the issue, the answer is the right filtration and a maintenance schedule for the emitters. For growers considering new plantings on a ridge, testing the water before committing to a variety and an irrigation layout is inexpensive insurance.

Why Local Drilling Knowledge Pays Off Here

San Marcos's granite is not uniform. Two wells a few hundred yards apart on the same ridge can produce very differently depending on how many water-bearing fractures each borehole happens to intersect. Crews from outside the region routinely misjudge this, drilling to a standard depth and assuming a standard yield that the rock simply will not deliver. After decades on these slopes, we approach each site on its own terms — evaluating the parcel, the surrounding wells we have serviced, and the grove's actual demand before recommending depth, equipment, or storage. That local judgment is the difference between a well that carries a grove and one that disappoints every August.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my uphill trees always look worse than the rest?

That pattern almost always points to pressure loss across elevation, not a soil or nutrient problem. Without proper pressure regulation, the highest emitters get the least water. Correcting regulation usually evens out the canopy without any change to fertilizer or variety.

How deep do wells go on San Marcos ridges?

Hillside wells in weathered granite are often several hundred feet deep because water is held in rock fractures rather than a shallow aquifer. Depth varies by parcel, so we evaluate each site rather than quoting a standard number.

My well's flow drops every summer. Is it failing?

Not necessarily. Fractured-granite wells naturally yield less as the water table falls in dry months, and recovery often comes with winter rains. A flow test distinguishes a normal seasonal dip from genuine well decline or equipment wear.

Can my existing well handle a few more rows of trees?

We measure real sustained output with a long-duration flow test, then compare it to your peak summer demand including the new trees. That is the only reliable way to know before you plant.

What causes the tip burn I'm seeing on the leaves?

Chloride and salt accumulation, to which avocados are unusually sensitive. We test your water and, if salts are high, recommend leaching adjustments, blending, or salt-tolerant rootstock.

Do you come out the same day in an emergency?

Yes, we offer same-day emergency response and prioritize grove irrigation failures during heat events.
