What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Skyline Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Skyline: Setting Expectations Honestly

Skyline—part of the Skyline-Paradise Hills area in southeastern San Diego—is a long-established urban neighborhood of hillside streets and postwar homes overlooking the southern reaches of the city. Like the rest of San Diego, it is served by City of San Diego municipal water, which the region imports in large part from the Colorado River and Northern California. In a built-out neighborhood like this, private domestic wells are vanishingly rare. So we will be direct: for the typical Skyline household on a city water meter, hydrofracturing is not the service you need, and we would rather say so than quote you for it.

This page is here because Skyline belongs to San Diego County, and across the county's inland and rural communities there are many homes that do run on wells drilled into fractured granite and crystalline rock. If you searched from a Skyline address but the property you are worried about is out in well country—or you simply want to understand the technique—the explanation and the steering advice below will help you put your money where it actually does some good.

On City Water? Here Is Where Your Problem Likely Lives

Most water complaints in a neighborhood like Skyline trace back to the home's own plumbing or to the characteristics of the municipal supply—never to a well. The usual suspects:

  1. Pressure-reducing valve trouble. The PRV where the city line enters the house can drift or fail, producing pressure that is too low or uncomfortably high. Replacing it is a modest plumbing job.
  2. Aging galvanized pipe. Some older Skyline homes still carry mid-century galvanized supply lines that rust internally and steadily strangle flow.
  3. Hillside elevation effects. On the higher lots, top-floor fixtures naturally see lower pressure; a booster pump evens it out.
  4. Scale and sediment at fixtures. Hard imported water leaves deposits in aerators, screens, and valves that mimic a bigger problem.
  5. Taste, hardness, and clarity. These are water-quality issues solved by treatment, not by anything done to a well.

For these, the right spend is on plumbing or treatment. Sediment filtration runs $300 to $900; a softener or iron and manganese system runs $1,500 to $3,500; and a booster or constant-pressure system to steady pressure on a hillside lot runs $2,000 to $4,500. A simple pressure switch, where one is involved, is just $150 to $350.

Why Skyline Almost Never Fits the Well Profile

The reason is history and infrastructure. Skyline and the adjoining Paradise Hills grew rapidly as residential subdivisions during San Diego's mid-twentieth-century expansion, platted into city blocks and connected to municipal water mains from the outset. There was no era of homestead wells feeding these hillside lots the way there was in the county's ranching valleys. The terrain—urban fill and graded slopes over the area's underlying formations—was developed for housing density, not for groundwater extraction, and the imported-water system reached the neighborhood as it was built. As a result, when a Skyline resident lands on a "well service" search, it is almost always a generic match to a pressure or water-quality frustration rather than evidence of an actual well. Knowing that spares you from chasing the wrong solution.

Living With Imported Water in Skyline

San Diego's imported supply is hard, and on long hillside service lines residents sometimes notice scale, spotting, and uneven pressure between the lower and upper floors of a home. These are comfort-and-maintenance issues with well-understood remedies: a whole-house softener to control hardness, a kitchen filter for taste, a sediment pre-filter to protect appliances, and a booster pump to firm up pressure on the highest lots. All of these are installed at or beside the home and have nothing to do with drilling or fracturing. If your frustration is really about water quality or hillside pressure, that is good news—the fixes are comparatively simple and affordable.

What Hydrofracturing Is For

Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation method for wells finished in hard rock. A technician seals a section of the well casing with an inflatable packer and injects clean water at high pressure—typically 1,000 to 3,000 PSI—to reopen and extend the natural fractures in the surrounding bedrock and flush out the silt and mineral scale that clog them over time. Reopened fractures let more groundwater reach the well, restoring or improving yield. The process uses only water, with no chemicals, so it is safe for drinking-water wells. None of that is relevant to a home on a municipal meter, which is why the first question we always ask is whether you have a well at all.

For Inland County Wells: When It Pays Off—and When to Drill Instead

Out in the county's fractured-rock country, hydrofracturing is often the smart first move on a low-yield well because it costs a fraction of a new well. But it is not a cure-all. When a well's casing is failing, when it is finished in loose sand and gravel rather than rock, or when the water table has dropped well below the screen, the honest recommendation may be deepening the existing well or drilling a new one. We test and inspect before recommending, so you are not paying to fracture a well that needs replacing.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Confirm the source. A monthly City of San Diego bill means municipal water and a plumbing—not well—issue.
  • Look for well equipment. A private well means a casing pipe, pressure tank, and control box somewhere on the property.
  • Read the symptom. Constant low pressure everywhere suggests a regulator or pipe problem; pressure that fades only under heavy use on a well suggests yield.
  • Try the free fixes. Clean aerators and showerhead screens before assuming anything expensive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Skyline homes have private wells?

Almost none. Skyline and Paradise Hills are urban San Diego neighborhoods on City of San Diego municipal water. If you receive a city water bill, hydrofracturing does not apply to your home.

Will hydrofracturing improve my Skyline water pressure?

No. On city water, low pressure comes from a pressure-reducing valve, old piping, or your hillside elevation—solved with plumbing or a booster, not well work.

I have a well on a property outside the city—can you help?

Yes. If it is in fractured rock and the casing is sound, hydrofracturing may restore yield. If not, we may recommend deepening or a new well after testing.

When is a new well better than hydrofracturing?

When the casing is failing, the formation is sand and gravel, or the water table has fallen below the screen. We inspect first so you do not pay to fracture a well that needs replacing.

Is the process safe for my drinking water?

Yes. Hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals introduced into the well or aquifer.

What should I do first in Skyline?

Determine whether you are on city water or a well. For city water, we will point you to the right plumbing or treatment fix; for a well, book a $125 diagnostic and flow test.
