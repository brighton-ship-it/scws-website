Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Crest Park well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Crest Park well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Water in Crest Park: What Your Mountain Well Is Telling You

Crest Park is one of those quiet pockets of the San Bernardino Mountains — a small forested community near Lake Arrowhead and Cedar Glen, sitting around 5,400 to 5,900 feet among the pines of San Bernardino County. Out here, away from any municipal water main, homes run on private wells drilled into the same fractured granite that underlies the whole Rim. That geology is part of the charm and part of the challenge, because it is also the reason so many Crest Park wells eventually start delivering water with a rusty, orange-brown cast. If you have noticed the stain creeping across your sink, the metallic edge in your morning coffee, or the brown film building inside the toilet tank, your well is telling you it has iron in it. The question worth answering is which kind, and where it is coming from.

Southern California Well Service has diagnosed and cured this exact issue across the mountain since 2020. We are a licensed C-57 water well contractor, and we approach rusty water the way a good doctor approaches a symptom: figure out the cause before prescribing the cure.

Iron Is the Headline, but the Story Has Chapters

Almost all rusty well water in Crest Park comes down to iron, but iron arrives by more than one route. The first and most common is dissolved iron, leached out of the granitic and metamorphic rock as groundwater works through the fractures. You will recognize it because the water is clear coming out of the tap and only turns orange after it sits and contacts the air. The second is iron bacteria, a living colony that eats iron and leaves rusty slime and an earthy or swampy odor — particularly likely in the many Crest Park homes that are weekend retreats and sit idle for stretches. The third is corrosion of an aging steel casing or galvanized household plumbing, which sheds genuine rust flakes into the supply.

These three look similar in the sink but call for completely different remedies, which is exactly why a guess at the hardware store so often fails Crest Park homeowners.

The Hidden Cost of Leaving It Alone

Rusty water is not just unpleasant to look at. Left untreated, iron quietly damages the things you have already paid for. It scales the inside of water heaters and shortens their life. It clogs aerators, fouls fixtures, and builds up in the small passages of dishwashers and washing machines. It bonds to laundry and grout in stains that grow harder to remove the longer they set. And when iron bacteria is involved, the slime it produces can restrict flow through pump screens and pressure tanks, eventually dragging down your water pressure. Treating the source early is almost always cheaper than replacing appliances and fixtures one by one.

Reading the Signs at Your Own Tap

A short, careful look tells you a lot. Fill a clear glass from a cold tap and set it aside; if it clouds and turns orange over the next hour, dissolved iron is the likely answer. If only the hot side runs rusty, your water heater — not the well — is corroding, and that is a far smaller repair. If the discoloration is dramatically worse after the cabin has been closed up, suspect iron bacteria or pipe corrosion tied to stagnation. And if a rotten-egg or musty smell rides along with the color, that combination deserves a lab test before you draw any conclusions. Bring these observations to your technician and you shorten the path to the right solution.

Matching the Fix to the Cause — With Real Numbers

Once we know what is driving the discoloration, the path is clear. Dissolved iron is handled with an oxidizing or catalytic filtration system; sediment and iron filtration in Crest Park typically runs $300 to $900. When iron travels with the hard, mineral-laden water common to mountain granite, a water softener — usually $1,500 to $3,500 installed — can manage both. Iron bacteria is not a filter problem; it requires shock chlorination and cleaning of the well itself, sometimes with follow-up treatment to keep it from returning. A badly corroded casing may need to be lined or replaced, and old galvanized pipes are best cured by repiping with PEX or copper. Every job begins with a $125 water test and diagnostic that we credit toward the work, so your money goes to fixing the problem, not just naming it.

When to Bring in a Pro

Some rusty-water situations are worth a same-day call. If the discoloration showed up suddenly with a loss of pressure or sputtering air, the issue may be a failing pump or a well drawing down hard, not a benign iron problem. If staining survives a water-heater replacement and a thorough line flush, the source is upstream in the well and needs testing. And anytime odor accompanies the color, have the water analyzed before using it for drinking or cooking.

Crest Park Rusty Water Questions, Answered

Should I stop drinking my water because it is rusty?

Iron at typical well concentrations is an aesthetic issue, not a recognized health hazard, so rusty water alone is usually not dangerous. However, because discoloration can occasionally accompany bacteria, the safest step is a water test to confirm what is actually present before you rely on it for drinking.

Why does my Crest Park water look clear but stain everything orange?

That is dissolved iron at work. It stays invisible in the pressurized well and plumbing, then oxidizes and turns orange once it meets air in your sink, tub, or laundry. It is one of the most common patterns in the mountain's fractured-rock wells.

What causes the rusty slime inside my toilet tank?

That slime is iron bacteria — harmless to health but persistent. It is especially common in part-time mountain homes where water sits unused. Because it is alive, it requires shock chlorination and well cleaning rather than a filter to truly resolve.

Will treating my water protect my appliances?

Yes. Removing iron before it reaches your plumbing protects water heaters, washing machines, dishwashers, and fixtures from staining and buildup, which typically extends their life and saves money over time.

How much should I budget to fix rusty water in Crest Park?

Sediment and iron filtration generally runs $300 to $900, a water softener $1,500 to $3,500, and well rehabilitation for iron bacteria is quoted after inspection. Our $125 test and diagnostic is credited toward whichever solution you choose.

Can a single system handle both iron and hard water?

Often, yes — a properly sized softener or a combination treatment system can address moderate iron and hardness together. The right configuration depends on your iron level and water chemistry, which is what the diagnostic test determines.
