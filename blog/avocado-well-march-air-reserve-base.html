Water in the March Air Reserve Base Area

March Air Reserve Base is a military installation in western Riverside County, hemmed in by Moreno Valley to the north, the City of Riverside to the west, and Perris to the south. The base and most surrounding subdivisions are served by municipal water districts, including Eastern Municipal Water District, which draws on a blend of imported supply and local groundwater from the San Jacinto and Perris basins. So to be straight with you: the immediate base footprint is institutional and municipal, not avocado-grove country.

But step just outside the developed core and the picture changes. The unincorporated pockets and rural-residential parcels around the March area, toward Perris, Mead Valley, and the Moreno Valley fringes, still run on private wells. These properties use groundwater for household supply, livestock and horses, fire protection, and irrigation of pasture, citrus, ornamentals, and small orchards. Avocados are not a major crop on this side of the county, where summer heat, salinity, and water cost favor hardier plantings, but the well systems that serve these parcels are exactly what we maintain every day.

The groundwater here is known for variable quality. Parts of the Perris and San Jacinto basins carry elevated salinity and minerals, which is why the regional water district even operates desalter facilities. For a private well owner, that same chemistry shows up as hard water, staining, and treatment needs.

How a Private Well System Works

If your property near March relies on a well, your system likely includes these core parts:

  • Well casing and borehole reaching the producing zone in the local basin sediments.
  • A submersible pump that lifts water to the surface and is the most common major repair.
  • A pressure tank that stores pressurized water so the pump does not short-cycle.
  • A pressure switch and controls that start and stop the pump.
  • Storage tanks on many parcels, buffering modest yields and supporting irrigation and fire flow.
  • Treatment equipment for sediment, hardness, iron, or manganese, which matters here given the basin's salinity and mineral load.

Irrigation parcels usually add a distribution network on top of this: valves, filters, and either drip lines or sprinklers. When pressure drops or a zone fails, the root cause can be in the well, the pump, the tank, or the irrigation side, so accurate diagnosis matters.

One detail that trips up out-of-area contractors is how shallow and variable the producing zones can be across western Riverside County. A well that performed well for years can lose yield as the regional water table shifts with drought and pumping demand. That is why we look at the whole system, including recent rainfall and basin conditions, before recommending a fix. Replacing a perfectly good pump will not help if the real issue is a dropping water level.

Common Local Well & Irrigation Scenarios

Across western Riverside County's rural-residential belt near March, these are the situations we see most:

  • Hard water and scale. The Perris and San Jacinto basins run mineral-rich and salty in spots. Scale shortens pump and appliance life and clogs irrigation emitters. A softener or targeted treatment is often the long-term fix.
  • Salinity stress on plantings. Salty groundwater is hard on sensitive plants. Owners manage it with leaching irrigation, blending, and salt-tolerant plant choices rather than fighting the chemistry.
  • Pump and pressure-tank failures. Heat, hard cycling, and mineral load wear out pumps, switches, and tanks. Many no-water calls trace to a failed switch or a waterlogged tank, not the pump.
  • Low summer yield. During hot, dry stretches, demand spikes and water tables dip. Storage tanks and constant-pressure systems even out supply for irrigation.
  • Aging infrastructure. Older wells around the base may need rehabilitation, a new pump, or proper decommissioning if they are being retired.
  • Power and control faults. Surges and failing pressure switches or control boxes are a frequent and inexpensive culprit behind what looks like a dead pump.

Because so many of these symptoms overlap, the most expensive mistake we see is owners replacing major components on a guess. A short diagnostic visit almost always pays for itself by pointing to the actual failed part instead of the most obvious one.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Check the pump breaker. A tripped breaker is the leading cause of sudden no-water. Reset once; if it trips again, call.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Zero suggests the pump is not running; rapid on-off cycling points to a waterlogged tank.
  3. Test the pressure tank. Hollow on top, solid on the bottom is healthy. Solid all the way up means a failed bladder.
  4. Hunt for leaks and open valves. A stuck irrigation valve or broken line can drain pressure and overwork the pump.
  5. Note quality changes. Cloudiness, sand, air, or new staining helps us pinpoint the issue.

Avoid repeatedly cycling a pump that will not build pressure, and never try to pull a submersible pump yourself; the weight and depth make it a job for proper equipment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there private wells near March Air Reserve Base?

The base itself and most surrounding subdivisions use municipal water, but the rural-residential parcels and unincorporated pockets nearby, toward Perris and Mead Valley, commonly rely on private wells for household supply, livestock, and irrigation. Those well systems are what we service.

Are avocados grown around March Air Reserve Base?

Avocados are not a significant crop in this part of western Riverside County. Summer heat, groundwater salinity, and water cost favor hardier plantings such as citrus, pasture, and ornamentals. Local wells mainly support household use, livestock, and general irrigation rather than avocado groves.

Why is my well water so hard in the March and Perris area?

The Perris and San Jacinto groundwater basins carry elevated minerals and salinity in places, which is why the regional water district even runs desalter facilities. For private wells this shows up as hard water, scale, and staining, usually addressed with a softener or targeted treatment.

What should I check when my well loses pressure?

Start with the pump breaker, then read the pressure gauge and listen for rapid pump cycling, which suggests a waterlogged pressure tank. Check for leaks or stuck-open irrigation valves. If pressure still will not build, call a professional rather than cycling the pump repeatedly.

Can you help with salty groundwater for irrigation?

Yes. We test your water and recommend practical strategies, including treatment, blending with a lower-salinity source where available, leaching irrigation to flush salts, and salt-tolerant plant selection, so your irrigation works with the local chemistry instead of against it.
