Well Pump Won't Start in Alpine?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Alpine homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

The Chain of Parts That Has to Work

A well pump never runs on its own — it waits for a signal. Understanding that chain is the fastest way to find a fault. Power comes off a dedicated 240-volt breaker in your panel and feeds a pressure switch near the tank. That switch senses tank pressure and closes when pressure falls to the cut-in setting, then opens at cut-out. Its closing is what wakes the pump.

The signal then reaches the pump. On most Alpine wells that is a deep submersible; three-wire units use an above-ground control box with a start capacitor and relay, while two-wire units carry that gear in the motor. The motor spins an impeller stack that lifts water hundreds of feet up the drop pipe into a pressure tank. The tank's air cushion stores pressurized water so the pump isn't slamming on and off with every faucet.

Break any link and the result at your tap is identical: nothing. So the work is figuring out which link.

Power and the breaker

A double-pole 240-volt breaker feeds the well. If it has tripped, reset it a single time. Holding is good news; an instant re-trip is a warning that there is a fault downstream — stop there.

The pressure switch and its sensing tube

Burned contacts and a sediment-clogged sensing tube are two of the most frequent finds on Alpine wells, where the granite groundwater carries enough mineral to gum up that small port over time. Either fault keeps the pump from ever getting its "run" command.

The control box and capacitor

If the pump hums but won't turn, the start capacitor in a three-wire control box is the prime suspect. Heat ages capacitors, and a wellhead box baking in the East County sun gives out sooner than one in the shade. Replacing it is far cheaper than pulling a pump.

The pressure tank

When a tank loses its air charge it becomes waterlogged, and the pump short-cycles — rapid on/off that grinds down the switch and motor. A tank that feels solid and heavy throughout has usually lost its bladder.

Motor, wiring, and splices

Long drop cables in deep Alpine wells are vulnerable at the splice and at the wellhead, where rodents and weather take a toll. A corroded splice or nicked wire can read as a dead pump. We confirm a true motor burnout with insulation and resistance tests, never by guesswork.

The well itself

In dry years the static level in a fractured-granite Alpine well can sink, and a low-water cutoff will idle the pump to save the motor. That is a feature, not a failure — but it presents as a pump that won't start.

Failure Patterns Common to Alpine

Out here, weather and the grid drive a lot of our calls. A backcountry power surge during a storm or wind event trips breakers and pits switch contacts across whole neighborhoods at once. South-facing wellheads cook their control-box capacitors during the first heat waves of summer. And older homesteads that have run the same well for two decades are prime candidates for a waterlogged tank or a tired motor. Recognizing the pattern often points us to the fix before we open a single panel.

Checks You Can Safely Make

  1. Confirm the well breaker is on; reset once if tripped, and walk away if it trips again.
  2. Read the pressure gauge — a flat zero with the pump idle means the system has drained.
  3. Open a tap and listen for the switch to click as pressure drops.
  4. Tap the pressure tank top to bottom, listening for the hollow-then-solid transition that means a healthy air charge.
  5. Eyeball the wellhead wiring for chewed insulation or burn marks — look only, never touch.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Alpine well pump was working last night and is dead this morning — what changed?

Overnight failures almost always trace back to electrical events rather than a worn-out pump. A late-night power flicker or surge in the backcountry grid east of El Cajon can trip the well breaker or weld the pressure-switch contacts shut. We also see overnight failures when a marginal start capacitor finally gives out after a hot day. Check the breaker first, then have the switch and control box tested.

Should I keep flipping the breaker until the pump catches?

No. One reset is fine. If the breaker trips again, leave it off. A repeated trip means there is a ground fault or a locked motor pulling far too much current, and forcing it can ignite wiring or destroy a motor that might otherwise have been salvageable. Call us instead of cycling the breaker.

How can I tell if it's the pressure switch and not the pump itself?

Open a faucet to drop system pressure and listen at the switch by the tank. A healthy switch clicks as it tries to call for the pump. Silence usually means the switch, its sensing tube, or its contacts have failed. If it clicks but the pump still does nothing, the trouble is more likely in the control box, wiring, or motor.

How deep are Alpine wells, and does that affect repair cost?

Wells in the Alpine and Viejas area are commonly drilled 250 to 500 feet into fractured granite, with submersible pumps set well below the static water level. Greater depth means a longer, heavier pull and more drop pipe and wire to inspect, so a true pump replacement on a deep Alpine well sits toward the upper end of the $2,500–$5,500 range.

Could a recent dry spell be why my pump won't run?

Yes. In drought years the water table under Alpine's granite ridges can drop, and many pumps have low-water cutoffs that shut the motor off before it runs dry. The pump looks dead but is actually protecting itself. We can measure the static and pumping water levels to tell whether the well, not the pump, is the real issue.
