What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Silver Lakes Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Near Silver Lakes: What Works in the High Desert

Silver Lakes is the lakeside community at Helendale, an unincorporated stretch of the Victor Valley in San Bernardino County's Mojave Desert, sitting around 2,460 feet of elevation roughly midway between Victorville and Barstow. The community was built in the early 1970s around two man-made lakes—North Lake and South Lake—and the water that fills them, like the water that serves the homes, is pumped from the ground. This is classic high-desert hydrogeology: there is little dependable surface water, so groundwater from the Upper Mojave River Basin, fed by the underground flow of the Mojave River, does the heavy lifting for domestic, agricultural, and community supply.

Here is the honest part. Most of Silver Lakes proper is served by a community water system rather than individual private wells, and the aquifer here is a deep alluvial basin—layered sand, gravel, and silt washed down over geologic time—not the fractured hard rock that hydrofracturing is designed to treat. That distinction is everything. Hydrofracturing reopens fractures in bedrock; it does almost nothing useful in loose alluvial sand and gravel, because there are no rock fractures to reopen. So for the Helendale and Silver Lakes area, hydrofracturing is usually a weak fit. The realistic paths to more water out here are deepening an existing well or drilling a new one to follow a declining water table—and we will steer you toward whichever genuinely solves the problem.

Why Desert Alluvium Changes the Plan

In a fractured-rock setting, low yield often means clogged fractures, and high-pressure injection can clear them. In a desert alluvial basin like the Alto subarea of the Upper Mojave River Basin, low yield usually means something else: the regional water table has dropped, the screened section of the well is now above or near the falling water level, or the well screen and gravel pack have fouled with fine sediment and mineral encrustation. Pumping high-pressure water into sand and gravel does not create durable new pathways; the formation simply collapses back. That is why, for most Helendale wells, the productive options are mechanical and depth-based rather than fracture-based.

This is not bad news—it just means matching the remedy to the geology. A well that has lost yield in alluvium can often be restored by rehabilitation methods suited to sand-and-gravel formations, by lowering or replacing the pump, by deepening the borehole, or, when the well has truly reached the end of its life, by drilling a new well to a depth that keeps pace with the basin's water level.

The Bigger Picture: A Basin Under Pressure

Silver Lakes sits within the broader Mojave groundwater system, where demand has long outpaced natural recharge. Decades of pumping for communities, agriculture, and landscaping across the Victor Valley have stressed the basin, and water levels in many areas have trended downward over the long term. For an individual well owner that translates into a moving target: the depth that delivered abundant water when a home was built in the 1970s or 1980s may no longer reach a dependable supply today. Understanding that your well is competing with a slowly declining table is the key to choosing the right fix, because no amount of pressure injection can manufacture water that the formation no longer holds at your screen depth. The durable answer is to follow the water—by lowering the pump, deepening the borehole, or drilling deeper—rather than to fracture sand that cannot hold an open fracture.

Common High-Desert Well Scenarios

  • Falling water table. Long-term groundwater decline in the Victor Valley means the level a well was drilled to decades ago may no longer be reliable, especially in dry years.
  • Pump set too high. As the water level drops, a pump that was fine for years starts breaking suction, short-cycling, or pumping air.
  • Screen and gravel-pack fouling. Fine desert sediment and hard-water scale clog the intake, choking flow even when water is present.
  • Sand pumping. A failing screen lets sand into the system, wearing out pumps and clogging fixtures—a sign the well needs rehabilitation or replacement.
  • Mineral-heavy water. Mojave groundwater can carry hardness, iron, and other minerals that call for treatment regardless of yield.

How Hydrofracturing Works—and Where It Does Not

For completeness: hydrofracturing isolates a zone of the well with an inflatable packer and injects clean water at 1,000 to 3,000 PSI to reopen and extend natural fractures in surrounding bedrock, flushing out the silt and scale that block them. It uses no chemicals and is safe for drinking-water wells. It is an excellent tool where wells are finished in granite or other hard rock, such as the mountain margins of the desert. But in the deep sand-and-gravel basin under Silver Lakes and Helendale, there is no fracture network to treat, so we will rarely recommend it here. Being straight about that is part of doing the job right.

Deepening and New Wells: The Practical High-Desert Options

When a Helendale well can no longer keep up, two paths usually make sense. Deepening extends an existing borehole so the pump can be set below the current water level—a good option when the casing is sound and the geology cooperates. When a well is old, the casing is corroded, or deepening is not feasible, a new well drilled to an appropriate depth is the reliable long-term answer. A new turnkey well is a larger investment, but in a declining basin it often costs less over time than repeatedly chasing a falling table with partial fixes. We will lay out the trade-offs honestly so you can decide with full information.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Know your source. Are you on the Silver Lakes community water system, or do you have a private well? Community-system issues go to the utility; private-well issues come to us.
  • Watch the pattern. Yield that fades during heavy use or worsens each dry season points to water-level decline rather than a quick fix.
  • Listen to the pump. Rapid cycling, air spitting, or sand in the water signals a pump-depth or screen problem.
  • Test the water. Hardness, iron, and sediment are common in Mojave groundwater and are treatable independently of yield.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing increase my Silver Lakes well's output?

Usually not. The aquifer here is deep alluvial sand and gravel, not fractured rock, and hydrofracturing only works on rock fractures. For most Helendale wells, deepening, pump work, or a new well is the effective path.

Why is my desert well producing less water?

Most often the regional water table has dropped, leaving your pump set too high, or the screen has fouled with fine sediment. Both are addressed by pump and well work rather than fracturing.

Should I deepen my well or drill a new one?

It depends on the casing condition, current depth, and how far the water level has fallen. We run a diagnostic and flow test first, then recommend the option that gives reliable water for the lowest sensible cost.

Is my water from the Mojave River?

Indirectly, yes. Although the Mojave River is dry at the surface most of the year, it flows underground and recharges the basin aquifer that supplies the Helendale area.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No—only clean water under pressure. It is safe for drinking-water wells, but it is simply the wrong tool for a sand-and-gravel basin like this one.

What is the first step for a Silver Lakes homeowner?

Confirm whether you are on the community water system or a private well. For a private well losing yield, schedule a $125 diagnostic so we can measure your water level and recommend deepening, pump work, or replacement.
