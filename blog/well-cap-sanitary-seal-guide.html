What is a Sanitary Well Seal?

A sanitary well seal is a specialized cap designed to create a watertight barrier at the top of your well casing. Unlike a simple cover, a true sanitary seal:

  • Overlaps the casing – Extends down over the outside of the casing
  • Uses rubber gaskets – Creates watertight seal against the casing
  • Has screened vents – Allows pressure equalization while blocking insects
  • Secures with bolts – Can't be easily removed or blown off
  • Accommodates wiring – Has sealed conduit for pump electrical connections

California Well Standards (Bulletin 74-81 and 74-90) require all private wells to have approved sanitary seals.

Types of Well Caps

Sanitary Well Seal (Recommended)

The standard for drinking water wells. Features:

  • Cast aluminum or heavy-duty plastic construction
  • Rubber compression gasket seals against casing
  • Screened vent to prevent vacuum/pressure buildup
  • Sealed electrical conduit
  • Tamper-resistant bolt pattern

Cost: $40–$100
Best for: All drinking water wells

Vermin-Proof Well Cap

Basic protection that keeps animals out but may not be fully watertight:

  • Overlapping design prevents animal entry
  • May have screened openings
  • Less expensive than full sanitary seals

Cost: $25–$50
Best for: Irrigation wells, non-potable uses

Pitless Adapter

For wells with below-ground water line connections:

  • Water line exits casing below frost line
  • Top seal includes sanitary cap
  • Allows pump removal without disturbing discharge line

Cost: $100–$200 (adapter only)
Best for: Cold climates, finished landscapes

Well House/Enclosure

A structure built over the wellhead:

  • Additional protection from weather and tampering
  • Houses pressure tank and treatment equipment
  • Still requires proper cap underneath

Why Proper Sealing Matters

Contamination Sources

An improper or damaged well cap allows entry of:

  • Surface water – Carries pesticides, fertilizers, bacteria, and sediment
  • Insects – Can introduce bacteria; some (like earwigs) fall in and die, contaminating water
  • Rodents – Mice and rats can enter surprisingly small gaps, drown, and contaminate the well
  • Debris – Leaves, dirt, and organic matter promote bacterial growth
  • Floodwater – Most serious contamination risk; can introduce E. coli, coliform, and sewage

Health Consequences

Contaminated well water can cause:

  • Gastrointestinal illness (E. coli, coliform bacteria)
  • Hepatitis A (from sewage contamination)
  • Giardia and Cryptosporidium infections
  • Nitrate poisoning (especially dangerous for infants)

Many contamination events are traced directly to improper well caps.

Inspecting Your Well Cap

Annual Inspection Checklist

Check your well cap at least yearly:

  1. Visual condition: Look for cracks, breaks, rust, or deterioration
  2. Secure attachment: All bolts present and tight?
  3. Gasket condition: Rubber seal intact, pliable, not cracked?
  4. Vent screen: In place and clear of debris?
  5. Electrical conduit: Sealed, no gaps or cracks?
  6. Casing condition: Casing extends 8-12" above ground level?
  7. Grading: Ground slopes away from wellhead?
  8. Clearance: No standing water, debris, or contaminants nearby?

After Storms or Flooding

Immediately inspect after:

  • Any flooding in the well area
  • Major rain events
  • Nearby construction or earthwork
  • Vehicle or equipment impact near well

If floodwater reached your wellhead, assume contamination and have water tested before drinking.

Common Well Cap Problems

Missing or Loose Cap

Signs: Cap not present, sitting loose, or can be easily removed

Risks: Direct contamination pathway—anything can fall in

Fix: Install proper sanitary seal immediately. Test water before resuming use.

Cracked or Deteriorated Gasket

Signs: Rubber seal is hard, cracked, compressed flat, or missing pieces

Risks: Water and insects can enter through gaps

Fix: Replace gasket (often included with new cap) or entire cap assembly.

Corroded or Damaged Cap

Signs: Rust, holes, cracks in cap material

Risks: Structural failure, contamination entry

Fix: Replace entire cap. Consider upgrading from metal to heavy-duty plastic.

Missing Vent Screen

Signs: Open hole where screen should be, torn or missing mesh

Risks: Insects (especially bees, wasps) enter well

Fix: Replace screen or cap. Use fine mesh (16 mesh or finer).

Casing Too Low

Signs: Wellhead at or below ground level, soil against cap

Risks: Surface water pools around and enters well

Fix: Excavate around casing, extend casing if possible, or create concrete collar to raise grade.

Installation Requirements

California Well Standards

San Diego County follows California well standards requiring:

  • Casing height: Minimum 8 inches above finished grade (12" recommended in flood-prone areas)
  • Sanitary seal: Watertight, vermin-proof cap
  • Vent: Screened with 24-mesh or finer screen
  • Electrical: Weatherproof conduit properly sealed
  • Grading: Ground slopes away from wellhead (minimum 6 feet)
  • Concrete collar: 4-inch thick, 4-foot diameter minimum around casing (new wells)

DIY vs Professional Installation

DIY-appropriate:

  • Replacing an existing cap of same size/type
  • Tightening loose bolts
  • Replacing vent screen

Call a professional for:

  • First-time sanitary seal installation
  • Casing extension needed
  • Electrical conduit work
  • After-flood remediation
  • Unknown cap size or type

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a sanitary well seal?

A sanitary well seal is a watertight cap or cover that prevents insects, rodents, surface water, and contaminants from entering the well. California requires all private wells to have a proper sanitary seal meeting state standards.

How often should well caps be inspected?

Inspect your well cap at least annually, plus after any major storms, flooding, or nearby construction. Look for cracks, gaps, missing bolts, damaged gaskets, or signs of tampering. Replace damaged caps immediately.

How much does a well cap replacement cost?

A quality sanitary well cap costs $30-$100 for materials. Professional installation runs $100-$300 including the cap. Given the critical role caps play in preventing contamination, this is one of the most cost-effective well maintenance investments.

My well cap has a hole in it. Is that OK?

Only if it's a properly screened vent. All well caps need vents to prevent vacuum/pressure buildup, but vents must be screened with fine mesh (16-24 mesh) to block insects. Unscreened holes are a contamination pathway.

How high should my well casing stick up above ground?

California standards require minimum 8 inches above finished grade. In flood-prone areas, 12 inches or more is recommended. Higher is always better for contamination prevention.
