Neighbor Well Disputes in California: Your Rights and What To Do

Understanding California Groundwater Rights

Before you can understand well disputes, you need to understand how California law treats groundwater. It's not as simple as "I own the land, I own the water."

The Correlative Rights Doctrine

California follows the correlative rights doctrine for groundwater. In plain English, this means:

  • All landowners above an aquifer have equal rights to reasonable use of the groundwater
  • No one landowner can take more than their fair share, especially in times of shortage
  • "Reasonable use" is tied to the overlying land—you generally can't pump water to sell elsewhere
  • During shortages, everyone must reduce proportionally

This is different from surface water (rivers, streams), which follows the older "first in time, first in right" appropriative rights system. For groundwater, being first doesn't give you priority.

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA)

California's 2014 SGMA law created a framework for managing groundwater basins. Key points:

  • Critically overdrafted basins must have sustainability plans by 2040
  • Local agencies (GSAs) can regulate pumping, require meters, and allocate water
  • Some basins may eventually limit how much each property can pump
  • Not all basins are regulated yet—many rural areas remain unmanaged

In San Diego and Riverside Counties, most groundwater basins are considered "low" or "medium" priority and aren't yet heavily regulated. But this is changing, especially in areas with documented overdraft.

Common Types of Well Disputes

1. Well Interference (Drawdown)

The most common dispute occurs when pumping from one well lowers the water level in a nearby well. Signs of interference include:

  • Reduced water pressure that coincides with neighbor's heavy usage
  • Well suddenly "running dry" after years of good production
  • Pump cycling on and off more frequently
  • Air sputtering from faucets
  • Correlation between neighbor's irrigation schedule and your water issues

📊 How Interference Works

When a well pumps, it creates a "cone of depression" in the water table around it. If your neighbor's cone extends to your well, your water level drops. The effect depends on pumping rate, aquifer characteristics, and distance between wells.

2. Property Line Setback Violations

Many counties require wells to be set back a certain distance from property lines. If your neighbor drills too close, you may have grounds for a complaint—even if the well hasn't affected your water yet.

3. Contamination Concerns

If a neighbor's well, septic system, or land use is contaminating groundwater that reaches your well, this is a serious issue that may involve environmental health departments and different legal remedies.

4. Shared Well Disagreements

Many older properties share wells through easements or informal agreements. Disputes arise over:

  • Who pays for repairs and maintenance
  • How much each party can use
  • Access to the well for service
  • What happens when the well fails

Well Setback Requirements by County

California doesn't have a statewide setback requirement from property lines—it's left to county ordinances. Here's what applies in Southern California:

County Setback from Property Line Notes
San Diego 50 feet May be reduced with neighbor's consent
Riverside 50-100 feet Varies by zone and well type
Orange 50 feet Check with OC Environmental Health
Los Angeles 50 feet typical Varies by jurisdiction
Imperial Varies Agricultural exemptions common

Important: Setbacks are also required from septic systems (typically 100 feet), streams, and other potential contamination sources. Your county's environmental health department is the authority on specific requirements.

What To Do If You Suspect Interference

Step 1: Document Everything

Before taking any action, start keeping records:

  • Water level measurements: Hire a well professional to measure static water level
  • Pump performance data: Record gallons per minute and pump cycling patterns
  • Timeline: Note when problems started and any correlation with neighbor activities
  • Photos and videos: Document air in lines, low pressure, etc.
  • Historical records: Find your well completion report and any past testing

Step 2: Get a Professional Assessment

Have a licensed well contractor or hydrogeologist evaluate your well. They can:

  • Measure current water level vs. historical levels
  • Test pump performance and well yield
  • Assess whether interference is likely or if other causes exist
  • Review geology and aquifer characteristics
  • Provide expert documentation if legal action becomes necessary

💡 Consider Other Causes First

Well interference is real, but so are other explanations: pump failure, clogged screens, aquifer decline from regional drought, or natural seasonal fluctuations. A professional assessment helps identify the true cause before you blame your neighbor.

Step 3: Talk to Your Neighbor (Really)

Before assuming the worst, have a calm, non-confrontational conversation:

  • They may not know their pumping is affecting you
  • Ask about their usage patterns—are they running irrigation heavily?
  • Share your documentation without being accusatory
  • Explore whether they'd consider modifying their pumping schedule
  • Some neighbors will voluntarily limit usage or contribute to solutions

Many disputes can be resolved neighbor-to-neighbor without lawyers or courts. A shared problem sometimes leads to shared solutions—like drilling a deeper well that both properties can use.

Step 4: Contact County Agencies

If informal resolution fails:

  • Environmental Health: Report setback or permit violations
  • Planning Department: Check if neighbor's water use matches their land use permits
  • Groundwater Sustainability Agency: If your basin is managed under SGMA

Step 5: Consider Legal Action (Last Resort)

Legal action over well interference is possible but difficult in California:

  • You must prove the neighbor is using water unreasonably, not just using a lot
  • Expert witnesses (hydrogeologists) are expensive but usually necessary
  • Lawsuits can cost $50,000-$200,000+ with uncertain outcomes
  • Courts may allocate water between parties rather than stop one entirely
  • The relationship with your neighbor will likely be destroyed

Consult a California water rights attorney before pursuing litigation. Many offer free initial consultations to assess your case.

Practical Solutions That Actually Work

Regardless of who's "right" legally, you need water now. Here are practical solutions:

Deepen Your Existing Well

If the water level has dropped, drilling deeper is often the most cost-effective solution:

  • Typically costs $3,000-$10,000 depending on depth needed
  • Faster than drilling new well
  • May access a deeper, more stable aquifer
  • Requires pump replacement at greater depth

Drill a New, Deeper Well

Sometimes a new well in a different location or to a different aquifer is the best answer:

  • Costs $20,000-$50,000+ for complete installation
  • Can access water your neighbor's shallow well can't reach
  • May provide better, more reliable supply long-term
  • Gives you water independence

Install Storage

If your well produces slowly but reliably, storage tanks let you accumulate water:

  • 1,500-5,000 gallon tanks: $3,000-$10,000 installed
  • Pump slowly fills tank 24/7
  • Booster pump provides household pressure from tank
  • Smooths out supply/demand mismatch

Reduce Your Water Use

While not ideal, conservation measures can help stretch limited supply:

  • Low-flow fixtures and appliances
  • Drought-tolerant landscaping
  • Drip irrigation instead of sprinklers
  • Rainwater harvesting for irrigation

Preventing Future Disputes

If You're Drilling a New Well

Be a good neighbor and protect yourself:

  • Exceed minimum setback requirements if possible
  • Inform nearby well owners before drilling
  • Consider baseline water level testing of nearby wells
  • Size your pump appropriately—don't over-pump
  • Document everything for future reference

If You're Buying Property with a Well

Do your due diligence before purchase:

  • Get the well completion report and production history
  • Have the well professionally inspected and tested
  • Research nearby wells and water usage
  • Check for shared well agreements in title documents
  • Understand the groundwater basin status under SGMA

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my neighbor's well affect my well in California?

Yes, if both wells draw from the same aquifer, heavy pumping from one well can lower water levels and affect production in nearby wells. This is called well interference and is increasingly common in drought-prone areas of California.

What is the legal setback for wells from property lines in California?

California has no statewide well setback from property lines—it varies by county. San Diego County requires 50 feet from property lines, Riverside County requires 50-100 feet depending on the zone, and some rural areas have no requirements. Check with your county's environmental health department.

Can I sue my neighbor if their well dries up mine?

Lawsuits for well interference are possible but difficult in California. Under the correlative rights doctrine, all overlying landowners have equal rights to reasonable use of groundwater. You'd need to prove your neighbor is using water unreasonably or wastefully. Legal action is expensive and outcomes uncertain.

What should I do if I think my neighbor's well is affecting mine?

First, document everything—water levels, pump cycles, dates of reduced production. Have a professional test your well's performance. Try talking to your neighbor calmly before assuming the worst. Consider practical solutions like deepening your well before pursuing legal action.

Who regulates groundwater disputes in California?

There's no single regulator. County environmental health departments handle well permits and setbacks. Groundwater Sustainability Agencies manage basins under SGMA. For actual disputes, you may need to go to civil court. The State Water Resources Control Board has limited groundwater jurisdiction.

The Bottom Line

Well disputes with neighbors are frustrating, but they're rarely solved quickly or cheaply through legal action. The most practical approach is usually:

  1. Document the problem professionally
  2. Attempt good-faith communication with your neighbor
  3. Explore practical solutions (deepening, storage, new well)
  4. Involve agencies if regulations were violated
  5. Consider legal action only as a last resort

Remember: you need water more than you need to win. Sometimes the fastest path to reliable water is technical, not legal. A deeper well or better storage system will restore your water supply faster than any lawsuit.
