Planning an Irrigation Well System?

Expert well drilling, pump sizing, and irrigation system design for Southern California properties.

📞 (760) 440-8520 Get Free Irrigation Well Estimate →

22 min read

Municipal water for irrigation is expensive—and in many Southern California rural areas, it's simply not available. Whether you're maintaining a 2-acre estate in Fallbrook, managing vineyard irrigation near Temecula, or keeping your Valley Center avocado grove alive, well water irrigation isn't just cost-effective—it's often your only viable option. But irrigation wells are fundamentally different from domestic water wells. You need higher flow rates (15-40+ GPM vs. 5-10 GPM for a house), different pump configurations optimized for volume over pressure, appropriate filtration to prevent iron staining on your walkways and walls, understanding of water quality impacts on plant health (salinity, pH, boron), pressure requirements that vary by irrigation method, and often storage tank systems to bridge the gap between well production and peak demand. Get the sizing wrong and your sprinklers sputter while your lawn browns. Ignore water quality and watch iron oxide stain your $50,000 hardscape permanently. This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know about using well water for landscape irrigation in Southern California—from calculating exact GPM requirements for your property size, to choosing between drip and sprinkler systems, to understanding why your dedicated irrigation well might cost $15,000-$30,000 but pay for itself in 5-7 years.

💧 Irrigation Reality: A typical 1-acre lawn requires 27,000 gallons per week during peak summer (about 25 GPM continuous for 3 hours daily). That's $150-$300/month in municipal water costs in many SoCal areas—$1,800-$3,600 per year. An irrigation well can pay for itself faster than you think.

Irrigation Wells vs Domestic Wells: Key Differences

Many homeowners wonder if they can simply use their existing domestic well for landscape irrigation. The answer depends on your well's capacity and your irrigation needs, but understanding the fundamental differences between irrigation and domestic wells is critical.

Flow Rate Requirements: Volume Over Pressure

Domestic wells are designed to provide adequate pressure (40-70 PSI) for household fixtures like showers, washing machines, and dishwashers. Flow rate is secondary—most homes use only 5-10 GPM even during peak morning hours.

Irrigation wells prioritize high flow rates (15-50+ GPM) to run multiple sprinkler zones or supply large drip systems. Pressure is less critical—40-60 PSI is sufficient for most irrigation, and localized booster pumps can increase pressure if needed.

Typical Well Specifications Comparison

  • Domestic Well: 6-inch diameter, 5-10 GPM flow rate, pumps to 40-70 PSI, 30-50 gallon pressure tank, total system cost $8,000-$18,000
  • Irrigation Well: 8-10 inch diameter, 15-50 GPM flow rate, pumps to 40-60 PSI, often no pressure tank (storage tank or direct), total system cost $12,000-$35,000

Water Quality Standards

Domestic wells must meet EPA and state drinking water standards—bacteria-free, low nitrates, acceptable arsenic levels, etc. This requires regular testing and often water treatment systems (softeners, iron filters, UV disinfection).

Irrigation wells face no drinking water requirements. The water quality concerns are entirely different:

  • Salinity and TDS (affects plant health, not human health)
  • pH and nutrient availability (impacts fertilizer effectiveness)
  • Iron content (cosmetic staining, not toxicity)
  • Hardness (clogs drip emitters, irrelevant for drinking)
  • Boron levels (toxic to plants, safe for humans in small amounts)
  • Sodium absorption ratio (soil structure, not health)

This means irrigation wells are often simpler and cheaper to treat than domestic wells—you're solving mechanical and aesthetic problems, not health risks.

Larger diameter wells support higher flow rates because they allow larger pumps and more surface area for water entry from the aquifer. This is why irrigation well drilling costs more—you're drilling a bigger hole through the same depth of rock and soil.

Permitting and Regulatory Differences

In California, domestic wells and irrigation wells have different permit requirements:

Domestic Wells:

  • County environmental health permit (construction)
  • Water quality testing for contaminants
  • Setback requirements from septic systems (100+ feet)
  • Well completion report required
  • Generally exempt from extraction permits for household use + incidental landscape watering (up to 0.5 acres)

Irrigation Wells:

  • County environmental health permit (construction)
  • May require groundwater extraction permit from water district or GSA (Groundwater Sustainability Agency)
  • SGMA compliance in many Southern California basins
  • Possible pumping fees or allocations in managed basins
  • Agricultural exemptions may apply for farming operations
  • Backflow prevention device required if connecting to any municipal supply
  • Annual reporting requirements in some jurisdictions

⚠️ SGMA Impact on New Irrigation Wells

The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) now regulates many California groundwater basins. In some areas, new irrigation wells require allocation from the local GSA, pumping fees apply, or new wells are restricted entirely. Check with your local water district BEFORE planning an irrigation well—rules vary significantly by basin and can affect project feasibility.

Can You Use a Domestic Well for Irrigation?

Yes—with limitations. The key question is: Does your well produce enough GPM for your irrigation needs?

Domestic Well Irrigation Feasibility Guide

✅ Domestic Well Can Handle:

  • Drip irrigation for gardens, shrub beds, and small lawn areas (typically 2-8 GPM)
  • Single-zone sprinkler systems with careful head selection (5-10 GPM per zone)
  • Small properties under 0.25 acres with drought-tolerant landscaping
  • Irrigation with storage tank buffer (pump fills tank slowly, irrigation draws from tank at higher flow)
  • Time-staggered zone watering (one zone at a time with delays between)

❌ Domestic Well Will Struggle With:

  • Multi-zone sprinkler systems running simultaneously
  • Large lawn areas (over 5,000 sq ft) with conventional spray heads
  • Properties over 0.5 acres with significant landscaping
  • Golf-course-quality turf requiring frequent, heavy watering
  • Commercial landscaping with high water demands
  • Simultaneous household and irrigation use during peak times

For detailed guidance on measuring your well's flow rate, see our complete guide to GPM and well flow rates.

If your existing domestic well is marginal for irrigation, consider a storage tank system (covered later) before drilling a new well. This allows a low-GPM well to supply high-flow irrigation by accumulating water slowly and delivering it quickly through a booster pump.

GPM Requirements by Property Size

Calculating irrigation GPM needs is more complex than estimating household water use. You must account for property size, landscape type, irrigation method, climate zone, and whether zones run simultaneously or sequentially.

Basic GPM Calculation Methods

There are two primary approaches to calculating irrigation GPM needs:

Method 1: Property Size and Irrigation Type (Quick Estimate)

This method provides ballpark estimates based on total irrigated area and system type:

  • Drip irrigation: 0.5-1.0 GPM per 1,000 sq ft of irrigated area
  • Low-volume spray heads: 1.5-3.0 GPM per 1,000 sq ft
  • Standard spray heads: 3-5 GPM per 1,000 sq ft
  • Rotor heads (large areas): 2-4 GPM per 1,000 sq ft (more efficient than spray heads for coverage)
  • Impact sprinklers (agricultural): 5-15 GPM per head depending on size and throw distance

Example: 10,000 sq ft lawn with rotor heads = 20-40 GPM if watered all at once, or 10-20 GPM with two-zone sequential watering.

Method 2: Sprinkler Head Count (Accurate Method)

For existing or planned systems with known head types:

  1. Identify all sprinkler heads in the largest zone (or all zones if running simultaneously)
  2. Determine GPM per head from manufacturer specifications:
    • Fixed spray heads: 0.75-2.5 GPM each (depending on nozzle)
    • Rotary nozzles: 0.5-1.0 GPM each (very efficient)
    • Small rotor heads (pop-up): 2-4 GPM each
    • Large rotor heads: 4-8 GPM each
    • Impact sprinklers: 5-15+ GPM each
  3. Sum all heads in the zone
  4. Add 10-15% safety margin for system losses and future expansion

Example: Zone with 12 rotor heads at 3 GPM each = 36 GPM + 15% = 41.4 GPM minimum well capacity needed for that zone.

💡 Pro Tip: Zone Your Irrigation to Match Well Capacity

Don't let your desired irrigation system dictate your well size—let your well capacity guide your system design. A 15 GPM well can irrigate a 1-acre property if you divide it into 4-6 zones that run sequentially. Smart zoning reduces required well capacity by 50-75% compared to running all zones simultaneously. This is especially important if you're working with an existing domestic well.

Factors That Increase GPM Requirements

The base calculations above can change significantly based on these factors:

  • High water-demand plants: Cool-season lawns (fescue, ryegrass), tropical plants, and annual flower beds need 30-50% more water than drought-tolerant landscaping
  • Sandy or fast-draining soil: Requires more frequent irrigation cycles, effectively increasing GPM needs by 20-30%
  • Steep slopes: Water runoff requires slower application rates or multiple cycles, increasing total water volume needed
  • Full sun exposure: Properties with little shade need 20-30% more water than partially shaded areas
  • Wind exposure: Coastal or hilltop properties lose significant water to evaporation and drift—add 15-25% to calculations
  • Hot microclimates: South-facing slopes, near buildings, or paved areas generate heat stress requiring additional water
  • Simultaneous household use: If irrigating while household is active (morning showers, etc.), add household GPM to irrigation GPM

Factors That Reduce GPM Requirements

  • Drought-tolerant landscaping: Native California plants, succulents, and Mediterranean species reduce water needs by 40-60%
  • Mulch and soil amendments: Organic mulch reduces evaporation by 25-35%, lowering irrigation frequency
  • Drip irrigation: Reduces water use 30-50% compared to spray systems through precise delivery and reduced evaporation
  • Clay or moisture-retentive soil: Holds water longer, reducing irrigation frequency
  • Partial shade: Trees and structures reduce evapotranspiration by 20-40%
  • Smart controllers with weather adjustment: Optimize watering based on actual plant needs, typically saving 15-30% over fixed timers
  • Storage tank buffering: Doesn't reduce total gallons needed but allows lower-GPM wells to meet peak demands

Drip vs Sprinkler Systems for Well Water

Choosing between drip and sprinkler irrigation significantly impacts your well system requirements. Each method has distinct advantages and challenges when paired with well water sources.

Drip Irrigation: Low-Flow, High-Efficiency

Drip irrigation delivers water slowly and directly to plant root zones through a network of tubing and emitters. It's the most water-efficient irrigation method, making it ideal for wells with limited capacity.

Advantages of Drip Irrigation with Well Water

  • Low GPM requirements: Most residential drip systems need only 2-15 GPM total—compatible with small domestic wells
  • High water efficiency: 85-95% efficient vs. 65-75% for sprinklers (reduced evaporation and runoff)
  • Low pressure requirements: Operates at 15-30 PSI, reducing pump energy costs
  • Weed suppression: Only irrigated areas get water—weeds in between stay dry
  • No overspray: Eliminates water waste on hardscapes, buildings, and sidewalks
  • Reduced disease pressure: Keeps foliage dry, preventing fungal issues
  • Works with low-yield wells: Can operate on wells producing as little as 3-5 GPM

Challenges of Drip Irrigation with Well Water

  • Emitter clogging: Requires excellent filtration (120-200 mesh) to prevent clogging from sediment, iron, calcium, and bacteria
  • Iron and hardness issues: Well water with high iron or hardness rapidly clogs emitters—filtration is essential
  • Not ideal for lawns: Drip works well for gardens, shrubs, and trees but is impractical for turf areas (though subsurface drip systems exist for specialty applications)
  • Maintenance intensity: Requires regular flushing, filter cleaning, and emitter inspection
  • Rodent and pest damage: Animals chew through exposed tubing—requires burial or protection in some areas
  • Initial setup complexity: Designing proper zones, emitter spacing, and pressure regulation takes expertise
  • Hidden leaks: Buried leaks can go unnoticed for weeks, wasting water and drowning plants

Sprinkler Systems: High-Flow, Versatile Coverage

Sprinkler irrigation applies water overhead through spray heads, rotors, or impact sprinklers. It's the traditional choice for lawns and large landscape areas.

Advantages of Sprinkler Systems with Well Water

  • Excellent for turf: Uniform water distribution across lawns—the standard for grass irrigation
  • Flexible coverage: Easy to adjust heads for different areas and plant types
  • Less sensitive to water quality: Tolerates iron, hardness, and sediment better than drip systems (but can cause staining)
  • Simpler filtration: Typically needs only 50-100 mesh screen filters rather than fine micro-filtration
  • Visual leak detection: Easy to spot broken heads, leaks, or misdirected spray
  • Cooling effect: Overhead watering cools plants and can reduce heat stress
  • Easier troubleshooting: Problems are visible and accessible—no digging to find issues

Challenges of Sprinkler Systems with Well Water

  • High GPM requirements: Large zones can require 20-60+ GPM—often exceeds domestic well capacity
  • Higher pressure needed: Spray heads need 30-40 PSI, rotors need 40-55 PSI for proper operation
  • Water waste: 25-35% water loss to evaporation, wind drift, and overspray onto impervious surfaces
  • Iron staining: High-iron well water causes permanent orange/brown stains on concrete, stucco, and fences—a major aesthetic problem
  • Mineral deposits: Hard water leaves white scale on foliage, especially with overhead watering
  • Disease promotion: Wets foliage, increasing fungal disease risk in humid climates (less of an issue in dry SoCal)
  • Wind sensitivity: Ineffective in windy conditions common on coastal and hilltop properties
  • Runoff on slopes: Water runs off faster than soil can absorb, wasting water and causing erosion

Hybrid Systems: Best of Both Worlds

Many well-irrigated properties use hybrid systems combining both methods:

  • Sprinklers for lawns, drip for garden beds, shrubs, and trees—optimizes water delivery for each plant type
  • Drip for front yard landscaping, sprinklers for backyard lawn—balances aesthetics with recreation areas
  • Rotor heads for large turf areas, drip for perimeter shrubs and slopes—reduces overall GPM requirements
  • Sequential zoning: Run high-GPM sprinkler zones first, then low-GPM drip zones—matches well recovery time

Hybrid systems let you maximize your well's capacity while tailoring irrigation to specific landscape needs. However, they require careful system design to balance pressure and flow between different emitter types.

💡 Recommendation for Well-Irrigated Properties

If your well produces under 15 GPM, design around drip irrigation with strategic hand-watering or hose-end sprinklers for small lawn areas. If your well produces 15-25 GPM, use a hybrid system with carefully zoned sprinklers and drip. If your well produces 25+ GPM, sprinkler systems are practical for most of your property. Always match your irrigation design to your well's proven capacity—not your ideal landscape wish list.

Pump Sizing for Irrigation Wells

Irrigation pumps differ from domestic well pumps in key ways. While household pumps prioritize pressure to run fixtures (showers, dishwashers), irrigation pumps emphasize high flow rates to deliver large volumes of water efficiently.

Key Pump Selection Criteria for Irrigation

When sizing a pump for irrigation, you need to calculate three critical values:

  1. Required Flow Rate (GPM)
    This is the sum of all sprinkler heads in your largest zone, plus 10-15% safety margin. For drip systems, total all emitter flow rates. Example: 40 GPM for large zone + 15% = 46 GPM minimum pump capacity.
  2. Required Pressure (PSI)
    This depends on irrigation type:
    • Drip irrigation: 15-30 PSI at emitters
    • Spray heads: 30-40 PSI at heads
    • Rotor heads: 40-55 PSI at heads
    • Impact sprinklers: 40-80 PSI depending on model
    Add 5-10 PSI for pressure losses through filters, valves, and backflow preventers.
  3. Total Dynamic Head (TDH)
    This is the total resistance the pump must overcome, measured in feet of head:
    • Static water level: Depth from ground surface to water level when pump is off (e.g., 150 feet)
    • Drawdown: How much water level drops when pumping (e.g., 20 feet)
    • Elevation gain: Height from wellhead to highest sprinkler (e.g., 30 feet on slope)
    • Pressure requirement: Convert PSI to feet (PSI × 2.31 = feet; 50 PSI = 115.5 feet)
    • Friction losses: Resistance in pipes, filters, valves (typically 10-30 feet depending on system)
    TDH = Static Level + Drawdown + Elevation + Pressure (in feet) + Friction Losses
    Example TDH: 150 + 20 + 30 + 115.5 + 20 = 335.5 feet

With these values, you select a pump that delivers your required GPM at your calculated TDH. Pump manufacturers provide pump curves showing GPM vs. head performance—your operating point must fall within the efficient range of the curve.

Common Irrigation Pump Configurations

1. Direct Submersible Pump System

Configuration: Single submersible pump in well delivers water directly to irrigation system

  • Best for: Wells producing 15+ GPM, properties under 2 acres, systems with pressure regulation at valves
  • Pros: Simple, cost-effective, no storage tank needed, reliable
  • Cons: Pump cycles on/off with irrigation (wear), requires well capacity to match peak demand
  • Typical cost: $2,500-$6,000 for pump, motor, controls, installation

2. Constant Pressure VFD System

Configuration: Submersible pump with variable frequency drive (VFD) controller adjusts pump speed to maintain constant pressure

  • Best for: Medium to large properties (2-5+ acres), systems with varying zone demands, professional installations
  • Pros: Perfect pressure control, energy efficient, gentle pump operation, no pressure tank cycling
  • Cons: Higher upfront cost, requires electronic controls, more complex troubleshooting
  • Typical cost: $4,000-$9,000 for pump, VFD, controls, installation

3. Storage Tank + Booster Pump System

Configuration: Well pump fills storage tank slowly (may run 24/7 if needed), separate booster pump delivers high-flow irrigation from tank

  • Best for: Low-yield wells (under 15 GPM), high peak demands (30-60 GPM zones), properties needing more flow than well can sustain
  • Pros: Allows low-GPM wells to support high-flow irrigation, reduces pump cycling, provides emergency water reserve
  • Cons: Higher cost, requires space for tank, more complex system, booster pump maintenance
  • Typical cost: $5,000-$12,000 for tank, booster pump, controls, plumbing, installation

This is the solution for many rural SoCal properties where wells produce 8-12 GPM but irrigation needs 20-30 GPM during peak watering. Covered in detail later in this guide.

4. Two-Wire Submersible System

Configuration: Specialized submersible pump uses two-wire technology for simplified installation in remote locations

  • Best for: Deep wells (500+ feet), remote well locations far from power source, difficult electrical installations
  • Pros: Reduced wire gauge requirements, easier installation, lower electrical costs for deep wells
  • Cons: Higher pump cost, specialized components, fewer pump options
  • Typical cost: $4,000-$10,000 depending on depth and capacity

⚠️ Don't Oversize Your Pump

Bigger isn't always better. An oversized pump for your well's capacity will draw down the water level excessively, causing the pump to cycle or run dry. It also wastes energy and increases wear. Always match pump capacity to your well's sustainable yield—not your wishful thinking about how much water you want.

Filtration Systems and Iron Staining Prevention

Well water almost always contains some level of sediment, minerals, and organic matter. For irrigation, filtration serves two primary purposes: protecting your system from clogs and damage, and preventing unsightly staining on hardscapes and structures.

Why Filtration Matters for Irrigation

Unlike treated municipal water, well water is raw groundwater with naturally occurring particulates and dissolved minerals:

  • Sand and sediment: Wears out pump seals, clogs emitters and nozzles, accumulates in valve boxes
  • Iron bacteria and biofilm: Forms slimy deposits that clog drip emitters within days
  • Dissolved iron and manganese: Oxidizes when exposed to air, creating rust-colored stains on everything water touches
  • Calcium and magnesium (hardness): Precipitates as white scale in drip lines and on foliage, clogging emitters
  • Organic matter: Algae, plant material, and bacteria create slime and odors in irrigation lines

The level of filtration you need depends on your irrigation system type and water quality.

Filtration Requirements by Irrigation Type

Drip Irrigation Filtration (Most Critical)

Drip emitters have tiny orifices (0.5-2mm) that clog easily. 120-200 mesh filtration is essential (120 mesh = 125 microns, 200 mesh = 74 microns).

Minimum Filtration Setup:

  • Primary filter: Centrifugal sand separator or media filter (removes sand, silt, heavy particles) - $150-$600
  • Secondary filter: 120-150 mesh screen or disc filter (removes fine particles) - $100-$400
  • Pressure regulation: To prevent emitter blowout - $50-$150
  • Flush valves: For periodic system flushing - $30-$100 per valve

For High-Iron Water (>0.3 ppm), Add:

  • Iron filter: Oxidation filter, greensand filter, or chemical injection system - $800-$3,000
  • Without iron removal, emitters clog within weeks and require constant maintenance

Total drip filtration system cost: $500-$4,000 depending on water quality and system size

Sprinkler Irrigation Filtration (Less Critical but Still Important)

Sprinkler nozzles are larger (2-6mm openings) and more forgiving. 50-100 mesh filtration is typically adequate (50 mesh = 297 microns, 100 mesh = 149 microns).

Minimum Filtration Setup:

  • Y-strainer or T-filter: 50-80 mesh screen (removes coarse debris) - $50-$200
  • Optional centrifugal separator: For wells with persistent sand issues - $150-$500

For High-Iron Water (>0.3 ppm):

  • Iron filtration is recommended to prevent staining—sprinklers will function without it, but your concrete, stucco, and fences will develop permanent orange/brown discoloration
  • Iron filters for sprinkler systems: $1,200-$3,000 installed
  • Alternative: Live with staining and clean periodically with oxalic acid cleaners, or design irrigation to minimize spray on hardscapes

Total sprinkler filtration system cost: $200-$3,500 depending on whether iron treatment is included

Understanding Iron in Well Water

Iron is the single most common water quality complaint for well-irrigated properties. Southern California groundwater often contains 0.5-5.0 ppm iron, well above the aesthetic threshold of 0.3 ppm.

How Iron Causes Staining

Iron dissolves in oxygen-free groundwater as ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), which is clear and invisible. When well water is pumped to the surface and exposed to air, ferrous iron oxidizes to ferric iron (Fe³⁺), which is the familiar rust-orange precipitate.

In sprinkler irrigation, tiny droplets of water hit surfaces and evaporate, leaving behind concentrated iron deposits. Over time, these accumulate as permanent stains on:

  • Concrete driveways, walkways, and patios
  • Stucco and painted walls
  • Fences, gates, and outdoor furniture
  • Pool decks and coping
  • White or light-colored foliage
  • Windows and glass doors

The staining is cumulative—even water with just 0.5 ppm iron will cause noticeable stains within months of regular irrigation exposure.

Iron Filtration Technologies

1. Oxidation Filters (Air Injection)

  • How it works: Injects air into water, oxidizing iron/manganese, then filters out precipitate through media bed
  • Best for: Moderate iron/manganese (0.3-10 ppm), residential to small commercial systems
  • Pros: No chemicals, automated backwash, handles both iron and manganese, 15-20 year lifespan
  • Cons: Requires electricity, backwash water disposal, $1,500-$3,000 installed
  • Maintenance: Annual media inspection, periodic media replacement ($200-$500 every 5-10 years)

2. Greensand Filters (Manganese Greensand)

  • How it works: Catalytic media (manganese dioxide-coated sand) oxidizes and filters iron/manganese, regenerated with potassium permanganate
  • Best for: High iron (3-15 ppm), iron bacteria issues, low pH water
  • Pros: Effective for difficult water, also removes hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg smell), handles iron bacteria
  • Cons: Requires chemical regeneration (potassium permanganate), more maintenance, $1,800-$3,500 installed
  • Maintenance: Monthly chemical addition, quarterly filter inspection, media replacement every 5-10 years

3. Chemical Injection Systems (Chlorine/Hydrogen Peroxide)

  • How it works: Injects oxidizing chemical (chlorine, hydrogen peroxide, or potassium permanganate) into water stream, oxidizes iron instantly, filters precipitate
  • Best for: Very high iron (>10 ppm), iron bacteria, hydrogen sulfide, commercial systems
  • Pros: Handles extreme iron levels, kills iron bacteria, highly effective
  • Cons: Requires chemical storage, ongoing chemical costs, more complex, $2,000-$5,000+ installed
  • Maintenance: Chemical refills ($20-$100/month depending on water use), pump service, filter backwash, annual system service

4. Centrifugal Separators + Sediment Filters (Partial Solution)

  • How it works: Spins water to separate heavy particles (oxidized iron, sand), followed by cartridge or screen filter
  • Best for: Removing already-oxidized iron (rusty water from older wells), sand, sediment
  • Pros: No chemicals, simple, affordable ($200-$800), no backwash
  • Cons: Does NOT remove dissolved ferrous iron—only helps if iron has already oxidized before entering filter
  • Maintenance: Filter cartridge replacement every 3-12 months ($15-$80 per cartridge)

For most Southern California irrigation applications with moderate iron (0.5-3 ppm), an oxidation filter provides the best balance of effectiveness, cost, and maintenance. For severe iron or iron bacteria problems, chemical injection may be necessary.

💡 Cost-Benefit Analysis: Iron Filtration vs. Living With Stains

Iron filtration for irrigation costs $1,500-$3,500 upfront. Removing established iron stains professionally costs $500-$2,000 per occurrence. Replacing stained concrete or stucco costs $10,000-$50,000+. If your water has >0.5 ppm iron and you're irrigating near hardscapes or light-colored surfaces, filtration pays for itself quickly. If your landscaping is isolated from structures, you may choose to skip filtration and accept cosmetic impacts on foliage.

Water Quality Requirements for Plants

Unlike drinking water standards focused on human health, irrigation water quality standards emphasize plant health, soil structure, and long-term agricultural productivity. Most well water is safe for plants, but certain parameters can cause problems ranging from reduced growth to complete crop failure.

Key Water Quality Parameters Affecting Plants

Parameter Excellent Good/Usable Marginal Problematic Plant Effects
Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) < 450 ppm 450-1,000 ppm 1,000-1,500 ppm > 1,500 ppm Salt accumulation in soil, reduced water uptake, leaf burn, stunted growth
Electrical Conductivity (EC) < 0.7 dS/m 0.7-1.5 dS/m 1.5-2.5 dS/m > 2.5 dS/m Osmotic stress, salt toxicity, root damage
pH 6.5-7.5 6.0-8.0 5.5-6.0 or 8.0-8.5 < 5.5 or > 8.5 Nutrient availability issues, aluminum/manganese toxicity (low pH), iron/phosphorus deficiency (high pH)
Sodium (Na⁺) < 70 ppm 70-200 ppm 200-500 ppm > 500 ppm Soil structure degradation, reduced infiltration, sodium toxicity in sensitive plants
SAR (Sodium Absorption Ratio) < 3 3-6 6-9 > 9 Soil dispersion, surface crusting, poor drainage
Chloride (Cl⁻) < 140 ppm 140-350 ppm 350-700 ppm > 700 ppm Leaf tip burn, leaf drop, stunted growth in sensitive species
Boron (B) < 0.5 ppm 0.5-1.0 ppm 1.0-2.0 ppm > 2.0 ppm Leaf yellowing and necrosis, severe growth reduction, plant death at high levels
Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) < 90 ppm 90-200 ppm 200-500 ppm > 500 ppm Raises soil pH over time, causes iron chlorosis (yellowing), clogs emitters
Nitrate-N (NO₃⁻-N) 0-10 ppm 10-30 ppm 30-50 ppm > 50 ppm Excessive vegetative growth, delayed maturity, reduced fruit quality, nitrate accumulation in edibles
Iron (Fe) < 0.3 ppm 0.3-5.0 ppm 5.0-15 ppm > 15 ppm Clogs emitters, stains foliage/hardscapes (cosmetic), rarely toxic to plants (can be beneficial as micronutrient)

⚠️ Species Sensitivity Varies Widely

The tolerances above are general guidelines for moderately salt-tolerant landscape plants. Sensitive species (azaleas, rhododendrons, most berries) suffer at much lower salinity/sodium levels. Tolerant species (many native California plants, Mediterranean plants, succulents) handle marginal water quality without issues. Always consider plant selection when dealing with less-than-ideal water quality.

Understanding SAR (Sodium Absorption Ratio)

SAR is a calculated value representing the relative proportion of sodium to calcium and magnesium in water. It predicts the impact on soil structure:

SAR Formula:
SAR = Na⁺ / √((Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) / 2)

(Concentrations in milliequivalents per liter—your water test lab will calculate this)

Why SAR Matters:

  • High sodium relative to calcium/magnesium causes soil dispersion—clay particles separate, destroying soil structure
  • Dispersed soil has poor infiltration (water puddles on surface), poor drainage, and forms a hard crust when dry
  • Plant roots suffocate from lack of air in compacted, waterlogged soil
  • High SAR water gradually degrades soil over years—effects are cumulative and difficult to reverse

Managing High SAR Water:

  • Apply gypsum (calcium sulfate) to soil annually—calcium displaces sodium and improves structure
  • Increase irrigation frequency (leach salts through root zone)
  • Improve drainage (French drains, soil amendments)
  • Select salt-tolerant plants adapted to sodic conditions
  • In severe cases (SAR >15), consider water treatment (reverse osmosis for high-value landscaping, alternate water source for most)

Boron Toxicity in Irrigation Water

Boron is a micronutrient essential for plant growth, but the line between beneficial and toxic is very narrow. Well water in some Southern California areas (especially near geothermal or volcanic activity) contains elevated boron.

Boron Sensitivity by Plant Type:

  • Very Sensitive (<0.5 ppm): Citrus, stone fruits (peach, apricot, nectarine), berries, avocado
  • Sensitive (0.5-1.0 ppm): Most vegetables, beans, roses, ornamental trees
  • Moderately Tolerant (1.0-2.0 ppm): Many landscape shrubs, turfgrass, some ornamentals
  • Tolerant (>2.0 ppm): Asparagus, date palms, desert-adapted plants, some native California species

Symptoms of Boron Toxicity:

  • Leaf tip and margin yellowing (chlorosis) progressing to necrosis (brown, dead tissue)
  • Leaf drop, starting with older leaves
  • Stunted growth and reduced vigor
  • Die-back of shoot tips in severe cases

Managing High Boron Water:

  • Leaching: Apply excess water to push boron below root zone (requires good drainage)
  • Plant selection: Choose boron-tolerant species—avoid fruit trees and vegetables
  • Dilution: Blend with low-boron water source if available
  • Reverse osmosis: Only practical treatment, but expensive for whole-property irrigation

Boron cannot be removed economically for most landscape irrigation. If your well water has >1.5 ppm boron, focus on selecting tolerant plants rather than attempting treatment.

Testing Your Irrigation Water

Every irrigation well should have a comprehensive water analysis before system design. Key tests include:

Standard Irrigation Water Quality Test Panel

  • pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS)
  • Major ions: calcium, magnesium, sodium, bicarbonate, chloride, sulfate
  • SAR calculation (sodium absorption ratio)
  • Boron
  • Iron and manganese (if filtration concerns)
  • Nitrate-nitrogen (especially near agricultural areas)
  • Hardness (especially for drip systems)

Cost: $80-$150 from agricultural or irrigation-specific labs
Frequency: Initial test before system installation, retest every 3-5 years or if plant problems develop

Note: Standard drinking water tests don't include critical irrigation parameters like SAR, boron, or sodium. Use a lab specializing in agricultural or irrigation water analysis.

Pressure Requirements by Irrigation Type

Irrigation systems have distinct pressure requirements. Too little pressure and sprinklers don't atomize properly or cover their intended area. Too much pressure and you get misting, fogging, waste, and potential equipment damage.

Optimal Pressure Ranges

Irrigation Type Minimum Pressure Optimal Pressure Maximum Pressure Effects of Wrong Pressure
Drip Irrigation (Standard Emitters) 10 PSI 15-25 PSI 30 PSI Low: uneven flow. High: emitter blowout, leaks at connections
Drip Irrigation (Pressure-Compensating Emitters) 15 PSI 20-40 PSI 50 PSI More forgiving, maintains consistent flow across wider pressure range
Fixed Spray Heads (Pop-up) 20 PSI 30-35 PSI 40 PSI Low: poor pattern, uneven coverage. High: misting, fogging, waste
Rotary Nozzles (MP Rotators, etc.) 25 PSI 35-45 PSI 55 PSI Low: incomplete rotation, poor distribution. High: overspray, fogging
Small Rotor Heads (Gear-Driven Pop-up) 30 PSI 40-50 PSI 65 PSI Low: reduced throw distance, skipped rotation. High: fine mist, wind drift
Large Rotor Heads (Impact or Gear) 35 PSI 45-60 PSI 80 PSI Low: short throw, poor coverage. High: excessive misting, mechanical stress
Agricultural Impact Sprinklers 40 PSI 50-80 PSI 100 PSI Varies by model; check manufacturer specs for optimal performance
Bubblers and Micro-Sprays 10 PSI 15-25 PSI 30 PSI Low: weak flow. High: splashing, excessive flow, erosion

Pressure Regulation Strategies

Most well pumps operate at 50-70 PSI to serve household needs. For irrigation systems requiring lower or more precise pressure, you need regulation:

1. Zone-Level Pressure Regulators

  • Location: After each zone valve, before sprinkler heads
  • Function: Reduces incoming pressure to set point (e.g., 40 PSI) regardless of supply pressure fluctuations
  • Best for: Systems with mixed head types, uneven terrain, long pipe runs causing pressure variation
  • Cost: $15-$50 per zone
  • Types: Preset (factory-set pressure) or adjustable (field-adjustable)

2. Main-Line Pressure Regulator

  • Location: At wellhead or where irrigation splits from domestic supply
  • Function: Reduces overall system pressure to safe level for irrigation (e.g., 50-55 PSI)
  • Best for: Protecting entire irrigation system from excessive well pressure
  • Cost: $50-$200 depending on size and quality

3. Pressure-Compensating Emitters/Heads

  • Function: Built-in regulation maintains consistent flow across wide pressure range (20-50 PSI)
  • Best for: Sloped terrain, long zone runs, systems with pressure variation
  • Applications: Drip emitters, rotary nozzles (MP Rotators), pressure-regulating spray heads
  • Cost premium: 20-50% more than standard emitters/heads
  • Benefit: Uniform water distribution despite pressure differences—critical for slopes

4. Constant-Pressure Pump System (VFD)

  • Function: Pump speed adjusts automatically to maintain exact pressure setpoint regardless of demand
  • Best for: New installations, properties with both household and irrigation on same well, high-end systems
  • Cost: $3,500-$8,000 for pump, VFD controller, installation
  • Benefits: Perfect pressure, energy savings, reduced maintenance, no pressure tank cycling

Managing Pressure on Sloped Properties

Sloped terrain creates pressure gradients—low points have higher pressure (gravity adds pressure), high points have lower pressure (pump must overcome elevation).

Rule of Thumb: Every 10 feet of elevation change = 4.3 PSI pressure change

Example: Irrigation zone spans 50 feet of elevation (top to bottom of slope)

  • Pressure at bottom = System pressure + (50 ÷ 10 × 4.3) = +21.5 PSI
  • Pressure at top = System pressure - 21.5 PSI
  • If system pressure is 50 PSI: bottom has 71.5 PSI, top has 28.5 PSI
  • Bottom heads mist and waste water, top heads barely reach their coverage area

Solutions for Sloped Properties:

  • Zone by elevation: Group heads at similar elevations in same zone, use different pressures for different zones
  • Pressure-compensating heads: Maintain uniform flow despite pressure variations
  • Pressure regulation at each head: Individual regulators balance pressure across zone
  • Dedicated pump/booster for high zones: For extreme elevation changes, pump separately to upper areas

Storage Tank + Pump Systems for Low-Yield Wells

One of the most common challenges in rural Southern California: your well only produces 8-12 GPM, but your desired irrigation system needs 20-40 GPM peak flow. The solution is a storage tank + booster pump system that decouples well production from irrigation delivery.

How Storage Tank Systems Work

The concept is simple but highly effective:

  1. Well pump fills storage tank slowly at well's sustainable rate (e.g., 8 GPM continuously or intermittently)
  2. Storage tank accumulates water over time (hours or days depending on capacity and usage)
  3. Irrigation controller triggers booster pump when zones activate
  4. Booster pump delivers high flow from tank to irrigation system (e.g., 30 GPM to sprinkler zones)
  5. Well continues to refill tank between or during irrigation cycles
  6. Tank level monitoring prevents running dry (low-level shutoff protects booster pump)

This allows you to irrigate large zones at high flow rates even though your well can't sustain that output continuously. The well keeps working at its comfortable pace, filling the "battery" that is the storage tank.

Sizing Your Storage Tank

Storage tank size depends on three factors:

Factor 1: Peak Irrigation Demand

Calculate total gallons needed for your longest irrigation cycle:

Example: Largest zone runs at 30 GPM for 45 minutes
30 GPM × 45 minutes = 1,350 gallons per cycle

Factor 2: Well Recovery Rate

Your well produces X gallons during irrigation cycle:

Example: Well produces 8 GPM
8 GPM × 45 minutes = 360 gallons replenished during cycle
