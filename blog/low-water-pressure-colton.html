Local Geology and Its Role in Water Pressure Challenges

The geology of Colton and the surrounding San Bernardino County area is dominated by the Bunker Hill Basin, a sub-basin of the larger Upper Santa Ana Valley Groundwater Basin. This basin consists of alluvial deposits—layers of sand, gravel, and silt—deposited over millennia by the Santa Ana River and its tributaries. While these materials generally have good permeability, allowing water to flow through, heavy pumping for municipal, agricultural, and industrial use has led to overdraft conditions in parts of the basin. This overdraft reduces groundwater levels, impacting the static water level in private wells and often resulting in decreased pressure as pumps struggle to draw water from deeper depths.

Another geological consideration is the presence of finer sediments like clay within the aquifer, which can create confining layers that restrict water movement to certain wells. Additionally, faulting in the region—Colton lies near the San Jacinto Fault zone—can compartmentalize groundwater, meaning some wells may tap into isolated pockets with limited recharge. High mineral content in the water, influenced by the river’s interaction with local soils, can also lead to scaling in pumps and pipes, further exacerbating pressure issues. At SCWS, our C-57 licensed technicians understand these geological intricacies and use them to inform our diagnostic and repair strategies for Colton properties.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Colton

Low water pressure in Colton can arise from multiple sources, many linked to the area’s geological and environmental characteristics. Drawing on our extensive experience in San Bernardino County, we’ve identified the following as frequent causes for well owners:

  • Groundwater Overdraft: Heavy extraction from the Bunker Hill Basin for urban and industrial needs lowers water tables, reducing well yield and forcing pumps to work harder, often resulting in lower pressure.
  • Mineral Scaling: Water from the Santa Ana River aquifer often carries high levels of calcium and other minerals, leading to scale buildup in pumps, pipes, and pressure tanks, which restricts flow.
  • Pump Wear from Sediment: Sandy and silty deposits from the alluvial aquifer can wear down pump impellers over time, reducing efficiency and pressure output, a common issue near river-influenced basins.
  • Pressure Tank Malfunctions: Fluctuating temperatures and mineral-rich water can cause pressure tanks to lose air charge or develop internal clogs, leading to inconsistent pressure and pump short cycling.
  • Corroded or Clogged Pipes: Older piping systems in Colton homes, especially galvanized steel, may corrode or accumulate sediment from the aquifer, reducing water flow and pressure.
  • Seasonal Water Table Drops: During dry months or drought years, reduced recharge from rainfall exacerbates groundwater depletion, impacting well performance in the region.

Maintenance Tips to Prevent Low Water Pressure in Colton

Regular maintenance can prevent low water pressure and extend the life of your well system, especially in Colton’s semi-arid environment with heavy aquifer demand. Here are practical tips suited to local conditions:

  • Annual Well Inspections: Schedule a professional check with SCWS each year ($150-$250) to assess pump, tank, and yield, ideally before the dry season when water tables drop.
  • Conserve Water During Droughts: Reduce non-essential usage during low rainfall periods to ease strain on the Bunker Hill Basin, helping maintain well pressure.
  • Monitor Pump Performance: Listen for unusual noises or cycling patterns that might indicate sediment wear or electrical issues, common in Colton’s aquifer conditions.
  • Flush Pipes Regularly: Run water through all outlets periodically to prevent sediment buildup, and request a professional flush if flow seems restricted.
  • Combat Hard Water: Use a water softener or descaler to minimize mineral deposits from Santa Ana River-influenced groundwater, and clean fixtures to detect scaling early.
  • Check Tank Pressure Monthly: Use a tire gauge to verify the pressure tank’s air charge (typically 2 PSI below the pump’s cut-in setting) to avoid short cycling and pressure drops.

Proactive care reduces the risk of sudden pressure loss. SCWS offers maintenance plans to manage these tasks for Colton residents, providing reliability in a region with stressed groundwater resources.
