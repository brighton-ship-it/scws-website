How Your Well Pressure System Works

A Colton well system builds pressure with four key parts. The pump draws water from the aquifer, usually a submersible pump in deeper Bunker Hill Basin wells. The pressure tank stores water under a cushion of compressed air so the pump does not run on every faucet. The pressure switch turns the pump on at the cut-in setting (commonly 40 psi) and off at cut-out (commonly 60 psi). The plumbing, screens, and any sediment filtration then carry water to your fixtures. Households are designed to hold a steady 40 to 60 psi, so when we arrive, that band is the first thing we measure. Weak flow almost always traces back to one of these components.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Colton Wells

Colton's alluvial aquifer, heavy regional pumping, and mineral-rich Santa Ana River water create recurring pressure problems:

  • Groundwater overdraft: Heavy municipal, industrial, and agricultural extraction from the Bunker Hill Basin lowers the water table, forcing pumps to work harder and reducing well yield in dry periods.
  • Mineral scaling: Calcium-rich groundwater leaves scale inside pumps, pipes, and pressure tanks, narrowing the flow path over time.
  • Sediment-worn pumps: Sand and silt from the alluvial basin grind down pump impellers, cutting efficiency and pressure.
  • Waterlogged or failing pressure tank: A lost air charge causes short-cycling and pressure that swings between weak and strong.
  • Corroded or clogged pipes: Older galvanized supply lines in Colton homes rust and collect sediment, restricting flow.
  • Seasonal water-table drops: During drought years, reduced recharge deepens groundwater decline and worsens pressure.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks can save time and sometimes a service call:

  1. One fixture or the whole house? A single weak tap usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge, not the well. Clean the aerator first.
  2. Read the gauge. Watch the pressure gauge near the tank during use. If it stays below 35 psi or swings rapidly, note that for us.
  3. Tap the tank. Hollow on top and solid below is healthy; solid all the way up suggests a waterlogged tank.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off points to the tank or switch; a pump that runs without building pressure points to the pump, a leak, or a falling water level.
  5. Check for leaks. A spinning meter with everything off or a sudden bill increase signals a hidden leak.

Please leave the 240-volt wiring, well cap, and pressurized components to a professional, the shock and stored-energy hazards are serious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Colton well pressure drop during late summer?

Heavy regional pumping from the Bunker Hill Basin plus reduced recharge in dry months lowers the water table, so pumps draw from deeper and pressure falls. We test your yield and pump setting to confirm and recommend the right fix.

Is mineral scaling really a problem with Colton water?

Yes. Santa Ana River-influenced groundwater is calcium-rich and leaves scale in pumps and pipes. Descaling and treatment such as a softener protect your equipment and restore flow.

How do I tell if my pump or tank is the problem?

Rapid pump cycling usually points to the tank or switch; a pump that runs constantly without building pressure usually points to the pump, a leak, or low water level. A gauge and tank test confirms it quickly.

Do I need a permit for well work in San Bernardino County?

Pump and tank swaps generally do not, but new wells, deepening, and decommissioning do. As a licensed C-57 contractor we handle the required permits.

Could low pressure mean my Colton well is running dry?

With basin overdraft it is possible. A pump running without building pressure can mean a falling water level. We run a yield test and recommend options from pump adjustment to hydrofracturing or, if needed, a new well.

Maintenance Tips for Colton Well Owners

Regular maintenance protects your pressure and your equipment in Colton's semi-arid, hard-water environment. A few consistent habits between service visits make a real difference:

  • Book an annual well checkup before the dry season, when basin water levels typically fall and pumps work hardest.
  • Combat mineral scale with a softener or descaler and clean fixtures periodically to catch buildup early.
  • Verify the tank air charge roughly every six months, keeping it about 2 psi below the pump cut-in setting.
  • Conserve during drought to ease strain on the Bunker Hill Basin and help hold pressure.
  • Listen to your pump for unusual cycling or noise that can signal sediment wear or an electrical fault.
