What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Jamul well.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

How Iron Gets Into Jamul Well Water

Rust is iron that has oxidized. On a Jamul well it reaches your tap through several distinct routes, and identifying the right one is the difference between an inexpensive filter and an unnecessary, costly repair.

Dissolved Iron From Granite

Jamul sits on weathered and fractured granitic rock. As groundwater percolates through decomposed granite, it picks up dissolved ferrous iron, which rises clear and then turns rusty orange as soon as it meets air and oxidizes. This is the most common cause of discolored water on local wells and is resolved with an iron-removal system.

Iron Bacteria

Iron bacteria are harmless microbes that feed on iron and leave a reddish, slimy residue inside the casing, pump, and pressure tank. The classic sign is a gelatinous orange film in the toilet tank or a rainbow sheen on standing water. They are widespread in backcountry wells and need shock chlorination to clear.

Corroding Steel Casing

Many older Jamul wells were finished with steel casing. After decades underground, that steel rusts and sheds particles into the water. A well that has slowly gotten rustier over many years may have a casing approaching the end of its service life.

Pump and Sediment Issues

A dropping water table during drought can cause the pump to draw in sediment and rust from near the bottom of the well, producing intermittent brown water tied to heavy water use.

Common Jamul Well Scenarios

  • Seasonal iron changes: Winter rains that recharge the aquifer, and late-summer drawdown, can both shift iron levels noticeably.
  • Restarting an idle well: Wells left off while a property sits vacant often produce a slug of rust and iron bacteria when brought back online.
  • Low-yield wells in fractured rock: Granite wells can produce modest flows (often single-digit gallons per minute), and pulling hard on a low-yield well stirs up sediment.
  • Decades-old steel casing: Many legacy Jamul wells are now at the age where casing corrosion becomes the leading rust source.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Hot, cold, or both? Hot-only points to the water heater; both sides points to the well source or casing.
  2. Does it clear after running the tap? Rust that clears sits in your pipes; rust that never clears comes continuously from the well.
  3. Constant or intermittent? Intermittent brown water during heavy use suggests sediment drawdown; constant rust suggests dissolved iron or casing corrosion.
  4. Check the toilet tank: Orange slime means iron bacteria; gritty orange sediment means dissolved iron or casing rust.
  5. The glass test: A uniform tint that stays put is dissolved iron; cloudiness that settles to the bottom is particulate rust and sediment.

Solutions for Rusty Jamul Well Water

Iron-Removal Systems

For clear-water iron, an oxidizing media filter — manganese greensand, catalytic carbon, or air injection — converts dissolved iron to a filterable solid and removes it. Higher iron levels or a sulfur smell may require chlorine or peroxide injection followed by filtration. Where iron is low and hardness is significant, a correctly sized water softener can handle both.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria has colonized the well, shock chlorination and mechanical cleaning of the casing and pump are the necessary first step. A filter installed over an infected well will simply foul.

Casing Repair and Yield Improvement

A corroded steel casing can be lined or, in serious cases, the well re-cased. For low-producing granite wells, hydrofracturing uses controlled water pressure to open existing rock fractures and improve flow — a service well suited to Jamul's fractured terrain.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when rust will not clear after flushing, when it is continuous rather than first-draw only, when it comes with a drop in pressure or yield, or when the water turns sulfurous. On a well, any sudden change in color, smell, or pressure deserves a prompt inspection — it can flag a failing pump, a dropping water table, or casing failure, all of which cost more the longer they are left.

Keeping Iron Under Control on a Rural Well

Because a Jamul well is your sole water source, a little routine care pays off in reliability as well as water quality. An annual well checkup is the single best habit: it catches early iron-bacteria growth before it becomes an entrenched colony, inspects the casing and wellhead for corrosion and pest intrusion, and confirms the pressure tank and switch are working within spec. Treatment media is consumable — greensand, catalytic carbon, and softener resin all lose capacity over time — so checking and replacing it on schedule keeps your water clear and prevents iron from slipping past a tired filter.

It also pays to keep an eye on your well's behavior between visits. A gradual rise in rust, a new metallic or rotten-egg odor, longer pump run times, or fluctuating pressure are all early signals worth a phone call. On a rural property, catching these trends early is far cheaper than an emergency at the worst possible moment, and it lets us schedule any work on your timeline rather than under pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is everyone in Jamul on a private well?

The large majority of Jamul properties rely on private wells, since municipal water does not reach most of the area. That means rusty water here is almost always a groundwater or well-equipment issue rather than a city plumbing problem.

Why does my Jamul well water come out clear but turn orange in the glass?

That is dissolved iron. It is invisible underground but oxidizes and turns rusty orange when it hits the air. An iron-removal filter fixes it.

Is rusty well water safe to drink?

Iron is not considered a health hazard at typical concentrations, but it tastes metallic and stains. Sudden or persistent discoloration, especially with a sulfur smell, should be tested before drinking.

My well is producing less water and more rust. What's happening?

That combination often means a dropping water table or a corroding casing causing the pump to pull sediment. A downhole inspection identifies the cause, and options range from a treatment system to hydrofracturing or re-casing.

Can hydrofracturing help my low-yield granite well?

Often, yes. Jamul wells sit in fractured granite, and hydrofracturing opens existing fractures to boost flow. It typically costs $3,000 to $8,000 and can be a cost-effective alternative to drilling a new well.
