What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your North Park well.

Rusty Well Water in North Park: What San Diego Homeowners Need to Know

North Park sits in the heart of San Diego's urban core, but the surrounding mesas and canyons of central San Diego County are still home to a surprising number of private wells, agricultural parcels, and older properties that draw groundwater rather than relying solely on city supply. If you have noticed your water turning a rusty orange, leaving brown rings in the toilet bowl, or tasting faintly of metal, you are dealing with one of the most common groundwater complaints in the region: iron. The good news is that rusty water is almost always a solvable problem once the actual source is identified.

At Southern California Well Service, we have spent more than 30 years diagnosing discolored water across San Diego County, and the pattern in and around North Park is consistent. The fractured granitic bedrock and decomposed granite soils that underlie much of central San Diego release dissolved iron and manganese into groundwater, and the older steel infrastructure on many properties accelerates the staining. Understanding which of these is to blame is the first step toward clear water.

How Iron and Manganese Get Into Your Water

Iron is one of the most abundant elements in the earth's crust, and the crystalline rock formations beneath San Diego County are full of it. As rainwater and irrigation percolate down through soil and weathered granite, slightly acidic water dissolves iron and its chemical cousin manganese, carrying them into the aquifer your well taps. When that water is drawn up and exposed to oxygen at your faucet, the dissolved iron oxidizes and precipitates out as the familiar orange-brown rust you see in the sink.

There are several distinct ways this shows up in a North Park-area home:

  • Dissolved (ferrous) iron: Water looks clear at the tap but turns orange after sitting in a glass or bathtub. This is the classic signature of groundwater iron.
  • Oxidized (ferric) iron: Water is already cloudy and rust-colored as it comes out, suggesting iron is precipitating inside the well or pipes.
  • Manganese: Produces blackish or brown-purple staining and is common alongside iron in San Diego County groundwater.
  • Iron bacteria: A reddish, slimy buildup in toilet tanks and on fixtures. These naturally occurring bacteria feed on iron and create a gelatinous mess that clogs screens and pumps.
  • Corroding steel components: An aging steel well casing, an old galvanized drop pipe, or galvanized household plumbing rusting from the inside out.

Local Scenarios We See in Central San Diego

Because many North Park and adjacent mid-city properties have wells or plumbing that date back decades, corrosion is a frequent culprit. We often find homes where the cold water runs clear but the hot water is rusty, which points squarely at a failing water heater anode rod rather than the well itself. In other cases, water that was fine for years suddenly turns orange after a pump replacement disturbs sediment, or after a dry winter lowers the water table and draws the pump intake into an iron-rich zone near the bottom of the well.

Is Rusty Water Dangerous to Drink?

Iron and manganese are classified by the EPA as secondary (aesthetic) contaminants, meaning they affect taste, color, and odor rather than posing an acute health threat at typical concentrations. That said, rusty water is rarely something to ignore. It stains laundry and porcelain permanently, leaves a metallic taste, scales up water heaters and pipes, and can harbor the iron bacteria that produce that unpleasant slime. Elevated manganese, in particular, is worth testing for, since high levels have raised health questions. The only way to know what is actually in your water and at what concentration is a proper test.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of observation can save you money and help us pinpoint the problem faster:

  • Hot only or both? If only hot water is discolored, suspect the water heater, not the well.
  • One fixture or the whole house? A single rusty faucet usually means a localized pipe or fixture issue.
  • Constant or intermittent? Rust that appears only when the pump first kicks on suggests sediment settling in the pressure tank or well.
  • Any slime? Reddish slime in the toilet tank is the tell-tale sign of iron bacteria.
  • Recent changes? New staining after heavy rain, a drought period, or recent pump work narrows the diagnosis considerably.

Proven Solutions for Rusty Water

Iron and Manganese Filtration

For dissolved iron and manganese from the aquifer, an oxidizing backwash filter (using catalytic media or air injection) is the workhorse solution. For lower iron levels combined with hard water, a properly sized water softener can also remove modest amounts of iron. Sizing matters, which is why we test first.

Shock Chlorination and Well Cleaning

When iron bacteria are the problem, filtration alone will not solve it. Shock chlorination disinfects the well, pump, and plumbing to kill the colony, often followed by a maintenance plan to keep it from returning.

Casing and Plumbing Repairs

If a corroded steel casing is contributing rust, lining or replacing the casing stops the contamination at the source. Inside the home, swapping old galvanized pipe for PEX or copper eliminates household rust.

Sediment Pre-Filtration

A sediment filter ahead of any treatment system protects the more expensive equipment and is an inexpensive first line of defense against particulate rust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my North Park well water turn orange only after it sits in a glass?

That is the classic signature of dissolved ferrous iron, which is colorless underground but oxidizes into orange rust once it contacts air. An oxidizing iron filter is the standard fix.

Is iron in my San Diego County well water a health risk?

Iron is an aesthetic contaminant, so it is generally not an acute health hazard, but the staining and metallic taste are real nuisances. We recommend testing to also check manganese levels.

Why is only my hot water rusty in my North Park home?

That usually points to the water heater rather than the well. A corroding anode rod or rusting tank discolors hot water while the well stays clear; servicing or replacing the heater typically resolves it.

What is the reddish slime in my toilet tank?

That is iron bacteria, a naturally occurring organism that feeds on iron and clogs fixtures and pumps. Shock chlorination kills the colony and clears the slime.

How much does it cost to test my water?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any treatment or repair we perform. It measures iron and manganese so we can size a system correctly.

Will a water softener get rid of my rusty water?

A softener can remove modest amounts of iron along with hardness, but for higher iron levels a dedicated oxidizing iron filter is more effective. Testing tells us which approach fits your North Park well.
