How Your Pressure Tank Works

Your pressure tank is one of the most important — and most overlooked — components in your well system. It serves as the buffer between your well pump and your household plumbing, storing pressurized water so the pump doesn't have to start every time someone opens a faucet, flushes a toilet, or runs the dishwasher.

Inside a modern bladder-type pressure tank, a heavy-duty rubber bladder separates the water from a pre-charged air pocket. When the pump fills the tank, water enters the bladder and compresses the air above it, building pressure. When you open a faucet, the compressed air pushes water out of the bladder and through your plumbing. The pump stays off until tank pressure drops to the cut-in setting — typically 30 PSI on a standard 30/50 system — then it kicks on to refill the tank.

This on-off cycle is the pump duty cycle, and keeping it controlled is critical to your pump's longevity. A properly sized pressure tank limits pump starts to 6 to 8 cycles per hour during peak demand. Without a functioning tank, your pump would start and stop with every glass of water, every toilet flush, every sprinkler zone. That rapid cycling — called short cycling — destroys pump motors, stresses electrical connections, damages pipe fittings, and significantly increases your electric bill.

Signs Your Pressure Tank Is Failing

Pressure tanks rarely fail all at once. They degrade gradually over months or years, and homeowners often adapt to the declining performance without realizing there's a problem until the pump burns out entirely. Here are the warning signs we see most often in the Navajo area:

  • Pump short cycling: The pump turns on and off every few seconds or kicks on every time any fixture is used. This is the number one indicator of a waterlogged pressure tank — the bladder has ruptured, the air charge has leaked, or the tank can no longer store pressurized water. Short cycling can slash a pump's lifespan from 15 years to 2 or 3.
  • Fluctuating water pressure: Pressure surges briefly when the pump activates, then drops rapidly before the pump restarts. You might notice a pulsing sensation in the shower or water that goes strong for a moment then weakens noticeably.
  • Waterlogged tank: Tap the side of your pressure tank from top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow near the top (air pocket) and solid near the bottom (water). If the entire tank sounds solid and feels uniformly heavy, it's waterlogged — the air cushion that provides the pressure buffer is gone.
  • Visible rust or corrosion: Check the tank base, pipe connections, and Schrader valve area for rust and mineral buildup. The Navajo area's warm inland climate and mineralized groundwater can accelerate corrosion, particularly on outdoor installations.
  • Water leaking from the tank shell: Any water dripping from the tank body itself — not from pipe connections — means the tank has corroded through and needs immediate replacement. Never attempt to patch or weld a leaking pressure tank.
  • Air spurting from faucets: Bursts of air mixed with water when you turn on a tap indicate a ruptured bladder, allowing the air charge to mix directly with the water instead of staying separated.
  • Pump running continuously without building pressure: If the pump runs for extended periods without the pressure gauge reaching the cut-off point, the tank may have a ruptured bladder or severe air charge loss. Left unchecked, the pump will overheat and fail.

Well Challenges Specific to Navajo

The Navajo community — encompassing neighborhoods around Lake Murray, San Carlos, Del Cerro, and Allied Gardens — is a well-established residential area in eastern San Diego. Developed primarily during the 1960s and 1970s suburban expansion, many properties in this area were originally built with private well systems. While most homes have since connected to city water, a significant number of properties still rely on wells for household use, landscape irrigation, or both. The local conditions create specific challenges for pressure tank performance and longevity.

Hard water and mineral deposits: Groundwater in the Navajo area tends to be moderately hard, with elevated calcium and magnesium levels drawn from the underlying granitic and metavolcanic bedrock formations. These minerals cause scale buildup inside pressure tanks over time, reducing effective capacity and coating the rubber bladder. Mineral deposits stiffen the bladder material, making it less flexible and more prone to cracking. Well owners in the Navajo area with untreated hard water should plan on more frequent tank inspections than the standard annual schedule — every 6 months is our recommendation for properties with hardness above 15 grains per gallon.

Lake Murray basin geology: The Navajo community sits along the Lake Murray drainage basin, where wells draw from fractured bedrock aquifers and decomposed granite formations. Well yields in this area can vary significantly from property to property — some wells produce 10+ GPM while neighboring properties might see only 2 to 3 GPM. For low-yield wells, proper pressure tank sizing is especially critical because the tank must maximize the usable water from each pump cycle. An undersized tank on a low-yield well forces the pump to cycle frequently, and each short cycle means the pump runs without producing its full capacity — wasting energy and accelerating wear.

Warm inland temperatures: The Navajo area sits far enough inland to experience significantly warmer summer temperatures than coastal San Diego — regularly reaching the mid-90s to low 100s during July through September. Pressure tanks installed outdoors or in uninsulated enclosures are subjected to daily temperature swings that stress the rubber bladder and cause air charge pressure to fluctuate. A tank that reads correct air pressure at 70°F in the morning may be over-pressured by mid-afternoon on a hot day. Over years, these thermal cycles fatigue the bladder material and shorten tank lifespan by 2 to 4 years compared to tanks in temperature-controlled environments.

Large lot irrigation demands: Many Navajo-area properties sit on larger suburban lots — half-acre and above — with established landscaping, mature trees, and garden areas that require substantial irrigation. Running household water and landscape irrigation from the same well system puts heavy strain on pressure tanks, especially during the dry season when outdoor watering peaks. We frequently see undersized tanks on Navajo properties that were originally specified for household use alone but now serve the added irrigation demand of decades of landscape growth. Upgrading to a properly sized tank — or installing a dedicated irrigation tank on a separate circuit — is one of the most cost-effective improvements for these properties.

1960s-1970s era infrastructure: Most of the Navajo community's housing stock was built during a period when well equipment standards were different than today. We encounter older diaphragm tanks (or air-over-water models with no bladder at all), undersized galvanized steel piping, aging pressure switches, and wellheads that may not meet current sealing requirements. When replacing a pressure tank in the Navajo area, we always inspect the surrounding components and recommend any upgrades needed to bring the system up to modern reliability and safety standards.

Pressure Tank Sizing Guide for Navajo Properties

Undersized pressure tanks are one of the most common problems we encounter in the Navajo area. A tank that's too small for your actual water demand causes excessive pump cycling, shortens pump life, wastes energy, and creates the pressure fluctuations that make showers inconsistent and irrigation unreliable.

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: 30 to 50 gallon tank minimum. A 44-gallon Well-X-Trol WX-203 is our standard recommendation for smaller Navajo homes.
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: 50 to 85 gallon tank. The WX-250 (62 gallon) or WX-302 (86 gallon) handles the higher demand of larger households.
  • Properties with landscape irrigation: Many Navajo homes sit on larger lots with significant watering needs. These properties need 85+ gallon tanks, or a dedicated second tank for the irrigation circuit to prevent competition between indoor and outdoor demand.
  • Low-yield wells (under 5 GPM): Common in the Navajo area's fractured bedrock geology. These wells benefit from larger tanks that store more water per cycle, letting the aquifer recover between pump starts.

Remember that a tank's rated size isn't the same as usable drawdown. A 50-gallon tank on a 30/50 system delivers only about 14 to 16 gallons between pump cycles. We calculate exact drawdown requirements based on your pump's GPM rating and actual peak usage to ensure proper sizing.

Pressure Tank Maintenance Tips for Navajo Well Owners

  • Check air charge every 6 months: Turn off the pump and drain the tank completely. Check the air pressure at the Schrader valve with a tire gauge — it should read 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (28 PSI for a 30/50 system). Add air with a bicycle pump if needed. Never check with the tank full of water.
  • Listen for short cycling: Stand near your tank and run water in the house. If the pump clicks on and off more than once per minute, or the pressure gauge bounces rapidly, your tank needs professional service.
  • Inspect for corrosion seasonally: Check pipe connections, the tank base, and the Schrader valve for rust and mineral buildup. Navajo's warm inland climate accelerates corrosion during the hotter months — check before and after summer.
  • Shade outdoor tanks: If your tank is exposed to direct sun, add shade cloth, a UV-resistant cover, or a simple roof structure. Reducing daily temperature swings extends bladder life significantly — especially important given Navajo's hot summers.
  • Test water quality annually: Annual testing catches changes in hardness, iron content, or pH that could affect your well equipment. Particularly important for Navajo's hard groundwater, which can accelerate mineral accumulation inside the tank.
  • Professional inspection for tanks over 8 years old: A technician can catch subtle bladder degradation, switch wear, and early-stage corrosion that visual checks miss.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size pressure tank do I need?

For most residential wells, we recommend a minimum 30-gallon pressure tank. Homes with higher water demand or multiple bathrooms benefit from 50-85 gallon tanks. Proper sizing reduces pump cycling and extends pump life.

How do I know if my pressure tank is failing?

Signs of a failing pressure tank include: pump short cycling (turning on and off frequently), waterlogged tank (heavy when you tap it), fluctuating water pressure, and the tank feeling uniformly heavy rather than having an air-filled top section.

How long do pressure tanks last?

Quality pressure tanks typically last 10-15 years. Bladder-type tanks (like Well-X-Trol) tend to last longer than diaphragm tanks. Annual pressure checks can extend tank life significantly.
