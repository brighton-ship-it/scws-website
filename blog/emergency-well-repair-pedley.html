Most Common Well Emergencies in Pedley

Pedley's older neighborhoods mean a mix of well ages and equipment vintages, and that variety shows up in the calls we get. Here are the failures we respond to most frequently across this part of Jurupa Valley:

  • Sudden no water at every tap — the most alarming symptom, and usually a sign of pump or electrical failure.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout — older submersible pumps on long-established properties eventually wear out, especially when feeding both a home and livestock.
  • Pressure tank failure — a failed bladder causes the pump to short-cycle, hammering the system and leaving you with erratic pressure.
  • Well going dry or low yield — heavy demand from barns and irrigation can outpace a marginal well during dry spells.
  • Control box or electrical failure — aging wiring and weathered control boxes are a frequent culprit on equestrian parcels.
  • Burst pipe or leak — cracked supply lines and failed fittings drain pressure and waste water.
  • No water after a power outage — a storm or grid disruption can knock a pump offline or trip its safeguards.

What to Check First

A handful of safe checks can occasionally restore your water on the spot, and even when they do not, they give us a head start. Go through them one at a time:

  1. Check the breaker. Open your electrical panel and look for a tripped well or pump breaker. Reset it once. If it trips again right away, leave it alone — that signals an electrical fault best handled by a technician.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Locate the gauge near your pressure tank. A healthy system typically reads between 40 and 60 psi. Zero usually means the pump is not producing pressure.
  3. Check the pressure tank. Rap on the tank with your knuckles. The top should sound hollow and the bottom solid; a tank that sounds full top to bottom has likely lost its air charge.
  4. Look at the pressure switch. The small box on the line near the tank often shows the problem — pitted contacts, corrosion, or insect intrusion can stop the pump from starting.
  5. Confirm the grid. Verify the outage is not affecting your whole street. If neighbors are also dark, the issue may be utility-side rather than your well.

What to Do While You Wait

After you have reached us, put the waiting time to good use. Draw whatever water remains in your pressure tank into buckets and troughs — on a Pedley horse property, keeping your animals watered is the first priority, so fill stock tanks before anything else. If you suspect an electrical problem, switch off the pump breaker so it cannot keep trying to start and damage the motor. Unlock gates and clear access to your wellhead, pressure tank, and panel; many Pedley parcels have equipment tucked behind fencing or near the barn, and easy access lets our technician get straight to work. It also helps to jot down a few details before we arrive: roughly how old the well is, when you last had it serviced, and any unusual sounds, smells, or pressure changes you noticed in the days leading up to the failure. If you have any paperwork on the original pump installation or your well depth, set it aside — that history can speed up diagnosis and help us match the right replacement parts to your system the first time.
