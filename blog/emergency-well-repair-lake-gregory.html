Most Common Well Emergencies in Lake Gregory

Mountain wells face stresses that lowland systems never see. Cold weather, snow load, and the demands of seasonal occupancy all factor into the emergencies we handle around Lake Gregory:

  • Sudden complete loss of water — nothing at the tap, frequently traced to a failed pump or a frozen, cracked line.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout — submersible pumps eventually wear out, and a failure during a snowstorm leaves a home without water at the worst possible time.
  • Pressure tank failure — a ruptured or waterlogged bladder causes sputtering taps and rapid pump cycling.
  • Frozen or burst pipes — the leading cold-season culprit up here, where exposed lines and unheated pump houses are vulnerable to hard freezes.
  • Well running low — fractured-rock mountain wells can produce unevenly, and demand spikes when seasonal cabins fill up.
  • Control box or electrical failure — moisture and cold are hard on control components and wiring.
  • No water after a power outage — mountain storms knock out power regularly, and pumps may not restart cleanly afterward.

What to Check First

Before you call, a few safe checks can sometimes pinpoint or even solve the problem. If they do not, the details help our technician arrive prepared. Take them in order:

  1. Check the breaker. In your main panel, find the well or pump breaker and look for a tripped position. Reset it once. If it trips again immediately, do not keep trying — that indicates an electrical fault for a technician to handle.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Find the gauge near your pressure tank. Most systems run between 40 and 60 psi. A reading of zero usually means no water is being delivered.
  3. Check the pressure tank. Knock along the tank's side. A hollow sound up high and a solid sound low down is normal; a uniformly solid sound suggests a failed bladder.
  4. Inspect the pressure switch. The small switch box near the tank can fail from moisture or corrosion. Burned contacts are a common, fixable cause of a dead pump.
  5. Confirm the grid. During mountain storms, verify the outage is not affecting the whole neighborhood before assuming the well itself is at fault.

What to Do While You Wait

Once help is on the way, prepare for the cold. Capture whatever water remains in your pressure tank for drinking, cooking, and flushing. If freezing temperatures are present and you suspect a frozen line, do not apply open flame to thaw pipes — that is a fire risk in a forested community. Instead, keep interior cabinets open so household heat reaches the plumbing, and let us assess the situation. Switch off the pump breaker if you suspect an electrical fault so the motor cannot keep trying to start. If snow has accumulated, clear a path to your wellhead, pump house, and electrical panel so our technician can reach the equipment without delay.
