When Your Perris Well Needs Rehabilitation

Perris is an incorporated city in western Riverside County, perched at roughly 1,470 feet in the Perris Valley just southwest of Lake Perris. From rural-residential parcels off Ramona Expressway to the agricultural ground stretching toward Nuevo, Romoland, and Mead Valley, private wells remain the backbone of daily water supply for thousands of households here. Those wells are drilled into valley-fill alluvium that blankets the granitic bedrock of the Perris Block, and that geology is exactly why local wells slow down over time. The same decomposed-granite sediment, mineral-laden groundwater, and bacteria that make Perris Valley water distinctive also work steadily against your well screen, gravel pack, and pump. Rehabilitation reverses that decline before it forces an expensive replacement.

Most owners do not wake up one morning to a dead well. The slide is gradual, and the early warning signs are easy to dismiss until the system finally cannot keep up with the house. Watch for these indicators that your Perris well is overdue for service:

  • Falling yield: the well delivers noticeably less water than it did a few seasons ago, and the pump runs far longer to refill the pressure tank.
  • Sand, silt, or grit: decomposed-granite fines and valley-fill sediment showing up in fixtures, filters, or the bottom of the toilet tank signal a deteriorating screen or an over-pumped gravel pack.
  • Hard-water scale and staining: chalky white calcium-carbonate buildup on fixtures, plus reddish iron or blackish manganese staining, points to mineral encrustation that is also coating the screen underground.
  • Rotten-egg odor or slime: a sulfur smell or gelatinous buildup is the classic fingerprint of iron-bacteria biofouling, which thrives in Perris Valley groundwater and plugs screen slots.
  • Climbing energy bills: a pump straining against added resistance draws more power, so a creeping electric bill often reflects a clogging well.
  • Short-cycling or air spitting: a pump that snaps on and off rapidly, or faucets that sputter air, usually means the static water level has dropped and the well can no longer keep the pump submerged comfortably.

Declining static levels are a regional reality, not just a single-well problem. Groundwater elevations across the Perris Valley basin have trended downward during dry cycles, so a well that once sat comfortably above the pump may now run close to the edge. Catching the symptoms early almost always means the difference between a routine cleaning and a full pump-and-screen overhaul.

Well Rehabilitation Methods We Use in Perris

There is no single fix for a tired well. The right program depends on how the well was constructed, how deep it is, how old the casing is, and what is actually fouling it. Because Perris wells are completed in alluvial valley-fill over the granitic Perris Block, we tailor each job to that setting rather than applying a one-size template. Here is how we work a typical Perris rehabilitation, step by step.

Video Inspection

Every rehabilitation begins with a downhole camera survey. Before we add a single chemical or pull a brush through the casing, we run a video log to document casing condition, locate the screen, measure the depth of accumulated sediment, and pinpoint exactly where scale, biofilm, or corrosion has taken hold. In a Perris well that often means seeing calcium-carbonate crust bridging the screen slots, rust-colored iron-bacteria growth, or a fan of decomposed-granite fill resting on the bottom. The footage tells us whether the casing is structurally sound enough to justify rehabilitation, and it gives you a clear before-and-after record of the work.

Mechanical Cleaning

Once we know the target, we attack the buildup physically. Wire brushing scours encrustation off the casing and screen, while controlled surging with a block or swab drives water in and out of the screen openings to break loose compacted scale and biofilm. High-pressure jetting reaches into individual slots and the near-well formation. Mechanical work loosens the hardened deposits that chemicals alone cannot fully dissolve, and it is especially effective on the dense calcium-carbonate scale common in hard Perris Valley water.

Chemical Treatment and Acidizing

Chemistry handles what brushing cannot reach. Targeted acid treatment dissolves calcium-carbonate scale and the iron and manganese deposits that armor the screen and the surrounding gravel pack, while specialized biocides and dispersants break down the slimy iron-bacteria colonies responsible for most biofouling odors. The blend is matched to what the camera and water tests reveal, then agitated in place so it penetrates the formation rather than just the casing wall. For many Perris wells this acidizing step alone recovers a large share of lost capacity, and paired with mechanical cleaning it delivers the most durable result.

Well Redevelopment

After loosening and dissolving the deposits, we have to get them out of the well and the formation. Air-lift pumping and additional surging pull the freed scale, broken biofilm, and migrated sediment up and out, and re-sort the gravel pack so the fine decomposed-granite particles that choke flow are removed. Redevelopment restores the natural pathways between aquifer and screen, which is the whole point of rehabilitation in a sandy alluvial setting like the Perris Valley. Skip it and the loosened debris simply re-clogs the well within months.

Screen Cleaning

The screen is where a well lives or dies, so it gets focused attention. We concentrate brushing, jetting, and chemical contact directly across the screened interval to reopen plugged slots and restore the open area the well needs to produce. A clean, fully open screen lowers the entrance velocity of water, which in turn slows the rate at which scale and iron bacteria can rebuild, and pulls less sand into the system. For older Perris wells with partially collapsed or heavily encrusted screens, this is often the single most important phase of the job.

Rehabilitation vs. New Well: What Makes Sense?

The honest question every owner asks is whether to fix the well they have or drill a new one. In Perris, a typical rehabilitation runs roughly $1,500 to $6,000, while a brand-new well commonly lands between $18,000 and $42,000 once drilling, casing, pump, and electrical are included. Rehabilitation is the clear winner when the casing is sound, the aquifer still holds water, and the lost capacity is driven by scale, biofouling, or sediment rather than a failed borehole. If the camera reveals collapsed casing, a corroded-through liner, or a static level that has fallen below a usable screen, a new well may be the smarter investment. We do not guess: the video inspection and a pumping test give us the evidence to recommend the option that actually serves your property.

When to Call a Professional

Well rehabilitation is not a do-it-yourself project. It involves pulling and reinstalling submersible equipment, handling acids and biocides safely, and reading downhole video and pumping data correctly. A misapplied chemical or an over-aggressive surge can damage a screen or drive sediment deeper into the formation, turning a routine cleaning into a replacement. If you have noticed declining yield, sand, staining, odor, or a pump that runs and runs, bring in a licensed contractor before the well fails on a hot Perris summer day.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes wells in Perris to lose production?

In the Perris Valley, the usual culprits are hard-water calcium-carbonate scale, iron and manganese deposits, iron-bacteria biofouling, and decomposed-granite sediment migrating into the screen and gravel pack. Declining basin static levels can compound the problem. Most of these are reversible with rehabilitation, so flow can often be restored without drilling a new well.

How much does well rehabilitation cost in Perris?

A typical Perris rehabilitation runs about $1,500 to $6,000 depending on well depth and the severity of the fouling. A downhole video inspection runs $300 to $600, and a diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward any rehabilitation you authorize. That is far less than the $18,000 to $42,000 a replacement well usually costs.

How long does the work take?

Most residential well rehabilitations in and around Perris are completed in one to three days. Simpler chemical-and-surge jobs may finish in a single visit, while a well that needs heavy mechanical cleaning, multiple chemical soaks, and extended redevelopment can take longer. The initial video inspection happens fast so you get answers the same visit.

Will rehabilitation fully restore my well's original flow?

Often a large share of the lost capacity comes back, especially when scale and biofouling are the main problems and the casing is sound. We cannot promise a well will pump like the day it was drilled, but the video log and a pumping test let us set realistic expectations before any work begins. If rehabilitation will not get you where you need to be, we will say so.

How often should a Perris well be rehabilitated?

Many wells benefit from rehabilitation every 10 to 20 years, but Perris Valley water is hard and prone to iron bacteria, so wells here can foul faster. The best practice is to watch for declining pressure, reduced flow, or new odor and staining, and to run a periodic video inspection so small problems are caught before they become expensive.

Do you service wells outside Perris proper?

Yes. We regularly work wells in Mead Valley, Nuevo, Romoland, Sun City, Menifee, Moreno Valley, and Lakeview, along with the rural parcels around Lake Perris. The same valley-fill geology and groundwater chemistry run across these communities, so our Perris rehabilitation methods translate directly to neighboring properties.
