What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lenwood Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Lenwood wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells Near Lenwood

Lenwood sits in the high desert of San Bernardino County, just west of Barstow along the Mojave River corridor. Well owners in this part of the Mojave face a very different groundwater picture than folks in the mountains, and it is worth being candid about that from the start. Much of the Lenwood and Barstow area sits on deep alluvial fill—layers of sand, gravel, and silt washed down over time rather than solid fractured bedrock. That distinction matters a great deal for hydrofracturing, because the technique works by opening cracks in rock, and loose desert sediment does not have those cracks to open.

We tell prospective customers the truth: a well drilled into deep Mojave River valley-floor alluvium is a weaker candidate for hydrofracturing than a mountain bedrock well. That does not mean it is never worth considering. Some properties on the desert edges and benches around Lenwood are completed in or near fractured rock at depth, and those wells can respond. The only way to know is a proper diagnostic. What we will not do is sell you a hydrofracturing job that has little chance of helping—we would rather steer you toward the option that actually fixes your water problem.

How Hydrofracturing Works—and Where It Fits in the Desert

Hydrofracturing uses an inflatable packer to seal off a section of the well, after which clean water is injected at pressures of roughly 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. In a fractured-rock well, that pressure flushes sediment from the existing cracks and widens them so groundwater flows in more freely. In a purely alluvial desert well, there is no fracture network to enhance, so the gains are usually modest at best. This is why a downhole video inspection and a look at your well log are so important around Lenwood: they tell us whether your borehole actually penetrates rock that can be fractured.

Good Candidates vs. Weak Candidates in the Mojave

  • Stronger candidate: a well on the desert margins that bottoms out in fractured bedrock or hard, consolidated formation
  • Weaker candidate: a well drawing entirely from deep, loose Mojave River sand and gravel with no rock fractures
  • Better fix for many alluvial wells: a properly sized pump set deeper, well rehabilitation and re-development, or in some cases a new or deepened well

Honesty here saves you money. If your Lenwood well is purely alluvial and the water table has simply dropped, hydrofracturing will not raise the aquifer—but lowering the pump, redeveloping the screen, or deepening the well might.

Signs Your Lenwood Well Is in Trouble

  • Falling yield — the well produces noticeably less than it did in past years
  • Sand and grit in the water — common in alluvial desert wells and a sign the screen or formation needs attention
  • Pump short-cycling or running dry — the water level is dropping faster than the well recovers
  • Air spitting from faucets — the pump is drawing the well down below the intake
  • Seasonal or drought-driven decline — Mojave groundwater levels have been under long-term pressure

Realistic Expectations for Desert Wells

Where hydrofracturing does apply—wells touching fractured rock—gains of two to several times the original flow are possible, the same range we see elsewhere in San Bernardino County. But for a textbook deep-alluvial Lenwood well, we would not promise that, and we will say so plainly after the diagnostic. Our goal is to match the right service to your well, whether that is hydrofracturing, pump and screen rehabilitation, or new construction. We back every job with documented before-and-after flow testing so the results speak for themselves.

Cost Comparison for Lenwood Property Owners

When Deepening or a New Well Makes More Sense

In the Mojave, a steadily dropping regional water table is a real factor. If your Lenwood well is alluvial and the static level has fallen below your pump, the practical fixes are setting the pump deeper, deepening the existing borehole, or drilling a new, deeper well—not hydrofracturing. Likewise, if a camera reveals a collapsed or corroded casing, rehabilitation of the old hole may not be worth it. We lay out the trade-offs in plain language and let you decide with full information.

The Bigger Picture: Mojave Groundwater

It helps to understand the regional context around Lenwood. The Mojave Basin has been under adjudication and active groundwater management for years because demand has long outpaced natural recharge, and static water levels in many desert wells have trended downward over decades. That backdrop is why we approach every Lenwood well diagnostically rather than assuming hydrofracturing is the answer. A well that is genuinely short on water because the surrounding aquifer has dropped needs a deeper pump or a deeper hole, not a rock-fracturing treatment. A well that has simply silted up or whose screen has fouled may be fully restored by redevelopment. And the occasional well that reaches fractured rock at depth can be a legitimate hydrofracturing candidate. Sorting those scenarios apart is the most valuable thing we do on the first visit, and it is why our recommendations save desert customers real money.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hydrofracturing in Lenwood

Is my Lenwood desert well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

It depends on your well's geology. Wells drilled into deep Mojave River alluvium loose sand and gravel are weak candidates because hydrofracturing works by opening rock fractures, which alluvium lacks. Wells that reach fractured bedrock on the desert margins can respond well. We use a diagnostic, well log review, and video inspection to tell you honestly which category your well falls into.

If hydrofracturing won't help, what will fix my low-yield desert well?

For alluvial Lenwood wells, the usual solutions are setting the pump deeper, redeveloping the well screen, deepening the existing borehole, or drilling a new, deeper well. Long-term declines in the Mojave water table often call for reaching deeper water rather than fracturing rock that is not there.

How much does hydrofracturing cost if it is appropriate for my well?

When hydrofracturing is a good fit, it typically costs $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey desert well generally runs $18,000 to $42,000, and a replacement pump $2,500 to $5,500, so rehabilitation is usually worth exploring first.

Why do you charge a diagnostic fee before recommending work?

The $125 diagnostic lets us flow-test your well, review the well log, and run a camera survey so we can give you an accurate, honest recommendation. The fee is credited toward the cost of any work you choose to have done.

Does hydrofracturing add chemicals to my well water?

No. The process uses only clean water under high pressure. There are no chemicals, sand, or additives involved, so it poses no contamination risk to your water supply.

My faucets spit air and the water has sand in it. What does that mean?

Air spitting usually means the pump is drawing the well down below its intake, often from a falling water level. Sand points to a screen or formation issue common in alluvial desert wells. Both warrant a diagnostic; the right fix may be pump adjustment and well redevelopment rather than hydrofracturing.
