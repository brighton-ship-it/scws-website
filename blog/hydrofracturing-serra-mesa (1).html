What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Serra Mesa Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

When the Problem Is City Water, Not a Well

If you are on municipal service in Serra Mesa and you are experiencing low pressure, sputtering faucets, discolored water, or sudden flow loss, hydrofracturing has nothing to offer you. The likely causes are entirely different, and several are inexpensive to diagnose:

  • Pressure-regulator failure — the pressure-reducing valve where the city line enters your home can fail, causing pressure that is too high or too low. This is a plumbing repair, not a well repair.
  • Galvanized or corroded supply lines — older Serra Mesa homes built in the 1950s and 1960s sometimes still have aging galvanized piping that narrows with rust and chokes flow.
  • Aerator and fixture clogging — mineral buildup from San Diego's hard imported water collects in faucet aerators, showerheads, and valve screens.
  • Hard-water and taste concerns — imported Colorado River water is notably hard. A point-of-entry softener or a point-of-use filter often solves the complaint.

For households like these, our recommendation is straightforward: skip the well services entirely and address the plumbing or treatment issue. Where treatment is the right call, a sediment filtration setup typically runs $300 to $900, and an iron, manganese, or softening system runs roughly $1,500 to $3,500 installed. If you want better, more consistent pressure throughout a multi-story home, a booster or constant-pressure system runs about $2,000 to $4,500.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works (for the Few Who Have Wells)

For the rare Serra Mesa-area property that does have a private well—or for backcountry clients reading this—here is the mechanism. Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation technique for wells drilled into hard rock. A technician lowers an inflatable device called a packer into the well casing, seals off a target depth, and then injects clean water at controlled high pressure, often in the range of 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure reopens and extends the natural hairline fractures in the surrounding bedrock and flushes out the fine clay, silt, and mineral scale that gradually plug those fractures over the years. When it works, groundwater finds new pathways into the borehole and yield improves. It uses only water; no chemicals are introduced, which is the key difference between water-well hydrofracturing and the oil-and-gas process that shares part of the name.

The critical caveat is geology. Hydrofracturing only helps wells set in fractured crystalline or hard sedimentary rock. It does nothing for a well finished in loose sand and gravel, and it does nothing at all for a home on municipal water. That is why an honest evaluation always begins with what your water source actually is.

What to Check Before You Call Anyone

A few minutes of investigation saves money and points you toward the right trade:

  • Confirm your source. Do you receive a monthly water bill from the City of San Diego? If so, you are on municipal water and this is a plumbing matter.
  • Look for a wellhead. A private well shows up as a casing pipe, a pressure tank, and an electrical control box, usually in a side yard or utility area.
  • Note the pattern. Pressure that is bad at every fixture all the time points to a regulator or main-line issue; pressure that fades only during heavy use on a well points to yield.
  • Check the simple stuff. Clean an aerator and a showerhead screen; if flow jumps, the problem was mineral buildup.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do homes in Serra Mesa use private wells?

Very few do. Serra Mesa is an established urban San Diego neighborhood served by City of San Diego municipal water. If you receive a city water bill, you are not on a well and hydrofracturing does not apply to you.

My Serra Mesa water pressure is low—will hydrofracturing fix it?

No. On city water, low pressure is a plumbing or regulator issue, not a well-yield issue. A pressure-reducing valve, corroded piping, or clogged aerators are the usual culprits, and the repairs are far less expensive than any well service.

What if my second property out in the county has a well?

Then hydrofracturing may help, provided the well is in fractured rock. We start with a $125 diagnostic and flow test and recommend treatment only when the geology and the numbers support it.

Is hydrofracturing safe for drinking-water wells?

Yes. The process uses only clean water under pressure—no chemicals—so it does not introduce contaminants into your aquifer or your supply.

How long does a hydrofracturing job take?

The high-pressure injection itself usually takes four to eight hours, with flow testing before and after. We verify the result and document the before-and-after yield.

What should a Serra Mesa homeowner do first?

Confirm whether you are on city water or a well. If it is city water, call a plumber or ask us for an honest referral. If it is a well, schedule a diagnostic so we can test yield before recommending anything.
