What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Oak Glen Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Increasing Well Yield in Oak Glen's Apple Country

Oak Glen sits at about 4,734 feet on the north-facing front of the San Bernardino Mountains, roughly fifteen miles east of San Bernardino and just above Yucaipa. It is famous for its century-old apple orchards — Los Rios Rancho, Oak Tree Village, and the family ranches along Oak Glen Road draw hundreds of thousands of visitors every fall. Behind that agritourism sits a real working landscape of orchards, pastures, and rural homes, and much of it depends on private wells drilled into the decomposed granite and fractured metamorphic bedrock of the mountain front. When an Oak Glen well can't keep pace with orchard irrigation, livestock, and household use, hydrofracturing is often the most affordable way to recover lost capacity without drilling a new bore.

Why Oak Glen's Setting Produces Fracture-Dependent Wells

The geology beneath Oak Glen is a transition zone where the granitic core of the San Bernardino Mountains meets older metamorphic rocks, all of it weathered at the surface into sandy decomposed granite and broken bedrock below. Water does not flow through this rock the way it flows through valley sand and gravel; it travels along fractures, joints, and the contacts between rock types. A well's yield is set by how many of those water-bearing fractures it happens to cross. That is why one orchard's well can run strong while a neighbor's a quarter-mile away struggles. Hydrofracturing addresses this directly by reopening silted fractures and extending the well's reach into the surrounding fracture network.

How the Treatment Works

Using inflatable packers, we isolate a section of the open borehole and inject clean water — never chemicals — at pressures between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. The pressurized water flushes accumulated mineral scale and fine sediment out of existing fractures and propagates them deeper into the formation, commonly connecting the well to seams it never reached when first drilled. On Oak Glen's competent bedrock, those reopened fractures tend to hold and keep producing. A standard treatment takes four to eight hours, and we measure flow before and after so the improvement is documented.

The Demands of Orchard and Rural Properties

Oak Glen wells often carry an unusually heavy load. Apple trees need consistent irrigation through the growing season, frost protection can call for water on cold spring nights, and many properties also support livestock, gardens, and a household or two. A well that was adequate when the orchard was smaller — or before a property added a second home or a tasting room — can fall short as demand grows. Rather than the major expense of a new agricultural well, owners frequently find that fracturing the existing well lifts its yield enough to carry the full operation. Improving an existing bore also avoids the permitting and drilling timeline that can stall a working farm.

Signs Your Oak Glen Well May Benefit

  • Irrigation falls short: you can no longer run the orchard's watering system and the house at the same time.
  • Seasonal decline: the well produces in spring but drops off through the dry late summer and fall harvest.
  • Slow recovery: the well draws down fast and refills slowly after heavy use.
  • A disappointing new well: recently drilled into rock but never delivered the expected gallons per minute.
  • Growing demand: you've added trees, animals, or living space that the well can't keep up with.

What to Gather Before Your Assessment

If you can locate the well completion report, note the total depth, the casing depth, and the yield the driller recorded — these tell us a great deal about whether fracturing will help. Observe your system over a week of normal use: when does pressure fade, and how long until it recovers? Confirm the basics too, because a waterlogged pressure tank, a tired pump, or a failed foot valve can imitate a low-yield well. We rule those out during the visit so you never pay to fracture a well that only needed a pump.

When to Call a Licensed Contractor

Hydrofracturing involves high pressure and specialized down-hole equipment and is not a project to attempt yourself. We begin every job with a pre-treatment flow test and a downhole video inspection to verify that your casing is sound and to identify the fracture zones worth targeting. That inspection also catches problems — casing corrosion, a collapsed section, or an equipment fault — that need attention before any treatment. A professional assessment ensures you spend money on the fix that will actually solve your water problem.

Cost Compared to a New Well in Oak Glen

Drilling a new well in this hard-rock mountain front commonly runs $18,000 to $42,000 once depth and a pump are included, and agricultural wells can run higher. Hydrofracturing an existing well typically costs $3,000 to $8,000, which makes it the logical first step for most owners. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward the work if you move ahead. If your system also needs equipment, a submersible pump generally falls between $2,500 and $5,500 and a pressure tank between $600 and $1,500.

How Oak Glen's Elevation Shapes Its Groundwater

Perched on the cooler, wetter north slope of the range, Oak Glen receives more precipitation and even seasonal snow compared with the valley floor below it, and that moisture is exactly what makes apple growing possible here. But the steep, fractured terrain also sheds water quickly down toward Yucaipa and the San Timoteo badlands, so groundwater that isn't captured in the fracture system moves on. Wells that intersect a well-connected fracture network behave like a reservoir that refills each wet season; wells with only a few thin cracks drain down and recharge slowly. Hydrofracturing helps a marginal well capture more of the available recharge by widening its connection to the surrounding rock, which is especially valuable on a property that has to store water through the long dry summer harvest run-up.

A Common Oak Glen Scenario

A frequent situation we encounter is the multi-generational ranch where the original well was drilled decades ago for a modest household and a small stand of trees. Over time the family expands the orchard, adds a farm stand or tasting operation that draws fall crowds, and perhaps builds a second residence for the next generation. The well hasn't failed — it simply was never sized for today's demand. Before committing to the cost and downtime of drilling a new agricultural well, it makes sense to test whether the existing bore can be brought up to the task. In sound rock, fracturing frequently delivers the additional gallons per minute needed to keep both the orchard and the homes supplied.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hydrofracturing supply enough water for my orchard?

It often can, when the well is in sound fractured rock. Many Oak Glen owners see yield rise two to five times after treatment — frequently enough to cover irrigation plus household use. We measure the before-and-after flow so you can plan around the real numbers.

Will it use chemicals near my apple trees?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water. Nothing is introduced into the ground that could affect your orchard, soil, or crops.

How long does the improvement last?

With sound bedrock, gains commonly last many years. A well may eventually benefit from a second treatment, but most owners enjoy a long, stable run of improved flow.

Is the best time to do this between harvests?

Often, yes. Many growers schedule treatment outside the peak irrigation and harvest windows so the work doesn't interrupt operations, though we can usually accommodate any season.

How is this different from oil-and-gas fracking?

Completely different. Well hydrofracturing uses clean water at far lower pressures and volumes and targets a shallow domestic or agricultural borehole, with no chemicals or sand proppants.

What if my well isn't a candidate?

We'll say so honestly and point you toward the right solution. We don't recommend fracturing for wells in loose formations or with damaged casing.
