Well Pump Keeps Tripping Breaker? Here's What's Wrong

Your well pump breaker tripped again. You reset it, it runs for a few seconds—or maybe a few minutes—and click, it trips again. Why Circuit Breakers Trip: The Basics

Before diving into well pump-specific causes, it helps to understand why breakers trip in the first place. A circuit breaker is a safety device designed to interrupt electrical flow when it detects:

  • Overcurrent (overload): More amperage is being drawn than the circuit is rated for
  • Short circuit: Electricity is taking an unintended path, usually due to damaged wiring
  • Ground fault: Electricity is flowing to ground through an unintended path

When your well pump trips the breaker, one of these three conditions is occurring. Your job—and ours—is to figure out why.

The 7 Most Common Causes of Well Pump Breaker Tripping

Based on our 30+ years of experience servicing wells throughout San Diego County, here are the most common reasons well pump breakers trip—ranked from most to least frequent:

1

Failing or Seized Pump Motor

The most common cause of repeated breaker trips is a pump motor that's dying. As motors age or develop problems, they draw more electrical current than they should. When the amperage exceeds what the breaker is rated for, it trips.

Signs of Motor Failure:

  • Breaker trips instantly when pump tries to start
  • Pump worked fine yesterday, now nothing
  • Pump is 10-15+ years old
  • History of running dry or short-cycling

The Fix:

A seized or failed motor requires pump replacement. This is a major repair but often the most reliable long-term solution. Modern pumps are more efficient and reliable than older models.

2

Water Infiltration in the Motor

Submersible pump motors are designed to operate underwater, but they're sealed to keep water out of the electrical components. When seals fail, water enters the motor housing and causes short circuits—which trip the breaker instantly.

Signs of Water Infiltration:

  • Breaker trips immediately upon starting
  • May work briefly when pump is cool
  • Megger test shows very low insulation resistance
  • Often occurs after many years of service

The Fix:

Once water enters the motor, it cannot be repaired—the pump must be replaced. Learn more about signs your pump is failing.

3

Damaged or Deteriorated Wiring

The wiring between your electrical panel and the pump can become damaged over time. This is especially common in San Diego County where we see damage from rodents, UV exposure on above-ground wiring, and corrosion from our mineral-rich soil.

Signs of Wiring Problems:

  • Breaker trips intermittently (not every time)
  • Visible damage to wires at wellhead
  • Burnt smell near control box or wellhead
  • Signs of rodent activity near wiring

The Fix:

Wiring repairs range from simple fixes (surface wire replacement) to complex ones (pulling the pump to replace the drop cable). A professional can diagnose the location and extent of damage.

4

Faulty Capacitor or Control Box

The control box manages your submersible pump's operation, and the capacitor provides the extra boost needed to start the motor. When these components fail, they can cause the motor to draw excessive starting current.

Signs of Control Box/Capacitor Failure:

  • Pump hums but doesn't start, then trips breaker
  • Burnt or swollen capacitor visible
  • Control box is hot to the touch
  • Recent lightning strike or power surge

The Fix:

Capacitor replacement costs $100-$200. Control box replacement costs $250-$450. These are relatively inexpensive compared to full pump replacement.

5

Pump Running Dry (Overheating)

When a well's water level drops below the pump, the pump runs "dry." Without water to cool it, the motor overheats rapidly, draws excessive current, and trips the breaker. This is especially common during San Diego's drought periods.

Signs of Running Dry:

  • Water sputtered before stopping completely
  • Problem started during high-usage period
  • Neighbors' wells also having issues
  • Pump runs for a while, then trips

The Fix:

Wait for the well to recover, then test again. Long-term solutions include well deepening, installing a low-water cutoff switch, or drilling a new well.

6

Bound or Stuck Pump

Sometimes the pump itself becomes mechanically stuck. This can happen when sand or sediment gets into the impellers, when mineral buildup jams the pump, or when the pump shifts in the casing. A stuck pump draws maximum current trying to start, instantly tripping the breaker.

Signs of a Bound Pump:

  • History of sandy or silty water
  • Pump trips instantly without any motor sound
  • Very high amp draw when tested
  • Well produces hard water with scale buildup

The Fix:

The pump must be pulled and inspected. Sometimes it can be cleaned and reinstalled; often it needs replacement. Consider adding a sediment filter to prevent recurrence.

7

Undersized or Faulty Breaker

Less common but worth checking: the breaker itself might be the problem. Breakers wear out over time and can begin tripping at lower amperages than their rating. Additionally, if someone installed the wrong size breaker, it may trip during normal pump operation.

Signs of Breaker Problems:

  • Breaker is warm or hot to the touch
  • Breaker is old (15+ years)
  • Problem started after electrical work
  • Breaker size doesn't match pump requirements

The Fix:

A licensed electrician can test the breaker and replace it if needed. However, always verify the pump system is healthy first—don't just upsize the breaker to mask a real problem.

What You Can Safely Check Yourself

Before calling for service, there are a few things you can safely inspect. However, if you're uncomfortable with electrical systems, skip these steps and call a professional—it's not worth the risk.

⚡ Safety Warning

Well pump systems operate on 240 volts—enough to cause serious injury or death. Always turn off the breaker before inspecting any electrical components. If you're unsure, call a professional.

Safe DIY Checks

  1. Check the breaker itself: Is it the correct size for your pump (check pump nameplate)? Is it hot to the touch? Is it properly seated in the panel?
  2. Visual wiring inspection: With the breaker OFF, look at the wiring from the panel to the wellhead. Look for visible damage, burnt areas, or rodent chews.
  3. Control box inspection: With the breaker OFF, open the control box (usually at the wellhead). Look for burnt components, swollen capacitors, or signs of moisture.
  4. Check water level: If you have a way to measure your well's water level (sounding tube or water level meter), check if the level is unusually low.
  5. Recent events: Think about what happened before the problem started. Lightning strike? Power outage? Running water for an extended time?

How Professionals Diagnose Breaker Tripping

When you call a licensed well contractor, here's how we diagnose a tripping breaker problem:

1

Visual and Electrical Panel Inspection

We verify breaker size, check for signs of overheating, and ensure proper installation.

2

Amp Draw Test

Using a clamp-on ammeter, we measure how much current the pump draws at startup and during operation. This tells us if the motor is working too hard.

3

Megger Test (Insulation Resistance)

This specialized test sends a high voltage through the motor windings to check insulation integrity. Low readings indicate water infiltration or insulation breakdown.

4

Control Box and Capacitor Testing

We test the capacitor's capacitance and check the relay contacts in the control box for proper operation.

5

Wire and Connection Inspection

We check all connections for tightness and corrosion, and inspect visible wiring for damage.

Based on these tests, we can pinpoint the problem and provide you with repair options—before you commit to any work. Learn more about what to expect during a professional well inspection.

Preventing Future Breaker Trips

Once you've fixed the immediate problem, take these steps to prevent future electrical issues with your well pump:

Install Surge Protection

Lightning and power surges damage more well pumps than almost anything else. A dedicated surge protector ($150-$300) can save you thousands in repairs.

Schedule Annual Maintenance

Regular well maintenance catches problems early—before they cause breaker trips or complete failure.

Prevent Dry Running

Install a low-water cutoff switch to protect the pump if water levels drop. This is especially important during drought conditions.

Address Pressure Tank Issues

A failing pressure tank causes short-cycling, which stresses the pump motor and can lead to breaker trips.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my well pump keep tripping the breaker?

The most common causes include a failing or seized pump motor, water infiltration into the motor, damaged wiring, a faulty capacitor or control box, the pump running dry, a mechanically stuck pump, or a faulty breaker. Each of these causes the pump to draw more current than the breaker's rating allows.

Is it safe to keep resetting a breaker that keeps tripping?

No—this is dangerous and can cause electrical fires. If the breaker trips more than once, leave it off and call a professional. The breaker is protecting your home from an electrical fault.

How do I know if my well pump motor is bad?

Signs include repeated breaker trips, a humming sound without water flow, reduced water output, higher electricity bills, and the motor running hot. A professional can perform a megger test to confirm motor failure.

Can a bad pressure switch cause the breaker to trip?

Yes, indirectly. If the switch is stuck closed, the pump runs continuously until it overheats. Arcing from burnt contacts or insects inside the switch can also cause electrical problems.

What size breaker should a well pump have?

It depends on pump horsepower: 1/2 HP uses 15-20 amps, 3/4 HP uses 20 amps, 1 HP uses 20-30 amps, 1.5 HP uses 30-40 amps, 2 HP uses 40 amps. Always use the manufacturer's specified breaker size.

How much does it cost to fix a well pump that keeps tripping the breaker?

Costs vary by cause: capacitor replacement $100-$200, control box $250-$450, wiring repair $200-$500, and complete pump replacement $1,500-$3,500+. A diagnostic call ($75-$150) identifies the exact problem first.

Can a well pump trip a breaker during a power surge?

Yes, power surges from lightning or utility problems can trip the breaker. If it trips once during a storm, you can try resetting after the storm passes. If it trips again, the surge may have damaged components.

Should I call an electrician or a well service company for a tripping breaker?

Call a well service company first. We specialize in well pump electrical systems and have the equipment to diagnose problems underground. If the issue is in your main panel, we'll advise you to call an electrician.
