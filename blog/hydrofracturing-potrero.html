What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Potrero Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a focused well-rehabilitation method, not the chemical-heavy oil-and-gas process people sometimes confuse it with. A well slows down over the years because the natural seams and fractures in the surrounding rock — the very channels that feed groundwater to the borehole — gradually clog with fine sediment and mineral scale, or were only partially open to begin with. The goal is simply to reopen and flush those existing pathways so water can move freely again.

To do that, our crew lowers an inflatable packer into the casing and inflates it to seal off the section of the well we want to treat. Clean water is then injected into that isolated zone at pressures that typically run from about 1,500 PSI up past 3,000 PSI. That pressure pushes into the granite, dislodges the debris choking the fractures, and extends the connected network of seams a little farther out into the rock. The result is more surface area feeding your well and a faster recharge rate.

What goes into the ground is nothing but water — no chemicals, no additives, no proppants. That distinction matters to Potrero families who depend on their well for drinking water. Most jobs are finished in a single visit lasting roughly four to eight hours, including the time to set up, treat each zone, and develop the well before we pack out.

Signs a Potrero Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Well decline tends to creep up gradually, so it helps to know the warning signs before you are hauling water in. Around Potrero, the patterns we hear about most often include:

  • Yield that has fallen off over the years — the well that once kept up with the house and the livestock now struggles to fill the tank.
  • Slow recovery after the pump runs — you draw the well down doing laundry or irrigating, and it takes hours to build back.
  • Running low in dry or drought months — the well coasts through winter but can't keep pace through a hot, rainless Potrero summer.
  • A newer well that simply under-produces — it was drilled into solid rock but never delivered the flow you expected.

If one or more of these sounds familiar, your well is a candidate worth evaluating rather than abandoning.

Why Local Geology Matters

The single biggest factor in whether hydrofracturing will help is what your well is drilled into, and Potrero's geology happens to be close to ideal. The Peninsular Ranges here are built from granitic and crystalline bedrock — hard rock laced with natural joints and fractures, often capped by a layer of weathered, decomposed granite. Groundwater in this setting doesn't sit in a uniform sand layer; it travels through that fracture network. When the fractures are partly blocked or sparsely connected, the well underperforms, and high-pressure water is exactly the tool to reopen them.

The flip side is just as important to be honest about: wells set in loose sand, gravel, or other unconsolidated material are not good hydrofracturing candidates. There are no rigid fractures to reopen in that kind of ground, so the pressure has nowhere useful to go. Most Potrero-area wells, fortunately, are seated in the fractured granite that responds best — but the only way to be sure about your specific well is to assess it first.

Safety First: What to Check Before Hydrofracturing

Not every drop in water pressure means the well itself has failed. Plenty of "low water" calls around Potrero turn out to be equipment problems that are far cheaper to fix. Before recommending hydrofracturing, we rule out the common culprits:

  • A failing or worn-out submersible pump that can no longer move water the way it should.
  • A waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air charge and keeps the pump cycling.
  • A bad or miscalibrated pressure switch sending the wrong signals to the pump.
  • A leak somewhere in the supply or distribution line bleeding off pressure.

That is why we begin with a $125 diagnostic flow test. We measure your actual yield, check the pump and tank, and pin down whether the problem is the well or the hardware attached to it. If you decide to move forward with treatment, that $125 is credited toward the work — so the diagnosis effectively pays for itself, and you never spend money fracturing a well that just needed a new pressure switch.
