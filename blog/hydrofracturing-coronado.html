What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Coronado Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Wells in the Coronado Area

Coronado is a coastal community on San Diego Bay in San Diego County, connected to the mainland by the iconic bridge and the Silver Strand. It is one of the least likely places in the region to find a working private well: the city is built on a low, sandy bay-side peninsula, sits almost entirely on imported and municipal water, and has no foothill bedrock aquifer of the kind that supplies wells across inland San Diego County. That said, well water is part of the broader San Diego County picture, and Coronado-area property owners do occasionally have reasons to understand hydrofracturing (also written hydrofracking): a family member or investment property on the inland fringes of the county, a legacy or irrigation well on an older estate parcel, or planning around a property elsewhere in the region. How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation method that uses clean, high-pressure water—and nothing else—to restore a well's output. Despite the name, it is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking: there are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no additives involved.

The process is straightforward. A crew lowers inflatable packers into the well casing and inflates them to seal off a specific section of the borehole. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated interval at pressures typically between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. The pressure drives water out into the natural fractures and water-bearing seams around the well, flushing out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have plugged them and widening the passages so groundwater can flow back into the well. The well is treated one zone at a time, and a flow test afterward measures how much the yield improved.

This technique is most effective in fractured granite and crystalline bedrock, the formation that supplies the great majority of San Diego County's hard-rock wells—found inland and in the foothills, not on the coastal flats of Coronado itself. A hard-rock well produces only as much water as its fracture network can deliver, so reopening clogged fractures is exactly what high-pressure water is suited to do.

It is worth understanding this distinction, because it explains why a coastal location like Coronado is fundamentally different from the well country east of the city. Wells thrive where there is fractured rock or a productive sediment basin to draw from; a sandy bay-side peninsula has neither in a form that supports private water supply. When people in the Coronado area do own wells, those wells are almost always on other property they hold inland, which is exactly where our work is concentrated.

Why a Well Loses Yield

For the legacy and irrigation wells that exist elsewhere in San Diego County, declining yield usually comes down to a few causes:

  • Fractures sealed by mineral scale. Dissolved minerals precipitate inside rock fractures and on the screen, gradually closing the pathways that feed the well.
  • Sediment and fines packing the well. Fine material works into the joints and gravel around the borehole and chokes flow.
  • Marginal fracture connectivity. Some hard-rock wells were always weak producers because they intersected only a few water-bearing fractures.
  • Drought-driven water-table decline. Long dry stretches can lower groundwater to where a shallow well struggles to keep up.
  • Decades without rehabilitation. Many older wells have simply never been cleaned or redeveloped.

Hydrofracturing directly addresses the first three. Where the real issue is a falling water table, an honest contractor will say so and discuss deepening or other options rather than push a treatment that will not last.

Signs a Well May Need Hydrofracturing

If you own or are evaluating a well anywhere in the county, these are the warning signs to watch for:

  • The well produces clearly less water than it did a few years ago.
  • It draws down fast and the pump short-cycles or runs dry during heavy use.
  • Water pressure to the property has fallen off.
  • Recovery after pumping is slow—the well takes much longer to refill.
  • You notice more grit, sand, or cloudiness in the water.
  • A newly drilled well came in weaker than expected.

What to Check Before You Call

A little information makes diagnosis faster. Note when the decline began and whether it follows the seasons. Watch for pump short-cycling. Locate any records of the well's depth, original yield, or completion report. And be ready to describe the property's water demand, since household use and irrigation place very different loads on a well. The more history you can share about how the well has performed over recent seasons, the more precisely we can target a treatment and the faster we can restore reliable flow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there private wells in Coronado?

Very few, if any, active drinking-water wells. Coronado sits on a sandy coastal peninsula and is served by municipal and imported water, without the bedrock aquifer that supplies inland wells. We are candid that most Coronado owners will not need well service, and we serve those who own wells elsewhere in San Diego County.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. It uses only clean, pressurized water. There are no chemicals, proppants, or additives. It is a safe, well-established method for rehabilitating water wells.

Where does hydrofracturing work best?

In fractured granite and crystalline bedrock, which is the formation behind most San Diego County hard-rock wells, found inland rather than on the coast. We confirm with a flow test and assessment before recommending it.

How much improvement can I expect?

Results vary by well, but many wells see a meaningful increase in flow once the plugged fractures are reopened. We document before-and-after yield with flow tests.

How long does it take?

A typical job takes about four to eight hours on site, and we usually confirm the improved yield with a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing typically costs $3,000–$8,000 versus $18,000–$42,000 for a new turnkey well, so it is the smart first step before considering replacement.
