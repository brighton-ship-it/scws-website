What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Sorrento Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Sorrento Valley: An Honest Read on a Tech-Corridor Neighborhood

It is firmly within the City of San Diego's municipal water system. For a place like this, the candid answer about hydrofracturing is that it has essentially no application to local properties, and the most useful thing we can do is explain why and point you toward what does help.

What Hydrofracturing Is

Hydrofracturing, also called hydrofracking in the water-well trade, increases the yield of a low-producing well drilled into hard, fractured crystalline bedrock, the granitic rock common in inland and mountain San Diego County. Such a well takes water in only through natural cracks. The technique injects clean water at high pressure, usually 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, into a sealed-off section of the borehole, clearing sediment and mineral scale from the existing fractures and opening new ones so the well connects to more water-bearing rock. It uses only water, involves no chemicals, and is completed in a single day inside the existing casing.

Why It Does Not Fit Sorrento Valley

Several local realities rule it out for properties here:

  • Municipal water. The office campuses and homes of Sorrento Valley are on the City of San Diego system, so there are essentially no private wells to rehabilitate.
  • Valley and lagoon sediments. The low ground along the corridor and near Los Penasquitos Lagoon consists of alluvial and lagoon deposits, sand, silt, and clay, not the fractured granite hydrofracturing requires.
  • Salt and brackish influence near the lagoon. Shallow groundwater toward the coastal lagoon is salt-influenced and unsuitable for domestic supply.

If a property in Sorrento Valley has a water-pressure or supply concern, the cause is in the building's plumbing or the municipal system, not in any aquifer. A licensed plumber or a call to the City is the correct, faster, and cheaper route.

Where Hydrofracturing Genuinely Helps

Move inland and up into the granitic foothills and backcountry of San Diego County, toward Poway's rural edges, Ramona, and beyond, and hydrofracturing becomes a powerful, economical option. Many rural homes there rely on bedrock wells, and the technique can revive a fading one for a fraction of the cost of a new well. Consider it when:

  • The well is drilled into granite or other hard crystalline rock.
  • Yield has declined from the original driller's report.
  • A newly drilled well came in disappointingly low.
  • The well recovers slowly after pumping or runs short in summer.
  • You want a lower-cost rehabilitation before drilling new.

Why a Bedrock Well Loses Yield

A granite well fades for understandable reasons. When first drilled, its fractures are open and productive. Over the years, fine silt migrates in, dissolved iron and calcium harden into scale, and biological growth films the rock face. The decline is gradual, so the owner notices only that the well no longer keeps up during heavy use. Hydrofracturing drives water through that fracture network under pressure, flushing out the buildup and reopening the connection to the surrounding rock. Because the mechanism depends on rigid, cracked rock, it accomplishes nothing in the sand, silt, and clay beneath Sorrento Valley.

The Process, Step by Step

  1. Diagnostic and flow test. We confirm the well is in suitable bedrock and record baseline yield. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward the job if you proceed.
  2. Pump removal. The pump and piping are pulled to open the borehole.
  3. Camera inspection. A downhole video checks casing and locates fracture zones.
  4. Packer isolation. An inflatable packer concentrates pressure on the target zone.
  5. High-pressure injection. Clean water at 1,000 to 3,000 PSI clears and opens fractures, zone by zone.
  6. Redevelopment and re-test. The well is flushed, the pump reset, and a fresh flow test documents the gain.

On-site work usually runs four to eight hours.

Realistic Expectations

In suitable bedrock wells, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, often two to five times the prior flow. It cannot create water in dry rock, and a reputable contractor will say so before quoting. Outcomes depend on the fracture network the borehole intersects.

Comparing the Options

For the inland owner with a tired bedrock well, the real choices are deepening, drilling new, or hydrofracturing. A new well in San Diego County typically runs $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey; deepening is priced by the foot with no guarantee; hydrofracturing, at $3,000 to $8,000, is the natural first experiment because it often doubles or triples flow at a small fraction of the new-well cost. For a Sorrento Valley property on city water, the relevant comparison instead is between accepting the municipal supply and adding a treatment system for quality, not between well techniques at all.

The Right Help for a Commercial or Canyon-Edge Property

Sorrento Valley is unusual in San Diego for mixing dense commercial campuses with canyon and lagoon open space, and property managers here sometimes ask about wells for irrigation or resilience. Even in that context, the underlying geology and the salt-influenced lagoon groundwater make a productive private well impractical along the corridor. For landscape irrigation, recycled or reclaimed water programs and efficient municipal-fed systems are the realistic routes. For water quality at the tap, point-of-use or building-scale treatment handles hardness and taste. And for any pressure or supply question, the building plumber and the City of San Diego are the right first calls. We would rather direct you to those resources than quote a well treatment that the ground will not support.

Maintenance Matters More Than Any One Technique

For owners who do have bedrock wells out in the county, it is worth remembering that hydrofracturing is one tool in a larger maintenance picture. A well that is pumped sensibly, fitted with a correctly sized pump set at the right depth, and inspected periodically will hold its yield far longer than one that is run hard and ignored. When decline does set in, a camera inspection and flow test tell us whether the answer is hydrofracturing, a screen or casing repair, a pump change, or deepening. Matching the remedy to the actual cause is what keeps a rural water system reliable and affordable over the decades.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Do you have a private well? In Sorrento Valley, essentially no.
  • What does the well log say? Depth, casing, and formation tell us whether hydrofrac applies.
  • Has the pump been ruled out? A failing pump or pressure switch mimics low yield cheaply.
  • Is the casing sound? Cracked casing must be repaired before pressure work.
