Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in Riverside County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Lake Matthews well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well Water Around Lake Matthews: A Realistic Local Guide

Lake Matthews is a large reservoir in the rolling hills southwest of the city of Riverside, in Riverside County. The lake itself is a protected drinking-water reservoir — part of the Colorado River Aqueduct system managed by the Metropolitan Water District — so it isn't open for recreation, but the surrounding countryside is classic Inland Empire ranch land. The Lake Matthews and neighboring Gavilan Hills, El Sobrante, and Mead Valley areas are dotted with horse properties, large rural lots, hobby orchards, and semi-rural homes, many of them on private wells. This is warmer, lower country than the mountains, and the inland Riverside region does include genuine citrus and avocado heritage on its frost-protected hillsides. Whether avocados make sense on a given Lake Matthews parcel depends on the specific site — its elevation, frost exposure, and above all its water supply. So an honest local guide here covers rural household and irrigation wells, the granite-and-alluvium geology of the hills, and the water realities of growing anything in a hot, dry inland climate.

For the many Lake Matthews-area owners running a private well, the day-to-day priorities are dependable household supply, enough flow for livestock and landscape, and water clean enough not to damage equipment or salt-sensitive plantings. For those tending citrus or avocado on a favorable parcel, water quality and reliability are everything.

How Wells Work in the Lake Matthews Hills

The terrain around Lake Matthews is a mix of granite and fractured bedrock in the hills and alluvial fill in the valleys and drainages. That dual geology means wells behave differently depending on where they sit. A well drilled into valley alluvium may tap a more continuous water-bearing layer and yield moderately well, while a well in the hard granite hills depends on intersecting water-bearing fractures — and yield can vary dramatically from one lot to the next. A bore that finds a productive fracture at 200 feet might sit near one that needed 500 feet to reach usable water.

Because many hill wells produce a steady but modest flow, the proven design pairs the well with a storage tank and a booster pump: the well fills the tank around the clock, and the booster delivers strong, even pressure to the house, the barn, and the irrigation system on demand. This lets a rural property run reliably even when the well's raw yield is limited — a common and sensible setup across the Gavilan Hills.

Common Local Well Scenarios

  • Drought-driven decline. Inland Riverside's dry cycles lower water tables; a well that performed well in wet years can fall short after several dry winters.
  • Hard, mineral-rich water. Local groundwater tends to be hard, scaling pipes, water heaters, and drip emitters, and on salt-sensitive crops it can scorch leaves.
  • Granite sediment and sand. Decomposed granite and alluvial sand wear pump impellers and clog screens and filters.
  • Iron and sulfur. Some area wells show iron staining or a sulfur odor that calls for targeted treatment.
  • Heat-stressed equipment. Triple-digit summer heat punishes pumps, controls, and pressure tanks just as irrigation demand peaks.
  • Long runs and elevation. Large rural lots mean long pipe runs and real elevation changes that can sap pressure if the system isn't sized for them.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Read the pressure gauge. Normal is roughly 40-60 psi; a zero reading usually points to the pump, the switch, or a tank that's lost its air charge.
  2. Check the breaker and pump power. A tripped breaker is common, but repeated tripping means stop and call a professional.
  3. Watch for short-cycling. A pump rapidly clicking on and off usually signals a waterlogged pressure tank.
  4. Look at the water. Sand, rust staining, or a sulfur smell helps a technician pinpoint the right treatment.
  5. Walk for leaks. On large lots, a buried leak can quietly bleed pressure and waste a lot of water.

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Call a C-57 contractor when the pump won't hold pressure, when the well runs dry under normal use, when water quality shifts, or when you're planning a new well, a treatment system, or an irrigation expansion for an orchard, pasture, or large landscape. A licensed professional can flow-test the well, camera-inspect the casing, and measure water levels and chemistry — critical in country where conditions change between the alluvial valleys and the granite hills. If you're irrigating avocado or citrus on a favorable parcel, water testing is the first step in protecting your trees from salt and scale.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you grow avocados near Lake Matthews?

On the right parcel, possibly. The inland Riverside hills have citrus and avocado heritage on frost-protected sites, but success depends on elevation, frost exposure, and a reliable, low-salinity water supply. It's site-specific, not a given across the whole area.

How deep are wells around Lake Matthews?

It depends on the geology of the lot. Valley alluvial wells and granite-hill wells behave differently, and depths range widely. A site evaluation is the only reliable guide.

Why is my well water hard or staining?

Local groundwater is often mineral-rich, and some wells carry iron or sulfur. Softeners and iron/manganese filtration address these issues.

Why did my well lose capacity after dry years?

Drought lowers the water table, so the pump may no longer sit deep enough or the aquifer yields less. A flow test and water-level check reveal the cause.

Can my modest hill well support a house, animals, and irrigation?

Usually yes, when paired with a storage tank and booster pump that turn a steady-but-modest yield into reliable on-demand pressure.
