Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Why Upland Wells Lose Their Flow at the Base of the San Gabriels

Upland sits right where the San Gabriel Mountains meet the northern edge of the upper Santa Ana Valley, and that location shapes almost everything about how a private well behaves here. Water that falls as snow and rain on the mountains north of Foothill Boulevard percolates down through Cucamonga Canyon and the alluvial fans that fan out below it, then collects in the Chino groundwater subbasin, which is bounded on the north by the Cucamonga Fault and the hard, impermeable rock of the San Gabriels. When a home on the north side of town starts pushing weak, sputtering flow at the tap, the cause almost always traces back to that geology: a well set high in coarse fan gravels near the mountain front can be very sensitive to how much water the aquifer is holding in any given year.

In an average year that setup works beautifully. But San Bernardino County has run through repeated multi-year droughts, and when regional recharge drops, the static water level in an Upland well can fall several feet or more. If the pump was originally set only a little below the historic water table, that decline is enough to leave the intake gasping for water part of the day. The result is what our customers describe as a well that "runs strong in the morning and quits by evening" or a pump that short-cycles and never quite keeps the pressure tank full.

What "Low Flow" Actually Means for an Upland Household

It helps to separate two different problems that both feel like low flow. The first is low yield: the aquifer simply cannot deliver water to the well fast enough. The second is a delivery problem: the aquifer has water, but something between the formation and your faucet is choking it off. On Upland properties we see both, and telling them apart is the whole point of a proper diagnosis.

Common culprits behind genuinely low delivery include:

  • A pump set too high in the casing. If the water table has dropped since the well was drilled, the intake may now sit near or above the seasonal low, so the pump breaks suction as the level draws down.
  • An undersized or worn submersible pump. Impellers wear out, especially in the sandy water common to the fan deposits below the foothills. A tired pump moves a fraction of its rated gallons per minute.
  • A clogged well screen or perforated casing. Upland's water is notably hard and mineral-rich. Over years, calcium and iron scale plus iron-bacteria slime can plaster the screen shut, strangling the flow even though the aquifer is full.
  • Silted-in or partially collapsed casing. Fine sand from the alluvial fans settles in the bottom of the well, burying the lowest and most productive perforations.

For reference, a single-family Upland home usually needs a steady 3-5 GPM; a larger household with several bathrooms or landscape irrigation wants 6-12 GPM. Many foothill wells here naturally produce in the low single digits, which is workable with the right storage and pump strategy but leaves little margin when something starts to clog.

A Typical Upland Scenario

Picture a half-acre property up near San Antonio Heights. The well was drilled decades ago into fractured rock and fan gravel, and for years it delivered plenty. Lately the homeowner notices the shower loses pressure whenever the washing machine runs, and by late afternoon the kitchen faucet barely dribbles. A neighbor blames "the drought," but when we measure the well we find the static water level is only modestly lower than historic norms — the real issue is a screen half-choked with mineral scale combined with a pump whose impellers have worn thin after fifteen years in gritty water. Neither problem alone would have crossed the line, but together they dropped effective flow below what the family uses at peak. That is the classic Upland story: it is rarely one thing.

How We Diagnose a Low-Flow Well

Guessing is expensive. Before we recommend anything, our technicians run a structured evaluation:

  1. Measure the static water level — the resting level with the pump off — to see how much water column the well actually holds.
  2. Run a drawdown and recovery test. We pump the well and watch how fast the level falls and how quickly it recovers. A well that draws down hard and recovers slowly is telling us the aquifer or the screen is the bottleneck.
  3. Perform a yield (GPM) test to establish the true sustainable production rate, not the peak-burst rate.
  4. Check pump depth, age, and condition, and inspect the pressure switch, pressure tank, and control box for faults that mimic low flow.

Only after those numbers are in hand do we talk about fixes. That discipline is why we can tell a customer "your pump is fine, your screen is fouled" instead of selling an unnecessary new pump.

Fixes That Make Sense in Upland

The right repair depends entirely on what the diagnosis reveals:

  • Lower or replace the pump. If the water table has dropped, resetting the pump deeper in the casing — or swapping a worn unit for a correctly sized submersible — often restores flow immediately. A submersible replacement typically runs $2,500-$5,500 depending on depth and horsepower.
  • Rehabilitate the well. When scale and biofilm have sealed the screen, mechanical brushing followed by an acid or chemical treatment can reopen the perforations and dramatically improve intake. This is frequently the best value on an older Upland well.
  • Add storage and a booster. For a naturally low-yield well, a storage or cistern tank that fills slowly around the clock, paired with a booster or constant-pressure system ($2,000-$4,500), lets a 2-3 GPM well comfortably serve a busy household.
  • Deepen or replace the well. If the formation itself is played out, deepening to reach a more productive zone — or a turnkey new well ($18,000-$42,000) — may be the long-term answer. We treat this as a last resort, after cheaper options are ruled out.

Supporting components matter too: a failing pressure switch ($150-$350) or a waterlogged pressure tank ($600-$1,500) can masquerade as low flow, and hard-water treatment such as a softener ($1,500-$3,500) or sediment filtration ($300-$900) protects whatever pump you install from the mineral load that fouled the last one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Upland well flow well in the morning but weakly at night?

That daily pattern almost always means the well draws down faster than the aquifer refills. Overnight the water level recovers, so morning flow is strong; heavy daytime use pulls the level below the pump intake by evening. A drawdown test confirms it, and storage plus a booster, or resetting the pump, usually solves it.

Is my low flow caused by the drought or by my equipment?

Often it is a mix of both. Drought lowers the regional water table, but the tipping point is frequently a worn pump or a scaled-over screen. We measure the static level and run a yield test to separate the two, so you only pay to fix the real problem.

Can a fouled screen really be cleaned, or do I need a new well?

In most cases it can be cleaned. Upland's hard water builds mineral scale and iron-bacteria biofilm on the screen, and mechanical brushing with a chemical or acid treatment reopens those perforations. Well rehabilitation is far cheaper than a new well and is usually worth trying first.

How much water should my Upland home actually need?

A typical single-family home needs about 3-5 GPM of sustained supply; homes with multiple bathrooms or irrigation want 6-12 GPM. If your well produces less, a storage tank and constant-pressure booster can multiply your effective delivery.

Will a bigger pump fix a low-yield well?

Not by itself. If the aquifer cannot deliver water fast enough, an oversized pump will just draw the well down and break suction, sometimes damaging the pump. The right answer for a genuinely low-yield well is storage plus a properly matched booster, not simply more horsepower.
