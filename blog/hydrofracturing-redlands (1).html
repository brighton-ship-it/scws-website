What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Redlands Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Redlands: A Realistic Look at Your Options

Redlands sits at the eastern edge of the San Bernardino Valley in San Bernardino County, a historic citrus city framed by the San Bernardino Mountains to the north, the Crafton Hills and Badlands to the south, and the Santa Ana River drainage running through the valley below. If you are researching hydrofracturing for a Redlands well, the honest starting point is this: across most of the city, hydrofracturing is usually not the right answer—and we would rather explain why than sell you a service the local geology cannot support.

The reason comes down to what lies beneath Redlands. The city draws roughly half its water from the Bunker Hill Groundwater Basin, a vast alluvial reservoir at the top of the Santa Ana River watershed. Large alluvial fans of sand, gravel, silt, and clay underlie much of the Redlands area. Hydrofracturing works by reopening cracks in hard bedrock—but there is no fractured bedrock to treat in an alluvial basin like Bunker Hill. Water already moves through the open spaces between sand and gravel grains, so there is nothing to "fracture." At Southern California Well Service, with 30-plus years working basins like this, we almost always recommend other, more effective solutions for Redlands properties.

Why Hydrofracturing Rarely Suits Bunker Hill Basin Wells

Think of the Bunker Hill Basin as a giant natural storage tank—by some estimates it holds around 5 million acre-feet of water, with more than a million acre-feet readily accessible. It is actively recharged: the San Bernardino Valley Water Conservation District diverts stormflows from the Santa Ana River and Mill Creek through dozens of percolation basins, letting water seep naturally into the aquifer. In that kind of sand-and-gravel system, a private well that underperforms is almost never suffering from clogged fractures, because there are no fractures involved. The real culprit is usually a fouled or sanded-in well screen, a pump set too high after years of changing water levels, mineral encrustation, or simply an aging well at the end of its service life.

Most homes within the City of Redlands are also served by municipal water rather than private wells. If your water comes from the city, a pressure or quality complaint is a plumbing or utility matter—not a well issue at all. Where private or agricultural wells do exist, especially on the older citrus parcels and the city's outskirts, the right repairs are the alternatives described below.

This is not a criticism of hydrofracturing itself—in the right ground it is a powerful, cost-saving technique. We use it regularly in the fractured-granite foothills of the San Diego County backcountry near our Ramona and Anza offices. But the technique only works where there is solid, jointed rock to open. Redlands and the broader San Bernardino Valley floor are the textbook case where it does not apply, and a contractor who proposes it for a valley alluvial well is either misreading the geology or overselling. Knowing this up front can save you thousands of dollars.

What Actually Helps a Redlands Well

  • Lowering the pump or deepening the well. As water levels in the basin shift with drought and recharge cycles, a pump can end up set too shallow. Resetting it deeper, or deepening the borehole, is often the most effective single fix.
  • Well rehabilitation. In alluvial wells, the screen clogs with fine sediment and mineral scale. Brushing, surging, and jetting the screen restores flow—this is the alluvial equivalent of "cleaning out" a well.
  • A new, properly designed well. When an old well is collapsed, sanded-in, or screened at the wrong depth, a new turnkey well engineered for current basin conditions is the durable solution.
  • Water treatment. Santa Ana watershed groundwater can carry hardness, iron, manganese, and sediment. Filtration and softening solve quality issues that no well-fracturing could ever address.

Common Redlands Water Scenarios

After years of service calls across the San Bernardino Valley, the underlying causes of "my well is failing" tend to repeat. Identifying yours helps you avoid paying for the wrong repair:

  • Sandy or gritty water: Almost always a screen or gravel-pack problem—addressed with rehabilitation or a new screen, plus sediment filtration ($300–$900).
  • Pressure slowly falling over years: Often a shifting water table leaving the pump too shallow; deepening or resetting the pump solves it.
  • Pump short-cycling: Frequently a waterlogged pressure tank ($600–$1,500) or a failing pressure switch ($150–$350), not an aquifer problem.
  • Hard, scaly, or stained water: A treatment matter—an iron/manganese filter or softener ($1,500–$3,500) is the correct tool.
  • City-water home with low pressure: A plumbing or utility issue; no well work applies.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of preparation lets us steer you toward the least expensive effective fix, sometimes before we ever set foot on your property:

  • Whether you are on a private well or city water. A City of Redlands water bill means municipal supply; a wellhead, pump, and pressure tank mean a private well.
  • Your well construction report. Screen depth and aquifer type confirm you are in alluvium (the Redlands norm), where hydrofracturing does not apply.
  • Pressure-tank and switch behavior. Many "low water" calls turn out to be inexpensive equipment fixes.
  • Recent water-quality changes. Sand, color, or scale point toward screen rehabilitation or treatment, not fracturing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing boost my well's output in Redlands?

Usually not. The Bunker Hill Basin beneath Redlands is sand, gravel, and clay—not fractured bedrock—so there are no fractures to reopen. We will assess your well honestly and almost always recommend a more effective alternative.

My water pressure keeps dropping. What is the real cause?

In an alluvial basin, that is most often a shifting water table leaving your pump set too shallow, or a clogged well screen. Deepening the well or resetting the pump typically restores flow.

My water is sandy—can hydrofracturing fix it?

No. Sandy water signals a screen or gravel-pack problem. The right fixes are well rehabilitation, a new screen, and sediment filtration ($300–$900).

How do I know if I am on a well or city water?

Most Redlands homes are served by the city's municipal supply. If you receive a City of Redlands water bill and have no wellhead or pump, your issue is a plumbing or utility matter, and we can help you confirm that.

When does a new well make sense here?

When an existing well is collapsed, sanded-in, or screened at the wrong depth for today's water levels. A new turnkey well runs $18,000–$42,000 and is engineered for current basin conditions.
