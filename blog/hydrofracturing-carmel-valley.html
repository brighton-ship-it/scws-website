What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Carmel Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Carmel Valley Wells

Carmel Valley is a coastal community in the northwestern reaches of the City of San Diego, in San Diego County, set among the mesas and canyons north of Los Peñasquitos Lagoon and east of the Torrey Pines bluffs. Most of the area is on municipal water, but some larger parcels and canyon-edge properties still draw from private wells. If your Carmel Valley well has lost yield, you may be considering hydrofracturing—and we want to be straight with you about when it helps and when it does not.

The honest starting point is geology. Hydrofracturing creates and reopens fractures in hard, crystalline bedrock, where groundwater moves through cracks. Much of coastal Carmel Valley sits instead on layered sandstone and marine-terrace deposits—the same coastal formations that characterize this part of San Diego County. These weakly cemented sands and sandstones do not hold fractures the way granite does; pressurize them and they tend to slump or simply re-fill, so a fracture treatment rarely produces lasting improvement. We will not recommend hydrofracturing for a well like that. Some inland and canyon properties do reach fractured bedrock and can be genuine candidates, which is why each well needs its own assessment.

How Hydrofracturing Works (and Where It Belongs)

For a well finished in suitable hard rock, the process is simple and chemical-free. We seal off a section of the casing with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. That pressure flushes silt and mineral scale from the existing fractures and forces them to open wider and reach farther into the bedrock, creating new pathways for groundwater. Only clean water enters the well—no chemicals, sand, or gels, and nothing resembling the chemical fracking used in oil and gas.

The deciding factor is always the formation. In fractured rock the treatment can dramatically improve yield; in coastal sandstone it generally cannot. An on-site evaluation tells us which situation you are in.

Why Carmel Valley Wells Lose Yield

  • Screen plugging: Sand and sandstone wells rely on a screen, which can clog over time with fine sediment, iron bacteria, or mineral incrustation, restricting flow.
  • Pump or equipment failure: A worn pump or a failed pressure tank can imitate a low-yield well even when the formation is fine.
  • Saltwater intrusion: Coastal over-pumping can pull saltwater toward the freshwater zone, limiting how hard the well can be run.
  • Aquifer drawdown: Dry years and surrounding pumping lower the shallow water table that coastal wells depend on.

What to Check and Signs of Trouble

  • The pump runs longer than it once did to refill the pressure tank.
  • Water pressure drops during peak use or late in the day.
  • Faucets sputter air, meaning the level is being drawn to the pump.
  • Increasing sand, sediment, or turbidity—often a screen issue in coastal wells.
  • A salty or brackish taste, a possible sign of intrusion.

Time the pump's tank-refill cycle when idle and during heavy use; a large gap signals a recovery problem. On a coastal Carmel Valley well, though, the right fix is more often development, screen rehabilitation, or pump work than hydrofracturing.

When to Call a Professional

With so many possible causes—and with the wrong treatment capable of wasting money or worsening intrusion—professional diagnosis is especially valuable on coastal wells. We start with a $125 diagnostic, credited toward any work we perform, to determine whether the trouble is a clogged screen, a failing pump, a pressure-system fault, or a genuine yield problem. Only then do we recommend a path forward, and we will steer you away from hydrofracturing when your geology does not support it.

We help coastal well owners understand that balance so their wells keep delivering clean water for years, rather than trading long-term reliability for a short-term bump in yield. For coastal property owners, that long-term view is the whole point. A well that is managed sustainably and maintained properly will outlast one that is pushed hard for a short-lived boost. Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing a fit for Carmel Valley wells?

It depends on the formation. Carmel Valley, in coastal San Diego near Los Peñasquitos, sits largely on sandstone and marine-terrace deposits, which generally do not respond well to hydrofracturing. Inland and canyon properties that reach fractured bedrock can be candidates. We assess each well before recommending anything—and we are candid when fracturing is not the right tool.

Why doesn't hydrofracturing work well in sandstone?

Hydrofracturing depends on opening cracks that stay open in solid rock. Weakly cemented sandstone and marine sediments tend to slump or re-fill instead of holding a fracture, so the gains usually do not last. For those wells, well development, screen rehabilitation, or pump work is generally the better solution.

Is saltwater intrusion a concern in Carmel Valley?

Near the coast it can be. Over-pumping a coastal well can draw saltwater toward the freshwater zone. We factor intrusion risk into any recommendation, which is one more reason coastal wells deserve careful assessment before treatment.

What does a diagnostic cost?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any work we perform. It tells you honestly whether hydrofracturing, well development, or pump work is the right path for your specific well.

What are the alternatives if fracturing won't help?

Common alternatives include mechanical or chemical well development to clean the screen, replacing a worn pump, fixing a failed pressure tank or switch, or rehabilitating the well screen. Our assessment points you to whichever applies.

How much does a new well cost if it comes to that?

A new turnkey well in San Diego County typically runs $18,000-$42,000 depending on depth and casing. Because that is a major investment, we always look first at whether your existing well can be rehabilitated more affordably.

How do I know whether my Carmel Valley well reaches fractured rock or sandstone?

Your well's original completion or driller's report usually records the formations it passed through, and a downhole camera survey confirms the current picture. We review both during the assessment. Wells that bottom out in fractured crystalline rock can be candidates for hydrofracturing; those screened in coastal sandstone or sand generally are not, and we will recommend development or pump work instead.
