What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Muscoy Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Muscoy, San Bernardino County

Muscoy is an unincorporated community on the northwest side of the San Bernardino Valley, just west of Interstate 215 and the city of San Bernardino in San Bernardino County. It is a flat, low-lying community of homes, small ranchitos, and agricultural lots sitting squarely on the valley floor. That setting is the most important thing to understand about hydrofracturing in Muscoy: the ground beneath the community is the deep alluvial fill of the Bunker Hill groundwater basin — sand, gravel, and clay washed down from the surrounding mountains over millions of years — not the fractured hard rock that hydrofracturing is designed to treat.

For that reason, we want to be candid with Muscoy well owners up front. Hydrofracturing is a powerful tool in the right geology, but the valley-floor alluvium under Muscoy is the wrong geology for it. If your well here is producing less than it used to, the real solutions are almost always deepening the existing well, rehabilitating the screen and gravel pack, replacing a worn pump, or drilling a new well — not high-pressure fracturing.

How Hydrofracturing Works — and Why Rock Matters

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates wells drilled into solid bedrock. A technician lowers an inflatable packer to seal off a section of the borehole, then injects clean water into that isolated zone at high pressure, commonly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. In granite or other crystalline rock, that pressure clears silt out of the natural fractures and widens them, reconnecting the well to water-bearing seams and often multiplying yield several times over. It uses clean water only, with no chemicals.

The catch is that fracturing needs fractures. In loose alluvial sand and gravel — exactly what underlies Muscoy and the rest of the Bunker Hill basin — there is no rock to crack open. Pressurizing that material does not create durable flow paths; the sediment simply shifts and re-packs around the borehole. This is why hydrofracturing is rarely appropriate for valley-floor wells, and why an honest contractor will steer you elsewhere here.

What Actually Causes Low Yield in Muscoy Wells

Because Muscoy sits on a productive alluvial aquifer, most low-yield problems have nothing to do with the rock and everything to do with the well and pump. Common causes include:

  • A clogged or encrusted well screen — mineral scale and iron bacteria slowly plug the slots that let water into the casing.
  • A collapsed or sanded-in screen interval — older wells can fill with sediment, shortening the effective intake.
  • A declining regional water table — valley-wide pumping and drought can lower the static level below an old, shallow pump setting.
  • A worn or undersized pump — impellers wear out and lose capacity, which feels exactly like a failing well.
  • A waterlogged pressure tank or failing pressure switch — cheap fixes often mistaken for a dry well.

The good news is that an alluvial aquifer like the Bunker Hill basin usually holds plenty of water; the trick is restoring the well's connection to it through screen rehabilitation, deepening, or a fresh well — methods that fit the geology.

Better Alternatives to Hydrofracturing Here

For Muscoy properties, we typically recommend one of the following instead of fracturing:

  1. Well rehabilitation — mechanical brushing, surging, and chemical treatment of the screen and gravel pack to restore intake capacity. This is the alluvial-aquifer equivalent of hydrofracturing and is often dramatically effective.
  2. Deepening or lowering the pump — if the water table has dropped, setting the pump deeper or extending the well can recover lost production.
  3. Pump and pressure-system repair — replacing a tired pump, pressure tank, or switch often restores flow at a fraction of the cost of major work.
  4. A new well — when an old well is structurally failing, a properly screened and gravel-packed new well in the basin is the durable answer.

Understanding the Bunker Hill Basin Under Muscoy

Muscoy and the surrounding San Bernardino Valley sit atop one of the most important groundwater reserves in Southern California: the Bunker Hill subbasin of the Upper Santa Ana Valley. This basin is filled with deep alluvial sediments — layered sand, gravel, silt, and clay carried down from the San Bernardino and San Gabriel Mountains over geologic time. In places those water-bearing sediments run hundreds of feet thick, and they store a remarkable volume of water shared by more than twenty public and private suppliers across the valley.

For a well owner, that geology is mostly good news: there is abundant groundwater below Muscoy, and a properly constructed, well-maintained well can tap it reliably. It also explains why hydrofracturing is the wrong approach. Fracturing is a bedrock technique, and there is no bedrock to fracture within reach of a typical Muscoy well — only saturated alluvium. The way you improve an alluvial well is by keeping its screen and gravel pack clean and open and by setting the pump at the right depth, which is exactly where our recommendations focus.

It is also worth noting that parts of the basin have a documented history of groundwater-quality concerns, including the Newmark and Muscoy contamination plumes that prompted long-running cleanup work. That history is a reason to test water quality and to construct or repair wells carefully — another argument for matching the method to the conditions rather than reaching for a fracturing rig that does not fit the ground.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Note when flow drops. A well that runs strong then fades after several minutes points to a screen or water-level problem; one that is weak all the time points to the pump.
  2. Listen for rapid pump cycling. That usually signals a waterlogged tank or pressure switch, not the aquifer.
  3. Watch for sand or grit at the taps. Sand pumping suggests a screen or gravel-pack issue worth inspecting.
  4. Find your well completion report. Depth, screen interval, and static water level tell us exactly what is happening.
  5. Check the breaker and wiring. Sometimes "no water" is simply lost power to the pump.
