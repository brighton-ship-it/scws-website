What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Birdland Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Birdland, California

Birdland is a small residential neighborhood in the City of San Diego, set between Serra Mesa, Kearny Mesa, and the Linda Vista area and known for streets named after birds. It sits inside San Diego County, a region where private water wells are a way of life across the inland valleys and foothills. When one of those wells slows down, hydrofracturing (also written hydrofracking) is frequently the most economical way to revive it without paying for a new well.

Southern California Well Service has worked on San Diego County wells for more than 30 years. The decomposed granite and fractured metamorphic rock of the Peninsular Ranges—the formation beneath much of the county—responds especially well to high-pressure rehabilitation.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing uses clean water and pressure—nothing else—to reopen the natural fractures that supply your well. The sequence is simple to picture:

  • Isolate with packers. We set an inflatable packer in the casing or open hole and inflate it to seal a target section, focusing all the pressure on the rock instead of letting it vent up the borehole.
  • Apply high pressure. Water is injected at 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI, scouring silt and mineral scale from the existing cracks and widening hairline fractures so groundwater flows more freely toward the well.
  • No chemicals, ever. Unlike oil-field fracking, water-well hydrofracturing introduces only water—no additives, no proppant, and no threat to your water quality.
  • Treat several zones. Repositioning the packer lets us work multiple fracture zones in one visit, concentrating on the depths most likely to yield.

A typical residential treatment runs four to eight hours, and we test the flow on the spot to confirm the improvement.

Why Wells in This Part of San Diego County Decline

The hard-rock wells that serve the inland areas tied to Birdland tend to lose yield for a few familiar reasons:

  • Clogged fractures. Fine grit from decomposed granite migrates into the water-bearing cracks over the years and gradually throttles the flow.
  • Mineral buildup. Hard local groundwater deposits calcium and iron on fracture faces and the well screen, narrowing the channels.
  • Drought-driven drawdown. Extended dry spells lower the regional water table, leaving thinly fractured wells short during summer peaks.
  • Biofilm. Iron and sulfur bacteria can coat the screen and nearby fractures with slime that hydrofracturing helps break loose.

What to Check — Signs You May Need Hydrofracturing

Watch for these signals that your well's yield—not its pump—is the problem:

  • The well draws down quickly during irrigation, laundry, or back-to-back showers.
  • The pump cycles rapidly or runs much longer than it used to.
  • Cloudy or sediment-laden water appears after the well rests.
  • Recovery is sluggish—refilling after heavy use now takes hours.
  • Your measured gallons-per-minute has fallen well below the original well log.
  • House pressure has dropped year over year despite sound pump and tank.

The most telling check is comparing today's yield against the tested yield on your well completion report. A clear decline in a hard-rock well is the classic hydrofracturing profile.

Wells across inland San Diego County are commonly completed between about 200 and 600 feet deep, drawing from fracture networks in the granite. Since that water moves through cracks rather than an even porous layer, yield can vary sharply from one parcel to the next. Hydrofracturing helps even out that variability by enlarging and linking the fractures your borehole already crosses.

When to Call a Professional

This is not a DIY task—the pressures are high enough to be dangerous and the equipment must match your casing. Call a licensed contractor at the first sustained drop in flow, when a new well comes in weak, or before you commit to a costly replacement. A professional starts with a baseline flow test, inspects the casing (often with a downhole camera), and confirms the well is in fractured rock rather than loose alluvium, since the method only works in solid rock. We also examine the pump and pressure system, because a flow complaint sometimes traces back to equipment rather than the aquifer.

Timing matters too. The ideal moment to treat is when you first see a steady decline, not after the well quits during a dry stretch. Acting early gives us a stronger well to work with and improves the odds of a durable result, while waiting until the well is empty narrows your options and can leave the household without water.

After the high-pressure stage, we develop the well—surging and pumping to flush out the loosened sediment—and then run a sustained-yield test. That gives you a documented before-and-after result rather than a vague assurance, which is handy for your records and for any future sale of the property. It also establishes a fresh baseline so you can spot the next slow decline early, well before it becomes an emergency.

It is worth remembering what hydrofracturing cannot accomplish. It will not create groundwater in a dry formation, and it cannot repair a collapsed casing or a failed pump and motor. That is the whole point of assessing the well first: we make sure your money goes toward a repair that will actually raise your water supply. When the well is sound and seated in fractured rock, this is one of the highest-value services a San Diego County well owner can choose, often restoring years of dependable production for a fraction of replacement cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing safe for my drinking water?

Yes. Only clean water under pressure is used. No chemicals or additives enter the well, so there is no contamination risk to your drinking water.

How much can my well's output improve?

In San Diego County's fractured rock, many wells see flow rise two to five times, sometimes more. We measure yield before and after so the gain is documented.

Is my well suitable for the procedure?

Hydrofracturing works in wells drilled into hard, fractured bedrock—the norm here. Wells in loose sand or gravel are not good candidates, and we verify your well type before recommending it.

How long will the work take?

Most residential jobs take four to eight hours on site, with a confirming flow test typically within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it really cheaper than a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing usually runs $3,000 to $8,000 against $18,000 to $42,000 or more for a new well, so we recommend it first for a declining well.

What if it does not improve my well?

A small share of wells do not respond, usually because of the formation. If yours is one, we tell you plainly and outline options like deepening or replacement based on the assessment.
