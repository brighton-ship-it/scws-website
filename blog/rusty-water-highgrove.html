Rusty Water from Your Highgrove Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Highgrove well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in Riverside County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Highgrove well.

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Why Highgrove Wells Turn Rusty, Orange, or Red

Highgrove sits in a distinctive pocket of Riverside County, tucked between the city of Riverside and the Grand Terrace and Colton area at the foot of Box Springs Mountain and Blue Mountain, just south of the San Bernardino County line. Homes here draw from the Riverside and Bunker Hill alluvial groundwater basins that are recharged by the Santa Ana River. That river-fed alluvium is exactly the kind of setting where dissolved iron and hard, mineral-rich water are common, so when a Highgrove tap suddenly runs the color of weak tea or wet rust, the cause is almost always traceable to one of a handful of well-understood sources.

The reassuring part is that rusty water is a solvable problem. Southern California Well Service has worked since 2020 working on private wells across Riverside County, and our licensed C-57 crews treat discoloration as a diagnostic puzzle rather than a guessing game. Below is a practical, Highgrove-specific walk through what causes the staining, how to pinpoint where it is coming from, what you can safely check yourself, and what professional treatment costs in this area.

The Common Causes in Highgrove's Groundwater

Iron reaches your faucets through several routes, and each one leaves slightly different clues:

  • Dissolved ferrous iron: Water moving through the Santa Ana River alluvium picks up iron in a clear, dissolved (ferrous) form. It looks fine straight from the well, then turns orange once it hits the air or your water heater and oxidizes into rust-colored ferric particles.
  • Iron and sulfur bacteria: These naturally occurring organisms feed on iron and leave behind a slimy, reddish-brown sludge inside the well, pressure tank, and toilet tanks. In Highgrove they often show up as a stubborn film that returns even after you clean fixtures.
  • Corroding steel casing: Many older Highgrove wells were completed with steel casing. Decades in mineral-laden, slightly acidic groundwater eat at that steel, and flakes of rust enter the water column directly.
  • Corroding galvanized household plumbing: Homes built before the 1970s frequently still have galvanized supply lines. The zinc coating wears away and the underlying steel rusts from the inside, shedding orange particles regardless of how clean the well itself is.
  • A failing water-heater anode or tank: A spent sacrificial anode rod or a rusting tank liner produces rusty water that is worst at the hot tap. This is one of the most frequently overlooked culprits.

Because Highgrove water also tends to be hard, iron staining and scale buildup often appear together, which is why a one-size-fits-all filter rarely solves the problem on the first try.

How to Pinpoint Where the Rust Is Coming From

Before spending a dime on treatment, narrow down the source with a few simple observations:

  • Hot only or every tap? If only the hot water is discolored, suspect the water heater. If cold and hot are both rusty, the problem is upstream in the well, casing, or service line.
  • Sudden or gradual? A sudden change after a repair, a storm, or a season of heavy irrigation points toward stirred-up sediment, a drawn-down water table, or bacteria. A slow, creeping increase over months suggests a corroding casing or aging plumbing.
  • Does it clear after running? Water that runs clear after a minute or two often means settled sediment in the pressure tank or lines. Water that stays orange the whole time means a steady source.
  • Look at the stains. Orange-brown stains usually mean iron. Reddish slime points to iron bacteria. Black streaks suggest manganese riding along with the iron.
  • Note the timing. Highgrove wells can draw harder during dry, high-demand summer stretches; if rust spikes when the water table drops, the pump may be pulling from a more mineral-laden or sediment-rich zone.

What You Can Check Yourself

There are a few safe, no-tools steps a Highgrove homeowner can take. Flush an outside hose bib for several minutes and see whether the water clears. Set your water heater to its drain valve and draw a bucket to check whether the hot side is far worse than the cold. Inspect toilet tanks for reddish slime, a telltale sign of iron bacteria. Replace any old whole-house sediment cartridge and note how quickly it discolors. Collect a sample in a clear glass and let it stand for an hour, watching whether sediment falls out or the color stays suspended. None of this requires opening the wellhead, which should be left to a professional.

Treatment Options and Realistic Highgrove Costs

The right fix depends entirely on the source, which is why testing comes first. Typical Riverside County options include:

  • Oxidizing / greensand iron filter: The workhorse for moderate to high dissolved iron, often the best match for Highgrove's river-recharged water. Installed, expect roughly $1,500 to $3,500.
  • Aeration plus filtration: Injects air to oxidize iron without chemicals, a good choice when iron and a little sulfur appear together.
  • Chlorination injection: A metered chlorine feed ahead of a filter handles heavy iron and knocks down bacteria at the same time.
  • Water softener: For low iron riding on hard water, a softener can manage both; budget about $1,500 to $3,500 installed. Many Highgrove homes pair a softener with a dedicated iron filter.
  • Shock chlorination and well rehabilitation: The standard treatment for iron bacteria, usually a few hundred dollars, sometimes repeated for stubborn colonies.
  • Casing lining or replacement: When corroded steel casing is the source, lining or replacing it stops rust at the origin.
  • Replacing galvanized plumbing with PEX or copper: Eliminates household-side rust permanently.

For context on related work, general filtration runs about $300 to $900, a pressure switch $150 to $350, a pressure tank $600 to $1,500, and a submersible pump $2,500 to $5,500. If a tired well needs more capacity, hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000 and a brand-new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000. We will always recommend the least invasive fix that actually solves the problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the rusty water from my Highgrove well dangerous to drink?

Iron at the levels typically found in Highgrove wells is an aesthetic problem, not a direct health hazard. It stains fixtures and laundry, tastes metallic, and can clog plumbing, but the bigger concern is what the rust may signal, such as a corroding casing or bacteria. We recommend a water test before drinking it routinely so you know exactly what is present.

Why does only my hot water look rusty?

Rust that appears only at the hot tap almost always points to the water heater. A sacrificial anode rod that has worn out, or a tank that is rusting internally, releases orange particles into the hot side while the cold water stays clear. Replacing the anode or the heater usually solves it without any well work.

My water turned orange right after a heavy storm. Why?

Storms and heavy seasonal draw can disturb sediment in the aquifer and around the wellhead, and a drawn-down water table can pull from more mineral-laden zones. In Highgrove, where wells tap Santa Ana River alluvium, a temporary spike in iron or sediment after big weather is common. If it does not clear within a day or two of normal use, have the well checked.

Will a water softener remove the rust from my Highgrove water?

A softener can handle small amounts of dissolved iron along with hardness, and Highgrove water is usually hard enough to justify one. But moderate to high iron, or any iron bacteria, needs a dedicated oxidizing or greensand iron filter. We often install both so the softener is not overwhelmed and the iron is removed at the right stage.

How much does it cost to fix rusty well water in Highgrove?

It depends on the source. Shock chlorination for bacteria is usually a few hundred dollars, a sediment or iron filter runs $300 to $900, and a full oxidizing or greensand iron filter runs about $1,500 to $3,500 installed.
