What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Elijo Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in San Elijo Hills

San Elijo Hills sits in the hills of San Marcos in northern San Diego County, a master-planned community wrapped in the granitic terrain of the Peninsular Ranges. While the development itself is on municipal water, the surrounding hillsides, the larger parcels on the edges of San Marcos, and the rural properties toward Elfin Forest and Harmony Grove often depend on private wells drilled into hard crystalline rock. That rock is precisely what makes hydrofracturing such an effective option here. When a bedrock well in this area starts giving you less water than it once did, the most common cause is not a vanished aquifer — it is a clogged fracture network, and that is exactly what high-pressure rehabilitation is designed to fix.

The elevated, fractured-granite setting around San Elijo Hills is close to ideal for this technique. Drilling a new well in this rock is slow and costly, but the same hardness that frustrates the drill rig means the formation holds open fractures that respond strongly to treatment. For property owners weighing a big expense, that is encouraging news.

Why Granitic Bedrock Responds So Well

Wells in the hills above San Marcos do not draw from a sandy aquifer. They draw from a web of cracks, joints, and weathered seams threaded through otherwise solid granite. Groundwater travels along those fractures to reach the borehole. Over years of pumping, fine clay, decomposed-granite grit, and precipitated minerals settle into the seams and slowly throttle the flow. The water is still down there; it just cannot move fast enough to satisfy the pump. Hydrofracturing injects clean water under controlled pressure — typically 1,500 to 3,000 PSI — to flush those passages clean and ease them open. Because the rock is competent, a single well can be treated at multiple depths in one day, and the improvement tends to hold. No chemicals are used at any point.

The Process, Start to Finish

  1. Baseline flow test: we measure your well's current output so the result is provable.
  2. Camera inspection: where casing allows, a downhole survey checks casing integrity and locates the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Isolation: an inflatable packer seals off a chosen interval of the borehole.
  4. Injection: clean water is pumped at high pressure to clear and open the fractures, repeated at additional depths as needed.
  5. Development and verification: we surge the well to flush debris, then re-test the yield and hand you a before-and-after report.

Most San Elijo Hills jobs wrap up in a single working day, with final flow verification within 24 to 48 hours.

Common Reasons Local Wells Lose Yield

  • Fracture clogging: the leading cause in this granitic terrain, and the one hydrofracturing directly addresses.
  • Mineral scaling: hard groundwater leaves deposits that narrow both the formation seams and the pump components over time.
  • Drought-driven water-table decline: dry years lower the regional table, so a marginal well begins running short in late summer.
  • Aging equipment: a failing pump, waterlogged tank, or bad pressure switch can mimic a "low water" problem while the formation is fine.

We separate these causes before recommending anything, because there is no sense fracturing a well whose real problem is a $200 pressure switch.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Locate your well log: it confirms you are in hard rock and tells us the drilled depth.
  • Observe the pump: rapid on-off cycling usually points to a tank or switch fault, not the aquifer.
  • Note the timing: a steady multi-season decline suggests the formation; an abrupt loss suggests equipment.
  • Read the tank gauge against the switch settings (often 30/50 or 40/60 PSI).
  • Watch for air or sputtering at the taps, a sign the water level may be dropping toward the pump intake.

What Makes a Strong Candidate Even Stronger

Not every bedrock well responds identically, and part of being honest with San Elijo Hills owners is explaining what tips the odds. The best candidates share a few traits. The well was originally drilled into competent rock and produced a respectable yield in its early years, which tells us the fracture network exists and once worked. The casing is intact, so the treatment pressure stays where we want it instead of leaking off through corroded steel. And the decline has been gradual rather than sudden, which points to the slow silting-up of fractures rather than a mechanical failure upstream. When those boxes are checked, the probability of a meaningful gain in this granitic terrain is high. When they are not, we say so, and we steer you toward the option that actually solves your problem rather than selling a treatment that will not.

Drought, Conservation, and Why Yield Matters Here

Northern San Diego County has lived through repeated multi-year droughts, and the regional water table responds to those cycles. A hillside well that comfortably met a household's needs a decade ago can fall short today not because the well failed but because the surrounding groundwater has thinned. Hydrofracturing cannot create water that is not in the ground, but by opening more of the fracture network it lets your well capture more of the water that is present and recover faster between pumping cycles. For properties with irrigation, landscaping, or a few animals to support, that improved recovery rate often makes the difference between rationing and comfortable supply through the dry season. It also reduces the strain on your pump, which spends less time fighting a starved well and more time running efficiently, a quiet benefit that extends equipment life and trims your power bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the San Elijo Hills area good for hydrofracturing?

Yes. Wells here are typically drilled into fractured granitic bedrock, which is among the best rock types for high-pressure rehabilitation when yield has declined.

Will it work if my well is brand new but low-producing?

Often, yes. A freshly drilled granite well that disappoints can frequently be developed and improved by hydrofracturing before you give up on it.

Does the process involve chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure is used. It reopens natural rock fractures and is not the same as oil-and-gas fracking.

How much more water will I get?

Suitable bedrock wells in San Diego County commonly see a two- to fivefold increase. We measure before and after so you see the real number.

How long does it take and how long do results last?

The treatment is usually a single day with verification within 48 hours. In competent granite, results tend to hold for years, especially with sensible pumping and maintenance.

What does it cost compared with a new well?

Hydrofracturing runs $3,000–$8,000, versus $18,000–$42,000 for a turnkey new well — which is why we treat it as the logical first step for a good candidate.
