Understanding Pump Horsepower

Horsepower (HP) in a well pump determines how much water it can lift and how fast. Higher HP means more lifting power (head) and higher flow rates (GPM). Residential submersible pumps typically range from 1/2 HP (light duty, shallow wells) to 2 HP (deep wells, high demand). A 1/2 HP pump might lift water 150 feet at 10 GPM, while a 1.5 HP pump can reach 400+ feet at similar flow rates. The relationship isn't linear—doubling HP doesn't double performance, but it significantly increases capability for deeper or higher-demand applications.

Factors That Determine HP Needs

Three main factors determine required horsepower: Total Dynamic Head (TDH) combines static water level, pumping drawdown, elevation from well to pressure tank, and pipe friction losses. A 300-foot well with 50 feet of elevation and friction losses might have 380 feet TDH. GPM requirements depend on household size—rule of thumb is peak demand of 3-5 GPM per bathroom. A 3-bathroom home needs 10-15 GPM. Well yield limits how much water is available; your pump should never exceed well capacity. Your well log provides static level and yield data essential for proper sizing.

Sizing Charts and Guidelines

General sizing guidelines: 1/2 HP works for wells under 150 feet TDH with modest 5-8 GPM demand—small homes or cabins. 3/4 HP handles 150-250 feet TDH at 8-12 GPM—average 2-3 bathroom homes. 1 HP covers 250-350 feet TDH at 10-15 GPM—larger homes with 3-4 bathrooms. 1.5 HP reaches 350-450 feet TDH at 12-18 GPM—high-demand homes or light irrigation. 2 HP tackles 450+ feet TDH or higher GPM needs. Always size with 10-15% safety margin. If calculations suggest 0.9 HP, go with 1 HP.

Common Sizing Mistakes

Oversizing wastes energy and causes problems. A 2 HP pump on a 150-foot well cycles rapidly because it fills the pressure tank too quickly, reducing pump and tank life. It also costs more upfront and uses more electricity. Undersizing is worse—the pump runs continuously, overheats, and burns out prematurely. Match pump capacity to well yield: if your well produces only 5 GPM, a 15 GPM pump will outpace the well, causing air ingestion and potential damage. The right size pump runs 1-2 minutes per cycle and never exceeds the well's recovery rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if my well pump is too small?

An undersized pump runs continuously during peak demand but can't keep up, causing low pressure and flow. Constant running overheats the motor, leading to premature failure—sometimes within 2-3 years instead of 15-20. You'll also notice pressure drops when running multiple fixtures. If your pump runs more than 10 minutes continuously during normal use, it may be undersized.

Can I upgrade to a higher HP pump without changing my electrical?

It depends on your current wiring. Going from 1/2 HP to 3/4 HP usually works on existing 10-gauge wire. Jumping to 1 HP or higher may require heavier wire (8 or 6 gauge) to handle increased amperage over the distance to your well. The control box also needs to match the new motor. Have an electrician verify wire capacity before upgrading—undersized wiring causes voltage drop, poor performance, and potential fire hazards.

How do I calculate total dynamic head for pump sizing?

Add these components: Static water level (depth to water when pump is off), pumping level drawdown (additional drop during pumping—check your well log), vertical distance from well to pressure tank, operating pressure (add 2.31 feet per PSI—for 50 PSI, add 115 feet), and friction losses (typically 2-5 feet per 100 feet of pipe). Example: 100-foot static level + 20-foot drawdown + 30-foot elevation + 115 feet pressure + 10 feet friction = 275 feet TDH.
