Tucked away in a filing cabinet, buried in closing documents, or lost entirely—your well log is probably the most underappreciated document in your home. But this single piece of paper holds the complete blueprint of your water system: how deep your well goes, what rocks and soil the driller encountered, where water enters the well, how much it produces, and whether it was built to code. It's the medical chart for your well, the service history for your most critical utility, and the roadmap for any future repairs or upgrades. Whether you're buying property with a well, diagnosing water problems, planning to deepen an aging well, or settling a dispute with neighbors over groundwater rights, understanding your well log is essential. This comprehensive guide will teach you how to find your California well completion report, decode every section from geology columns to flow test results, and use this information to make informed decisions about your well system.

📄 California Law: Every well drilled in California must have a completion report filed with the Department of Water Resources (DWR) within 60 days. This requirement has been in place since 1949, though enforcement and record-keeping quality has improved dramatically since the 1980s.

What Is a Well Log or Completion Report?

A well log—officially called a Well Completion Report or driller's report—is a detailed legal document that records everything about your well's construction. Think of it as a birth certificate, blueprint, and construction diary rolled into one permanent record.

The report contains critical information including:

  • Identification information: Well owner name, property location, assessor's parcel number (APN), permit numbers
  • Well construction details: Total depth, diameter, casing specifications, screen or perforations, seal/grout information
  • Geology/lithology log: Detailed description of soil and rock layers encountered at each depth
  • Water information: Static water level, zones where water was encountered, water quality observations
  • Development and testing: Flow rate (GPM), pumping water level, drawdown, test duration, method
  • Completion date and certification: When the work was completed, driller's license number and signature

The well log serves multiple purposes beyond regulatory compliance. It's your reference guide for maintenance and repairs, provides essential information for property transactions, helps diagnose well problems, aids in planning modifications like deepening or rehabilitation, and documents your legal right to groundwater use.

Who Files Well Logs and Why?

In California, licensed well drillers are legally required to submit a Well Completion Report to the Department of Water Resources (DWR) within 60 days of finishing any well construction, deepening, repair, or abandonment work. This requirement is mandated by the California Water Code and enforced by the DWR and local county environmental health departments.

Why This Requirement Exists

The well log filing requirement protects both individual well owners and the broader public interest:

  • Public health protection: Ensures wells are constructed to prevent contamination of groundwater resources
  • Water resource management: Helps state and local agencies track groundwater use, levels, and availability
  • Property documentation: Creates a permanent record of well construction for current and future owners
  • Quality assurance: Holds drillers accountable to construction standards and provides evidence of proper licensing
  • Scientific data: Geology information contributes to understanding of regional aquifer systems and hydrogeology
  • Emergency response: Helps authorities identify and address groundwater contamination or drought impacts

⚠️ Warning Signs of Illegal Drilling

If a driller suggests skipping the paperwork to "save money" or "avoid red tape," walk away immediately. Operating without proper permits and completion reports is illegal and can result in fines, mandatory well destruction, inability to sell your property, and liability if the well causes contamination. Always verify your driller holds a current California C-57 license.

How to Find Your Well Log in California

Locating your well log is usually straightforward, though older wells may require more detective work. Here's where to look:

1. California DWR Well Completion Report Database (Primary Source)

Website: https://data.cnra.ca.gov/dataset/well-completion-reports

The DWR maintains an online database of over 2 million well completion reports dating back to the 1940s. Most reports from the 1980s onward have been digitized and are searchable online.

Search tips:

  • Search by address (most common method)
  • Search by Assessor's Parcel Number (APN) found on property tax bills
  • Search by coordinates (latitude/longitude) if you know them
  • Search by county and township/range/section (Public Land Survey System)
  • Search by well driller name if you know who installed the well
  • Use the well number if it appears on any documentation you have

Note: The database interface can be tricky. Try multiple search variations—different address formats, with and without street type abbreviations (St, Street, Rd, Road), and expand your search radius if the exact address doesn't work.

2. County Environmental Health Department

Your local county environmental health department receives copies of all well permits and completion reports. They often have records not yet digitized in the state database, especially for older wells.

San Diego County residents: Contact the Department of Environmental Health, Land and Water Quality Division. They maintain well records and can help locate historical reports.

Counties typically charge a small fee ($5-$25) for copies of well records, but they can search by property address and provide reports not available online.

3. Your Property Documents

Check your property closing documents—well logs are often included in disclosure packages when property changes hands. Look in:

  • Escrow closing documents
  • Home inspection reports
  • Well inspection certificates (if well inspection was performed)
  • Previous owner records or file boxes left with the property
  • Property disclosure statements

4. Contact the Original Driller

If you know who drilled your well (check for stickers on your pressure tank or well house), contact that company. Most drillers maintain copies of their completion reports and can provide duplicates, though they may charge a records search fee.

Understanding Each Section of Your Well Log

California well completion reports follow a standardized format (DWR Form 188), though older reports may look different. Here's what each section tells you:

Owner and Location Information

What it includes: Well owner name, mailing address, property location, county, APN, coordinates (latitude/longitude), township/range/section

Why it matters: Confirms the well's exact location and legal property association. The coordinates are particularly important if you need to reference your well in legal disputes or if your address has changed since drilling.

Well Construction Section

What it includes:

  • Total depth: How deep the well was drilled (measured in feet from ground surface)
  • Diameter: Borehole size, typically 6-12 inches for domestic wells
  • Casing details: Size (diameter), material (usually steel), thickness (gauge), and depth of each casing string
  • Perforations or screen: Depth intervals where water enters the well
  • Seal/grout information: Material used (cement, bentonite) and depth of sanitary seal
  • Drilling method: Rotary, cable tool, or other technique used

Why it matters: This section describes your well's physical structure and determines what size pump can be installed, whether the well is properly sealed against contamination, if deepening is feasible, and compliance with modern construction standards.

Geology/Lithology Section

What it includes: A column describing soil and rock formations encountered during drilling, listed by depth interval. For example:

0-10 ft: Topsoil and clay
10-45 ft: Decomposed granite, brown
45-80 ft: Clay, gray
80-150 ft: Sand and gravel, water bearing
150-220 ft: Granite bedrock, fractured

Why it matters: This geological record reveals where water-bearing zones (aquifers) are located, shows protective clay layers that prevent surface contamination from reaching groundwater, indicates well stability (clay can swell; loose sand may require special casing techniques), and guides decisions about deepening—you can see what formations lie below your current depth.

Water Level Information

What it includes:

  • Static water level: Depth to water before any pumping (measured from ground surface or top of casing)
  • Water-bearing zones: Depth intervals where water entered the well during drilling
  • Estimated yield: Driller's initial assessment of water production potential

Why it matters: Static water level determines pump depth requirements and provides a baseline for tracking water table changes over time. Knowing where water enters helps diagnose production problems.

Yield and Flow Test Section

What it includes:

  • Flow rate (GPM): Gallons per minute produced during testing
  • Test duration: How long the pump ran (typically 1-4 hours)
  • Pumping water level: Depth to water during the test
  • Drawdown: Difference between static and pumping water levels
  • Recovery time: How quickly water level returned to static after pumping stopped
  • Test method: Air test, bailer test, or submersible pump test

Why it matters: These numbers tell you whether your well can meet household demands, help size your pump appropriately, and provide a performance baseline—if current GPM is significantly lower than the original test, something has changed (screen clogging, aquifer depletion, pump problems).

Seal and Backfill Information

What it includes: Type of seal material (Portland cement, neat cement, bentonite), depth interval sealed, and any backfill materials used

Why it matters: The sanitary seal prevents surface water and contaminants from entering your well through the space between the casing and the borehole wall. Proper sealing is critical for water quality and is required by code to extend at least 20 feet (often 50+ feet in California).

Reading the Geology & Lithology Section

The geology log is often the most confusing part of a well log, but it's also one of the most valuable. It's a vertical slice through the earth beneath your property, recorded as the drill bit advanced downward.

How to Read the Column

Geology logs list formations by depth interval, starting at ground surface (0 feet) and continuing to total depth. Each entry describes:

  • Depth range: From-to depths (e.g., "45-80 feet")
  • Material type: Clay, sand, gravel, rock type
  • Color: Red clay, brown sand, white granite, etc.
  • Grain size: Fine, medium, coarse (for sand/gravel)
  • Consistency: Soft, hard, fractured, weathered
  • Water observations: "Water bearing," "dry," "moderate water"

Example interpretation: If your log shows "80-150 ft: Sand and gravel, coarse, water bearing," this indicates a productive aquifer zone between 80 and 150 feet depth. Your well screen or perforations should be placed in this interval to maximize water production.

Common Southern California Formations

Southern California's geology varies widely, but certain formations appear frequently on well logs in our region. Here's what they mean:

Formation Description Water Characteristics
Clay Fine-grained, plastic soil that's often red, brown, or gray. Can swell when wet. Generally poor water producer. Acts as confining layer protecting deeper aquifers. Can cause well instability.
Sand Granular material ranging from fine (like beach sand) to coarse. Often layered with clay or gravel. Good to excellent water producer, especially medium to coarse sand. Fine sand may require well screens to prevent sand pumping.
Gravel Rounded stones larger than sand, often mixed with sand. Indicates ancient stream deposits. Excellent water producer with high flow rates. Best aquifer material. Often yields 10-30+ GPM.
Decomposed Granite (DG) Weathered granite that has broken down into granular material. Very common in San Diego County. Variable—can be good water producer when fractured or contain clay that restricts flow. Moderate yield.
Granite Bedrock Hard crystalline rock, the "basement" layer. Water only present in fractures and joints. Variable—fractures can yield good water (5-15 GPM), but solid granite produces little to none. Drilling is expensive.
Sandstone Sedimentary rock made of compressed sand. Can be soft to hard. Moderate water producer depending on porosity and fracturing. Common in coastal areas.
Alluvium Mixed sediments deposited by streams—combination of clay, sand, gravel, and cobbles. Generally good water producer, especially in valley areas. Quality varies with composition.

🏔️ Regional Variations

Coastal areas: Often show marine sediments, sandstone, and shale layers. Water tables are typically shallow (100-300 feet). Inland valleys (Ramona, Valley Center): Deep alluvial fills with clay, sand, and gravel layers. Wells typically 200-500 feet deep. Mountain/foothill areas (Julian, Alpine): Decomposed granite over fractured granite bedrock. Wells often 300-800+ feet deep. Water primarily in fracture zones.

Understanding Depth and Casing Information

Total Depth

The total depth is measured from ground surface (or a reference point like the top of the casing) to the bottom of the borehole. This number tells you:

  • Maximum pump setting depth: Your pump must be set above the bottom to avoid sediment
  • Deepening potential: If water levels are declining, can the well be drilled deeper?
  • Investment scale: Deeper wells cost significantly more to drill and maintain
  • Relative productivity: In general, deeper wells access more aquifer zones, but not always

Typical Southern California well depths:

  • Coastal areas: 100-300 feet
  • Inland valleys: 200-500 feet
  • Foothill/mountain areas: 300-800+ feet
  • Desert areas: 400-1,000+ feet

Casing Details

The casing is the steel pipe that lines your well bore. Understanding casing specifications is critical:

Casing diameter: Typically 6-10 inches for domestic wells. Larger diameter allows higher flow rates and accommodates larger pumps. If your well is only 4 inches, you're limited to low-flow pumps.

Casing depth: How far down the steel casing extends. Must extend through any unstable formations and below potential contamination sources. Modern standards require casing through the sanitary seal (at least 20-50 feet, often deeper).

Casing material and gauge: Usually steel (black iron or galvanized), occasionally PVC in newer wells. Gauge indicates wall thickness—lower gauge numbers mean thicker walls. Gauge 8-14 is common for well casing.

Multiple casing strings: Some wells have "nested" casing—larger diameter surface casing to protect shallow formations, then smaller production casing extending deeper. This is shown as separate entries with different depth ranges.

Perforations and Screens

Water doesn't enter a well through the bottom—it comes through openings (perforations or screens) placed opposite water-bearing formations:

  • Perforations: Holes cut or shot through the casing at specific depths. Your log will list depth intervals (e.g., "perforated 150-180 ft, 220-240 ft").
  • Well screens: Sections of slotted pipe that allow water in while keeping sand and gravel out. More common in sandy formations.
  • Open hole: Some wells have casing that stops partway down, leaving the bottom section as uncased "open hole" in stable bedrock.

If your well is producing sand (gritty water, sediment in pressure tank), the perforations may be placed incorrectly or screens may be damaged.

Understanding Flow Test Results (GPM, Water Levels, Drawdown)

The flow test section is where you learn whether your well can actually deliver the water you need. Here's how to interpret the key measurements:

GPM (Gallons Per Minute)

What it is: The flow rate measured during the pump test—how many gallons the well produced per minute.

What you need: A typical household needs 5-10 GPM for adequate water supply. Larger homes, irrigation, or multiple simultaneous fixtures may require 10-15+ GPM.

Interpretation:

  • Excellent: 20+ GPM
  • Good: 10-20 GPM
  • Adequate: 5-10 GPM (sufficient for most homes)
  • Marginal: 2-5 GPM (may need storage tank to meet peak demand)
  • Poor: Under 2 GPM (likely requires significant storage or well rehabilitation)

Important: The GPM on your well log is from a specific pump test, often conducted with an airlift or bailer—not your actual household pump. Your installed pump GPM may differ. See our complete GPM guide for details.

Static Water Level

What it is: The depth to water measured before any pumping—the well's natural resting water level.

Why it matters: Static level determines how deep your pump must be set (usually 20-40 feet below static to account for drawdown). It also provides a baseline to track water table changes over time.

Example: "Static water level: 120 feet" means the water surface is 120 feet below ground level (or below the measuring point, usually top of casing).

Trend tracking: Compare your current static level to what's on the well log. If it's dropped significantly (20+ feet), the aquifer may be declining due to drought or overpumping.

Pumping Water Level

What it is: The depth to water measured while pumping at the test flow rate—the working water level.

Why it matters: This is the lowest water level you'll see during normal operation. Your pump must be set below this depth or it will run dry.

Example: If your report shows "Pumping level: 165 feet at 10 GPM," the water dropped to 165 feet depth when pumping 10 gallons per minute.

Drawdown

What it is: Drawdown = Pumping Water Level - Static Water Level. It's how much the water level drops when you pump.

Example: Static level 120 feet, pumping level 165 feet → Drawdown = 45 feet

What it means:

  • Low drawdown (10-30 feet): Excellent aquifer with high permeability—water recharges quickly
  • Moderate drawdown (30-60 feet): Good aquifer performance—typical for many San Diego County wells
  • High drawdown (60-100+ feet): Limited aquifer capacity or slow recharge—may need to limit pumping rate or install larger storage

Important: High drawdown isn't necessarily bad if you have enough depth. A well with 150 feet of static water level and 80 feet of drawdown still has 70 feet of water available. But a well with 120 feet static and 80 feet drawdown leaves only 40 feet—much less room for error.

Recovery Time

What it is: How quickly the water level returns to static after pumping stops. Sometimes expressed as a percentage recovery rate.

Why it matters: Fast recovery indicates good aquifer recharge. Slow recovery may mean limited aquifer capacity or that you're pumping faster than the aquifer can replenish.

Example: "Recovered to static level in 30 minutes" suggests healthy aquifer performance.

⚠️ Red Flag: Excessive Drawdown

If your well log shows drawdown approaching the total well depth (e.g., 180 feet of drawdown in a 220-foot well), the well may not be able to sustain the tested flow rate for long periods. This can indicate poor well development, insufficient depth for the aquifer, or aggressive pumping during the test. Consider professional evaluation before purchasing the property.

Why Well Logs Matter: Critical Uses

Your well log isn't just a regulatory formality—it's a practical tool you'll need throughout your well's lifespan:

1. Buying or Selling Property with a Well

Real estate transactions involving wells should always include review of the well log, and most lenders require it. The well log helps you:

  • Verify the well exists and is permitted (some "wells" are actually illegal or abandoned)
  • Assess water quantity and quality history
  • Estimate remaining well lifespan based on age and construction
  • Identify red flags like shallow construction, poor yield, or missing seals
  • Plan for future costs (pump replacement, deepening, rehabilitation)
  • Negotiate price adjustments for deficiencies

See our guide on buying property with a well for a complete pre-purchase checklist.

2. Diagnosing Well Problems

When your well isn't performing as expected, the well log is your first diagnostic tool:

  • Low water pressure: Compare current static level to original—has the water table dropped?
  • Decreased flow: Original GPM vs. current—screen clogging? Aquifer depletion?
  • Water quality changes: Geology log shows potential contamination pathways or problematic formations
  • Sand in water: Check perforation depths—are they in sandy zones? Screens damaged?
  • Pump failures: Is the pump set at the right depth? Adequate room for drawdown?

3. Planning Well Deepening or Rehabilitation

If your well needs work, the geology log guides the contractor:

  • Deepening: What formations lie below current depth? Is there potential for more water?
  • Rehabilitation: What's the current construction? Can it be cleaned, reperforated, or lined?
  • Cost estimation: Depth and diameter determine drilling costs and feasibility

4. Resolving Neighbor Disputes

Well logs can prove critical in groundwater rights disputes:

  • Establish priority of use (who drilled first)
  • Document historical water levels and production
  • Show aquifer zones each well draws from (different depths may mean different aquifers)
  • Prove compliance with well spacing regulations

5. Permit Applications and Compliance

You'll need your well log for:

  • County well permits for modifications or new construction
  • Environmental studies if you're subdividing property
  • Water quality investigations by health departments
  • Groundwater sustainability plan reporting (increasingly required in California)

When the Well Log Is Missing or Incomplete

Unfortunately, many older wells have incomplete or missing well logs. This is common for wells drilled before the 1980s, when record-keeping was less rigorous, or for wells that were never properly permitted. Here's what to do:

Search Thoroughly First

  • Try multiple search methods in the DWR database (address variations, coordinates, APN, driller name)
  • Contact your county environmental health department—they often have paper records not yet digitized
  • Check with previous property owners or their estate if possible
  • Look for any identifying information on your pressure tank, well cap, or well house (driller stickers, dates)
  • Search neighboring properties—if their wells were drilled around the same time by the same company, geology is probably similar

Document What You Can

If the original well log is truly lost, create your own documentation:

Hire a professional well inspection: A licensed well contractor can perform a video inspection to assess current condition, measure actual depth (if not too deep), document casing size and condition, identify any obvious construction issues, and test current flow rate and water levels.

Water quality testing: Get a comprehensive water quality test to establish a baseline for future reference. This is especially important for property sales.

Create a retroactive report: Some counties allow or require a retroactive well completion report when work is done on undocumented wells. Your contractor can file this during pump replacement or rehabilitation work.

What to Do When Selling

If you're selling property and can't locate the well log:

  • Disclose the situation to potential buyers immediately—lack of documentation will come up during due diligence
  • Offer to pay for a professional well inspection and flow test
  • Provide any documentation you do have (pump installation records, water test results, property disclosures from when you purchased)
  • Be prepared for buyer requests for price reductions or repair credits
  • Consider proactively documenting the well before listing to avoid delays during escrow

🚨 Legal Risks of Undocumented Wells

Wells drilled without permits or never properly documented may face legal issues including county orders to obtain retroactive permits, required upgrades to meet current code, fines for unpermitted work, or even destruction orders in extreme cases. If you discover your well is undocumented, consult with a licensed well contractor and your county about the best path forward. Ignoring the issue won't make it go away and will complicate property sales.

Sample Well Log Walkthrough with Explanations

Let's walk through a fictional but realistic well log for a San Diego County property to see how all the pieces fit together:

Sample Well Completion Report - Ramona, CA

Owner Information:

John Smith, 12345 Highland Valley Road, Ramona, CA 92065

APN: 123-456-78-00

Completion Date:

August 15, 2015

Well Construction:

Total Depth: 380 feet

Diameter: 8 inches

Casing: 8" steel, 12-gauge, to 280 feet

Open hole: 280-380 feet (in granite bedrock)

Perforated: 240-260 feet (gravel zone)

Seal: Cement grout, 0-50 feet

Geology/Lithology:

0-15 ft: Topsoil and clay, brown
15-60 ft: Decomposed granite, reddish-brown, dry
60-120 ft: Clay, gray, dry
120-180 ft: Sand and gravel, brown, water bearing
180-240 ft: Decomposed granite, tan, moderate water
240-270 ft: Gravel, coarse, water bearing
270-380 ft: Granite bedrock, fractured, water in fractures

Water Information:

Static Water Level: 145 feet

First water encountered: 120 feet (sand/gravel layer)

Main producing zone: 240-270 feet (gravel)

Yield Test:

Test Method: Submersible pump test

Duration: 4 hours

Flow Rate: 12 GPM

Pumping Water Level: 198 feet

Drawdown: 53 feet

Recovery: Returned to static level in 45 minutes

How to Interpret This Log:

✅ Good depth for the area:

380 feet is typical for Ramona. The well reaches multiple water-bearing zones and terminates in fractured granite, giving good production potential.

✅ Adequate flow rate:

12 GPM is excellent for household use. This well can easily support a family of 4-6 with normal water usage including irrigation.

✅ Proper sanitary seal:

Cement grout extends 50 feet down, well beyond the clay layer at 60 feet. This protects against surface contamination.

✅ Multiple producing zones:

Water enters at 120-180 feet (sand/gravel), 240-270 feet (gravel), and through fractures below 270 feet. Multiple zones provide redundancy if one becomes less productive.

⚠️ Moderate drawdown:

53 feet of drawdown is moderate but not concerning. The pumping level (198 feet) is still 182 feet above the well bottom, providing plenty of margin.

✅ Good recovery:

Water level returned to static in 45 minutes, indicating healthy aquifer recharge. This well can sustain 12 GPM pumping without risk of running dry.

📝 Pump setting recommendation:

Set pump at 220-240 feet (at least 20 feet below pumping level of 198 feet, with room for seasonal decline). This leaves 140+ feet of well below the pump for water storage and aquifer access.

🔮 Future considerations:

If water levels decline significantly in the future, this well could potentially be deepened beyond 380 feet into additional granite fracture zones, though this would be expensive. The existing 8-inch casing provides good capacity for higher flow if needed.

Overall assessment: This is a well-constructed, productive well with good depth, multiple water-bearing zones, proper sealing, and adequate flow for domestic use. The completion report provides all the information needed for proper pump installation, maintenance planning, and future decision-making.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a well log or well completion report?

A well log (also called a well completion report or driller's report) is a legal document that provides a detailed record of your well's construction, including its depth, diameter, casing specifications, geology encountered during drilling, water levels, flow test results, and seal information. In California, licensed well drillers are required by law to file this report with the Department of Water Resources (DWR) within 60 days of completing a well. The report serves as a permanent record of your well's construction and provides critical information for maintenance, repairs, property transactions, and water resource management.

How do I find my well log in California?

To find your California well log, search the Department of Water Resources Well Completion Report database at https://data.cnra.ca.gov/dataset/well-completion-reports. You can search by county, address, assessor's parcel number (APN), well number, or driller name. The database includes over 2 million well records dating back decades. If you can't find your well log online, contact your county environmental health department or the DWR directly. Older wells (pre-1950s) may not be in the digital database. Your well driller should have also provided you with a copy when the well was completed.

What does the geology section of a well log tell me?

The geology or lithology section describes the soil and rock formations the driller encountered at each depth interval during drilling. Common Southern California formations include clay, sand, gravel, granite, and decomposed granite. This information reveals where water-bearing zones (aquifers) are located, helps identify potential contamination pathways, indicates well stability, and guides decisions about deepening or rehabilitating a well. For example, clay layers can act as natural seals protecting deeper water, while gravel zones typically indicate good water production.

What do GPM, static water level, and drawdown mean on a well log?

GPM (gallons per minute) measures your well's flow rate during the pump test. Static water level is the depth to water before pumping starts (measured from ground surface). Pumping water level is the depth to water during the test. Drawdown is the difference between static and pumping levels—it shows how much the water level drops when you're pumping. A good well in Southern California typically produces 5-20 GPM with moderate drawdown. High drawdown (over 50 feet) may indicate limited aquifer capacity or poor well construction. These numbers help determine appropriate pump sizing and sustainable yield.

Why is my well log important when buying property?

A well log is essential during property transactions because it reveals the well's age, depth, construction quality, water quantity and quality history, and compliance with regulations. It helps you assess whether the well can support your household needs, identify potential problems before purchase, estimate costs for upgrades or repairs, verify the well was properly permitted and constructed, and negotiate price adjustments for well issues. Many lenders require well inspection and flow testing before approving mortgages on properties with private wells. Missing or incomplete well logs are red flags that warrant thorough professional inspection. See our guide on buying property with a well for details.

What should I do if my well log is missing or incomplete?

If your well log is missing or incomplete, first search the DWR database thoroughly using multiple search criteria (address, APN, coordinates, driller name). Contact your county environmental health department—they often have records not yet digitized. Request previous owner records if available. Hire a licensed well contractor to perform a video inspection to assess current condition, measure actual depth and casing, and identify any issues. Consider having a professional flow test and water quality analysis performed to establish baseline data. For very old wells with no records, you may need to create a retroactive well report during any major work or when required by the county. Document everything for future reference.

How deep should my well be in Southern California?

Well depth in Southern California varies significantly by location and geology, typically ranging from 100 to 800+ feet. Coastal areas often have shallow water tables (100-300 feet), while inland valleys like Ramona and Valley Center typically require 200-500 feet. Mountain and desert areas may need 400-800+ feet or more. The depth needed depends on local aquifer depth, water table elevation, seasonal fluctuations, and well yield requirements. Your well log shows your current depth and water levels. If considering deepening, consult a licensed well driller familiar with your area's geology and groundwater conditions. Learn more about well drilling costs in our detailed guide.

What is well casing and why does it matter?

Well casing is the steel pipe installed in your well to prevent the hole from collapsing, protect water quality by sealing out surface contamination, provide a conduit for the pump and pipes, and maintain structural integrity. Your well log lists casing diameter (typically 6-10 inches for domestic wells) and depth. Proper casing depth is critical—it must extend below any contamination sources and through unstable formations. The annular seal (grout) between the casing and the borehole wall prevents surface water from entering the well. Corroded or damaged casing can lead to well failure, contamination, and safety hazards.
