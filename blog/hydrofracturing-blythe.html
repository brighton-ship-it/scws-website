What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Blythe Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Well Service in Blythe, California

Blythe sits on the eastern edge of Riverside County, in the Palo Verde Valley right along the Colorado River at the California-Arizona line. The valley's water comes from a shallow alluvial aquifer—sand, silt, and clay with gravel lenses, roughly 160 to 600 feet thick—that is hydraulically connected to the Colorado River. Shallow groundwater underlies virtually the entire valley, often within a few feet to a couple dozen feet of the surface, and a deeper layer known as the Bouse Formation lies below. That river-fed, alluvial setting defines how Blythe-area wells are built and which rehabilitation methods will genuinely help when output drops.

Southern California Well Service has serviced Riverside County wells for more than 30 years. We are honest with Palo Verde Valley clients about when hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) is the appropriate tool and when another repair will serve better—because the loose, water-bearing sands and gravels here behave very differently from the fractured bedrock found in the mountains to the west.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing uses clean water and pressure—no chemicals—to reopen the fractures that feed a well:

  • Packers isolate a zone. An inflatable packer is set in the casing or open hole and inflated to seal off the target section so the pressure works on the formation rather than escaping up the borehole.
  • High-pressure injection. Water is pumped at 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI to clear silt and scale from existing fractures and widen the cracks that carry groundwater to the well.
  • No chemicals. Only water enters the well—no additives, no proppant, no risk to water quality.
  • Best in hard rock. The technique excels in fractured granite and crystalline rock. In purely loose sand-and-gravel formations it is generally not the right tool, which is why an honest assessment comes first.

Most wells on the Palo Verde Valley floor are screened in alluvium rather than bedrock, so for those wells we typically recommend screen rehabilitation, redevelopment, or pump service instead. For parcels that reach harder rock—along the valley margins or on the surrounding mesas—hydrofracturing can be an excellent option, and we are equipped to identify and treat those wells.

Why Palo Verde Valley Wells Lose Capacity

Production problems around Blythe usually trace to causes tied to the river-connected aquifer:

  • Screen and gravel-pack plugging. Fine sand and mineral scale build up on the well screen and gravel pack, throttling flow even when water remains abundant in the formation.
  • Mineral encrustation. Palo Verde groundwater can be hard and locally saline, and the dissolved minerals encrust screens and reduce the open area for water to enter.
  • Sand pumping. In loose alluvium, a worn or undersized screen can begin drawing sand, which both fouls the system and damages the pump.
  • Pump and equipment wear. Lower output is frequently a worn pump, a dropped setting, or a pressure-system fault rather than a problem with the aquifer.

What to Check — Signs Your Well Needs Service

Watch for these signs that your Blythe well needs professional attention:

  • Output has dropped noticeably from past years or from the original well log.
  • The pump short-cycles, runs continuously, or cannot hold pressure.
  • Sand or sediment appears in the water—a common alluvial-well warning sign.
  • Water quality or taste changes, which in a river-connected aquifer can shift with conditions.
  • House pressure has weakened despite a sound pressure tank and switch.

Pull your well completion report if you can and compare the original tested yield, depth, and screen interval to today's performance—it tells us a great deal before we arrive.

Because the shallow floodplain aquifer is the principal water source here and groundwater levels are often very near the surface, many Blythe wells are comparatively shallow, while a few near town draw from the deeper Bouse Formation where water quality improves. Knowing your well's depth, screen interval, and construction lets us recommend the right fix—whether that is redeveloping a plugged screen, treating a fractured-rock interval at a mesa-edge parcel, or replacing tired equipment.

The Colorado River connection is a defining feature of Blythe groundwater. Because the shallow aquifer exchanges water with the river and is heavily influenced by the percolation of irrigation water across the valley's farmland, both water levels and water quality can shift with river stage and the irrigation season. For a well owner that has two implications: a marginal well can behave differently month to month, and water-quality issues such as salinity may call for treatment rather than well rehabilitation. Sorting out which factor is driving your particular problem is a core part of our diagnostic visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Blythe well a good fit for hydrofracturing?

Often not, and that is the honest answer. Most Palo Verde Valley wells are screened in sand and gravel, where other rehabilitation methods work better. Hydrofracturing is reserved for wells that reach hard, fractured rock, which we confirm during the assessment.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure is used. There are no chemical additives, so there is no contamination risk to your drinking water.

There is sand in my water—what does that mean?

Sand usually points to a worn or failing screen and should be addressed promptly, because pumped sand damages the pump. A diagnostic visit identifies whether redevelopment, a screen repair, or pump work is needed.

How deep are wells in this area?

Many Palo Verde Valley wells are relatively shallow because the floodplain aquifer is near the surface, though some near Blythe reach the deeper Bouse Formation. Your well log lists the exact depth and screen interval.

Is fixing my well cheaper than a new one?

Almost always. Rehabilitation and pump work cost far less than a new well, which can run $18,000 to $42,000 or more, so we diagnose and repair the existing well first whenever possible.
