Well Pump Won't Start in Bermuda Dunes?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Bermuda Dunes homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem

The Desert Well System, Component by Component

  • Power supply: A 240-volt circuit with a fused disconnect feeds the pump. In the valley's heat, grid voltage quality is a genuine variable.
  • Pressure switch: Signals the pump on and off based on tank pressure; fine windblown sand can clog its sensing port.
  • Control box and capacitor: The surface control box on three-wire pumps holds a start capacitor that suffers in direct desert sun.
  • Pressure tank: Buffers demand so the pump isn't constantly cycling against the heat.
  • Motor and drop cable: Coachella Valley wells are often several hundred feet deep, so motors work against substantial lift.
  • Well and water level: The regional aquifer has seen long-term decline in places, and a falling level near the intake triggers protective shutdowns.

Why Bermuda Dunes Pumps Won't Start

The Coachella Valley's combination of brutal heat, blowing sand, mineral-rich water, and a stressed summer grid produces a recognizable set of failures:

  • Heat-killed start capacitor: The single most common desert no-start. A capacitor cooked in a sun-baked control box can't deliver the starting surge, so the motor hums without spinning.
  • Tripped breaker from grid strain: Peak summer demand causes voltage sags and surges that trip pump breakers.
  • Sand-clogged pressure switch: Windblown grit blocks the sensing port so the switch never calls for the pump.
  • Worn deep-set motor: Deeper valley wells work motors hard, and after 10-15 years windings fail, often tripping the breaker on startup.
  • Drop-cable faults: Heat cycling and the occasional rodent damage wiring at and below the wellhead.
  • Low static water level: Long-term aquifer decline can leave the pump intake near the water line, triggering dry-run protection.

What You Can Safely Check

  1. Reset the breaker once. If it trips again, leave it off and call a professional.
  2. Read the gauge. Zero pressure with no recovery means the pump probably never ran.
  3. Feel the control box (cover closed). Excessive heat or a burnt smell points to a failed capacitor.
  4. Listen at the switch. A click, then a hum, then silence indicates a start-component problem.
  5. Rule out the grid. Confirm whether other 240-volt loads work before assuming the pump is the issue.

Caution: Never open energized equipment, and never repeatedly reset a tripping breaker. In the dry desert, an electrical fault is a serious fire risk.

How Coachella Valley Heat Shortens Component Life

It is hard to overstate how much the desert climate dictates pump failures in Bermuda Dunes. A control box mounted in direct sun can reach internal temperatures far above the ambient 110-plus-degree summer air, and start capacitors are simply not designed to live in that kind of heat indefinitely. Electrolytic capacitors dry out and lose capacitance faster the hotter they run, which is why a heat-killed capacitor is the single most common no-start we diagnose in the valley. The same heat stresses relays, terminal connections, and the insulation on surface wiring. When you add the summer grid strain from valley-wide air conditioning, which causes voltage sags and surges, you have a recipe for frequent breaker trips and premature electrical failures.

For valley installations, we therefore recommend mounting control boxes in shade or ventilated enclosures and adding surge protection at the disconnect. These inexpensive measures meaningfully extend the life of the most failure-prone parts of a desert pump system.

Deep Wells and a Declining Water Table

Coachella Valley wells are frequently several hundred feet deep, and parts of the regional aquifer have experienced long-term decline from decades of heavy use. Both facts affect no-starts. A deeper setting means the motor fights more lift and runs hotter, hastening winding failure, while a declining water table can leave the pump intake close to the water line, where low-water protection shuts the motor off to prevent a dry run. When a Bermuda Dunes pump won't start, confirming the actual water level is an essential part of the diagnosis, because the problem may be the well itself rather than the equipment. In some low-yield cases, hydrofracturing the well can restore productive flow at a fraction of the cost of drilling a new well.
