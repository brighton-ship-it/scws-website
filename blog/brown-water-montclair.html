Common Causes of Brown Well Water

  • Sediment Disturbance: Recent pump work, earthquakes, or construction
  • Iron/Manganese: Dissolved minerals oxidizing when exposed to air
  • Tannins: Organic matter from decaying vegetation
  • Surface Water Intrusion: Contamination from runoff entering well
  • Failing Well Screen: Sand and sediment entering well
  • Clay/Silt: Fine particles from the aquifer

When Brown Water is an Emergency

Contact us immediately if brown water is accompanied by:

  • Sudden change after no recent work on well
  • Foul odor (possible contamination)
  • Recent flooding or heavy rains
  • Sand or grit in the water
  • Neighbors reporting similar issues

Solutions for Brown Well Water in Montclair

  • Water Testing: Identify what's causing the discoloration
  • Well Cleaning: Remove accumulated sediment
  • Sediment Filter: Whole-house filtration system
  • Screen Repair: Fix damaged well screen letting in sediment
  • Grout Seal: Prevent surface water intrusion

Brown or Rusty Well Water in Montclair? Here Is What Is Going On

Montclair is one of the compact, close-in cities of southwestern San Bernardino County, tucked between Ontario, Upland, and the Los Angeles County line and sitting squarely over the northern half of the Chino Groundwater Basin. The alluvial aquifer beneath Montclair is fed by runoff and percolation coming out of the San Gabriel foothills, and it carries the same iron-rich sands and clay layers found throughout the basin. For property owners here who still rely on a private well, that geology is usually the reason the water sometimes runs brown, orange, or cloudy. If you are dealing with discolored water right now, this guide will help you understand the cause and the cure.

Brown water tends to trigger worry about safety, and that concern is reasonable, but in most Montclair cases the color comes from harmless minerals rather than anything dangerous. The key is figuring out which cause is behind your particular discoloration, because the fix for dissolved iron is completely different from the fix for a corroded pipe or a well pulling silt. Southern California Well Service has worked on wells across San Bernardino County for over 30 years, and the sections below lay out exactly how to think about the problem.

The Root Causes We See on Montclair Wells

Discolored well water in the Montclair area almost always traces back to one of five sources. Learning to recognize the signature of each is half the battle.

1. Dissolved iron and manganese oxidizing in the air

This is the dominant cause across the Chino Basin, Montclair included. The metals stay dissolved and invisible in the oxygen-free aquifer, then turn to rust-brown iron and inky manganese particles the instant the water is exposed to air. If your water is clear when it pours but browns as it sits, and you are fighting orange stains on porcelain and laundry, this is almost certainly your issue.

2. A rusting steel casing or galvanized supply lines

Older Montclair homes and wells were often built with steel and galvanized pipe that corrodes over the decades. The result is a metallic taste and rusty first-draw water in the morning that clears once the tap runs for a minute or two. When the discoloration is tied to that overnight-standing water, the plumbing or casing is usually to blame rather than the aquifer.

3. Silt and sediment from pumping or storm recharge

The northern Chino Basin under Montclair is a primary recharge area, and after heavy winter rains the fines in the alluvium can be stirred up. A pump set too low or a worn well screen makes it worse. Sediment-driven brown water is cloudy and gritty and settles into a layer at the bottom of a glass.

4. Tannins from organic matter

Where groundwater passes through decaying vegetation, dissolved tannins can tint it a steady tea-brown that does not settle out. This is less common in Montclair's central-basin sands but not impossible.

5. Iron bacteria

A reddish, slimy buildup in the toilet tank and an occasional swampy or rotten-egg odor point to iron bacteria. They are a nuisance rather than a health threat, but they clog plumbing and filters over time.

How Brown Water Affects Your Home and Equipment

Beyond the visible stains, brown water quietly costs you money. Iron and manganese leave rust rings on tubs and toilets, discolor white laundry, and can give coffee and cooked food a metallic edge. Sediment and rust are abrasive, so they wear pump components, score valves, and clog faucet aerators and appliance inlet screens. Iron bacteria build slime inside pipes and around pressure switches, gradually restricting flow. Left alone, a treatable water-quality problem can turn into a mechanical one, which is why addressing the color early usually saves you from a much larger repair down the road. A modest sediment or iron filter is far cheaper than the pump or appliance it protects.

Diagnose It Yourself in a Few Minutes

A little observation before you call goes a long way toward a fast, accurate repair.

  1. The glass test. Clear-then-brown equals dissolved iron or manganese. Cloudy-and-brown from the tap equals sediment or a failing screen.
  2. Hot versus cold. Brown on the hot side only usually means the water heater, not the well.
  3. First-draw versus continuous. Rust that clears after a minute points to corroding pipe or casing. Persistent color points to the well itself.
  4. The settle test. Grit at the bottom of a glass equals sediment. Uniform color with no grit equals tannins.
  5. The tank check. Rust-colored slime in the toilet tank equals iron bacteria.

No home test substitutes for a real water analysis covering iron, manganese, pH, hardness, and bacteria. We build that test into our diagnostic so we can match treatment precisely to your Montclair water.

Signs You Should Call Right Away

Some situations warrant same-day service rather than a routine appointment. Reach out immediately if brown water appears out of nowhere and keeps worsening, if there is a foul or sewage odor, if flooding or heavy runoff recently reached your wellhead, if you notice sand or grit that could damage your pump, or if neighbors on nearby wells report the same sudden change. Any of these could mean surface-water intrusion or a mechanical failure. Use bottled water until the well is verified safe.

Montclair Brown Water FAQ

Is it safe to drink brown well water in Montclair?

Iron and manganese discoloration is generally an aesthetic issue rather than a health hazard. However, if the brown water carries an odor, followed recent flooding, or came on suddenly, have it tested before drinking and use bottled water in the meantime.

Why is my water clear at the tap but brown in the glass a few minutes later?

That delay is the fingerprint of dissolved iron and manganese oxidizing as they contact the air. An oxidizing or air-injection iron filter removes them at the source and stops the browning.

Only my hot water is discolored. Is my well the problem?

Probably not. When only the hot side is affected, the water heater is the usual culprit. Sediment collects in the tank and the anode rod corrodes. Flushing the heater or replacing the anode often clears it.

Can a water softener fix brown Montclair water?

A softener manages hardness and light iron, but it will not keep up with moderate or heavy iron. If iron is your main problem, a dedicated iron filter is the more effective and durable choice.

The color appeared right after a storm. Should I worry?

Montclair sits over a major recharge zone, so storms can briefly stir silt into well water. It often clears on its own, but if it lingers or smells off, get the well inspected to rule out surface-water intrusion.
