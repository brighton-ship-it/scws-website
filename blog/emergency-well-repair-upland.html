The Most Common Well Emergencies We See in Upland

Wells on the north edge of Upland and up in San Antonio Heights tend to be deeper and to draw from fractured foothill formations, and the orographic rainfall that the San Gabriels wring out of passing storms can swing the water table from year to year. Combine that with hardworking pumps and you get a predictable set of emergencies:

  • Sudden, total loss of water. When every faucet runs dry at the same moment, the problem is in the well or pump, not in the house plumbing.
  • Submersible pump failure or motor burnout. Deep foothill wells work their pumps hard, and an aging motor will eventually seize or burn out, sometimes after years of faithful service.
  • Pressure tank failure. A failed bladder leaves the system waterlogged, causing the pump to short-cycle and risking rapid motor damage.
  • A well running low or producing sand. In dry years the water level in foothill wells can drop, leaving the pump drawing air or grit instead of clean water.
  • Electrical and control box failures. Relays, capacitors, and control boxes degrade over time and are a leading reason a pump simply refuses to start.
  • Burst pipe or leak. A cracked drop pipe, failed fitting, or leaking pitless adapter bleeds away pressure and wastes water.
  • No water after a power outage. Foothill neighborhoods see their share of outages, and the grid coming back does not guarantee the well system restarts cleanly.

What to Check First Before You Call

A handful of safe checks may bring your water back, and if they do not, they give our technician a head start. Never remove the well cap, handle wiring near standing water, or try to lift a pump on your own.

  1. Inspect the breaker. In your main panel, find the breaker serving the well or pump, switch it fully off and then on. If it trips again right away, leave it off and call, because that signals an electrical fault.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. The gauge by your pressure tank should sit roughly between 40 and 60 psi. A reading of zero tells you the pump is not building pressure.
  3. Check the pressure tank. Rap on the tank from top to bottom; a hollow top and solid bottom is normal, while a tank that feels solid throughout is waterlogged and failing.
  4. Listen at the pressure switch. The small switch on the pipe near the tank should click when the system demands water. Silence can mean a bad switch or no power reaching the pump.
  5. Verify the grid. Confirm power is on across the property and that any disconnect or sub-panel feeding the well is energized, which matters on foothill lots where the well sits well away from the house.

What to Do While You Wait for Us

After you call, a few steps keep your household comfortable and protect your equipment. If the well breaker keeps tripping, leave it off so the motor is not damaged by repeated start attempts. Ration the water still held in your pressure tank and water heater for drinking and essentials. On the steeper San Antonio Heights lots, make sure our technician will have clear, safe access to the wellhead, the pressure tank, and the electrical disconnect, and move vehicles or stored items that might block a service truck on a narrow foothill driveway. If you have stored water, set some aside for cooking and sanitation until we arrive.

When It's Truly Urgent

Certain situations should not wait. Call right away if you have no water and someone in the home depends on it for medical reasons, if a breaker trips again and again or you notice a burning smell near the pump or control box, or if water is flooding around the wellhead or pump house. On foothill properties, a pump grinding away in a low well or an unattended leak can cause damage that dwarfs the original repair, and a saturated hillside near a leak is its own hazard. If you are unsure how serious it is, call and let a technician help you decide.
