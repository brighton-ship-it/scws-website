Well Inspection Before Buying

Protect Your Investment With Proper Due Diligence

SC

By SCWS Team

Published February 17, 2026 · 10 min read

A well can be a home's most valuable feature—or its most expensive problem. Standard home inspections often give wells only cursory attention. Hiring a well specialist to inspect before you buy protects you from costly surprises and gives you negotiating leverage.

Don't skip this step. Well problems can cost $5,000-50,000+ to fix. A $500 inspection is cheap insurance. Budget for both well inspection and water testing during your inspection contingency period.

What a Well Inspection Covers

Visual Inspection

  • Wellhead condition and seal integrity
  • Proper height above grade (minimum 12")
  • Cap/seal condition
  • Surface drainage away from well
  • Setback from septic, fuel tanks, etc.

Equipment Evaluation

  • Pump type, age, and condition
  • Pressure tank condition and sizing
  • Electrical components and wiring
  • Control box and pressure switch
  • Treatment systems present

Performance Testing

  • Flow rate measurement (GPM)
  • Static water level
  • System pressure check
  • Recovery rate (how fast well recharges)
  • Pump cycle timing

Optional: Camera Inspection

A video camera lowered into the well shows casing condition, screen condition, depth, and any problems not visible from the surface. Worth the extra cost for older wells or if there are any concerns.

Water Testing

Water testing is typically separate from well inspection. Use a certified laboratory for defensible results.

Essential Tests

  • Bacteria: Coliform and E. coli (safety critical)
  • Nitrates: Especially near agriculture (safety critical)
  • pH: Affects plumbing and taste
  • General chemistry: Hardness, TDS, iron, manganese

Area-Specific Tests

  • Arsenic: Common in many California areas
  • Uranium: Granite bedrock areas
  • Pesticides: Near agricultural land
  • VOCs: Near industrial sites

Red Flags to Watch For

Warning Signs

  • 🚩 Failed bacteria or nitrate tests
  • 🚩 Flow rate under 3 GPM
  • 🚩 Pump age over 15-20 years
  • 🚩 Improper wellhead construction
  • 🚩 Missing or damaged sanitary seal
  • 🚩 Signs of flooding around wellhead
  • 🚩 No documentation available
  • 🚩 Seller resistance to testing

Using Findings in Negotiation

Inspection findings are negotiating tools. Options include:

  • Request repairs: Seller fixes issues before closing
  • Credit/price reduction: Seller credits you for repair costs
  • Escrow holdback: Funds held until repairs completed
  • Walk away: Exercise your contingency if problems are too severe

Get repair estimates from a well contractor to support your negotiation position. Sellers are more likely to negotiate when you have documented problems and cost estimates.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a well inspection include?

A thorough well inspection includes: visual inspection of wellhead and equipment, pump operation and pressure test, flow rate measurement, static water level measurement, electrical system check, pressure tank evaluation, and review of visible components. Some inspections include camera inspection down the well. Water quality testing is usually separate.

How much does a well inspection cost?

Basic well inspections typically cost $200-500 in California. Comprehensive inspections with flow testing may run $300-600. Water quality testing adds $100-400 depending on what's tested. Camera inspection of the well is additional ($200-500). Total budget $500-1000 for thorough evaluation. This is minimal compared to potential repair costs.

What water tests should I request?

At minimum: bacteria (coliform/E. coli), nitrates, pH, and general chemistry. Consider adding: arsenic (common in California), iron/manganese, hardness, and TDS. If near agriculture, test for pesticides. If near old industrial sites, test for VOCs. Your inspector or county health department can recommend area-specific tests.

What are red flags in a well inspection?

Major concerns: failed bacteria or nitrate tests, very low flow rate (<3 GPM), old or failing equipment, improper wellhead construction, missing or broken sanitary seal, signs of surface water infiltration, low static water level, and missing documentation. Any of these may warrant walking away or significant price negotiation.
