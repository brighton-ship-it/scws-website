Well Water Has Purple or Pink Tint: Causes & Solutions

Purple or pink water coming from your taps is definitely alarming—and uncommon enough that many well owners have never encountered it. Unlike brown or orange water (which usually indicates iron), purple water has more specific causes that are usually related to water treatment equipment.

Common Causes of Purple/Pink Well Water

1. Potassium Permanganate from Treatment System (Most Common)

If you have a greensand filter or other iron/manganese treatment system, this is the most likely culprit.

Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) is a purple chemical used to regenerate greensand and other oxidizing filter media. During regeneration, the chemical flushes through the system and should be rinsed away before treated water enters your home.

Why purple water appears:

  • Regeneration cycle interrupted or timed incorrectly
  • Rinse cycle too short
  • Chemical feeder overdosing
  • Valve malfunction allowing untreated water through
  • System used during regeneration cycle

Signs this is your problem:

  • You have an iron/manganese filter (greensand, Birm, or similar)
  • Purple water appeared suddenly
  • May coincide with regeneration time (often overnight)
  • Water may also stain fixtures and laundry purple

2. High Manganese Levels

Manganese is naturally present in many aquifers. At very high concentrations, oxidized manganese can create purple or pinkish-black tints.

Characteristics:

  • Usually more of a dark purple or black tinge
  • Often accompanies black staining on fixtures
  • May appear after water sits (manganese oxidizes over time)
  • Can develop gradually rather than suddenly

Manganese at levels high enough to color water visibly is above recommended limits (EPA secondary standard is 0.05 mg/L).

3. Water Softener Issues

Some water softeners use potassium permanganate for iron removal. Similar to greensand filters, if the rinse cycle doesn't complete properly, purple water can result.

Additionally, if you recently switched from sodium chloride to potassium chloride salt, certain reactions with existing conditions can occasionally cause pink tinting.

4. Bacteria (Rare)

Certain bacteria can produce purple or pink pigments, though this is uncommon:

  • Serratia marcescens: Produces pink/red pigment, usually seen as pink slime in humid areas (shower, toilet)
  • Chromobacterium: Can produce violet pigment, but very rare in well water

Bacterial causes usually show as slime or growth rather than dissolved color in the water.

5. Cross-Contamination (Very Rare)

In industrial or agricultural areas, chemical contamination from nearby operations could theoretically cause unusual water colors. This is extremely rare but worth considering if other causes are ruled out.

Is Purple Water Safe?

Potassium Permanganate

At treatment levels, potassium permanganate is approved for water treatment and not harmful. However, the visible purple color indicates excess concentration that:

  • Tastes metallic or unpleasant
  • Stains fixtures, laundry, and skin
  • Indicates a malfunction that should be corrected

Recommendation: Don't drink visibly purple water. Flush the system until water runs clear.

High Manganese

The EPA's health-based guidance level for manganese is 0.3 mg/L. Long-term consumption of high manganese water may affect the nervous system, particularly in infants and children.

Recommendation: Test the water and install appropriate treatment if manganese is elevated.

General Rule

Any visibly discolored water should be tested before regular consumption. While purple water is often harmless (just treatment chemical excess), identifying the cause ensures safety and prevents ongoing issues.

Solutions by Cause

For Treatment System Issues

  1. Flush the system: Run water at an outdoor faucet (to avoid staining fixtures) until it runs clear
  2. Check regeneration timing: Ensure it occurs when no water is being used
  3. Verify rinse cycle: The post-regeneration rinse should run long enough to clear all chemicals
  4. Inspect chemical feeder: Check for overdosing, clogging, or malfunction
  5. Test media: Old or exhausted greensand may not properly utilize the regenerant
  6. Call your water treatment professional: System may need adjustment or repair

For High Manganese

  • Oxidizing filter: Greensand, Birm, or similar media removes manganese
  • Aeration + filtration: Oxidizes and filters manganese
  • Chlorination: Oxidizes manganese for filtration
  • Water softener: Removes low levels of manganese

Treatment choice depends on manganese levels and what other contaminants are present (often iron accompanies manganese).

For Bacteria

  • Shock chlorination: Kills bacteria in the well and plumbing
  • Regular cleaning: For surface bacteria like Serratia
  • UV or chlorine disinfection: For ongoing bacterial issues

Removing Purple Stains

If potassium permanganate has stained fixtures, skin, or laundry:

Fixtures and Surfaces

  • Make paste of baking soda and water, apply and scrub
  • Oxalic acid-based cleaners (Bar Keeper's Friend)
  • Sodium bisulfite solution
  • Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) can help neutralize permanganate

Skin

  • Usually wears off in a few days
  • Scrubbing with soap and water helps
  • Lemon juice or vitamin C may lighten stains

Laundry

  • Soak in solution of oxalic acid or sodium bisulfite
  • Commercial rust removers may help
  • Some stains may be permanent on fabrics

Preventing Future Occurrences

  • Regular treatment system maintenance: Annual professional inspection
  • Monitor regeneration: Note if purple water appears at certain times
  • Verify timer settings: After power outages, check that timers are correct
  • Use water during safe hours: Avoid drawing water during regeneration cycles
  • Test raw water periodically: Know your manganese levels

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well water purple or pink?
Purple or pink well water is usually caused by: potassium permanganate from a malfunctioning greensand filter, high manganese levels oxidizing in the water, or in rare cases, certain bacteria. If you have an iron/manganese treatment system, check it first—this is the most common cause.
Is purple well water safe to drink?
It depends on the cause. Potassium permanganate in small amounts is used in water treatment and is generally not harmful, but visible purple color indicates excess that should be corrected. High manganese may have health concerns at elevated levels. Don't drink visibly discolored water until you identify the cause.
How do I fix purple water from my greensand filter?
Check the potassium permanganate feeder—it may be overdosing. Verify the regeneration cycle timing and that the system is rinsing properly before water enters the house. The chemical feed rate may need adjustment, or the filter media may need replacement if it's no longer working correctly.
Will purple water stain my fixtures and clothes?
Yes, potassium permanganate stains are difficult to remove. If you notice purple water, immediately stop using it for laundry or bathing. Flush at an outdoor faucet until clear. Existing stains may require special cleaners like oxalic acid (Bar Keeper's Friend) or sodium bisulfite.
Why does purple water only appear sometimes?
If your treatment system regenerates at a set time (often overnight), purple water may appear if you use water during or shortly after regeneration before the rinse cycle completes. Note when the purple water appears and compare to your system's regeneration schedule.
Can high manganese cause purple water naturally?
Very high manganese levels can create dark purple or blackish tints when the manganese oxidizes. This usually develops gradually rather than appearing suddenly. Get your water tested—if manganese is the cause, you likely need treatment equipment installed.
