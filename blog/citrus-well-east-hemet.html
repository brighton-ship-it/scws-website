Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Citrus Grove Well Services in East Hemet, Riverside County

East Hemet sits on the eastern side of the Hemet-San Jacinto Valley in central Riverside County, where the valley floor rises gently toward the San Jacinto Mountains and the foothill communities of Valle Vista and the country around Diamond Valley Lake. This has long been orchard and citrus territory. The combination of warm days, a long frost-managed growing season, and deep valley soils made the East Hemet area a natural home for oranges, lemons, grapefruit, and mandarins, and many of those groves, along with newer plantings on rural-residential parcels, still depend on private agricultural wells for every drop of irrigation. Southern California Well Service, a licensed C-57 contractor with local experience since 2020 and a 4.9-star reputation, drills, equips, and maintains the wells that keep East Hemet citrus productive.

Because dependable surface water is rarely an option for these groves, the well is the whole irrigation supply. A correctly sized, properly equipped, well-maintained system is the difference between a grove that sizes a full crop and one that drops fruit under summer heat or loses trees to a winter freeze.

What Citrus Demands From a Well

Mature citrus in the hot Hemet-San Jacinto Valley is a heavy water user. A sound planning number for healthy bearing trees is roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year, leaning to the high end through the valley's long, dry summers. Since an acre-foot is about 326,000 gallons, even a few acres of grove can run through millions of gallons in a season, and a commercial block far more. Citrus that goes short during bloom, fruit set, or the summer sizing window will shed fruit and lose grade, so there is little tolerance for a well that cannot keep pace.

In practical terms, East Hemet groves are often served by wells producing from 50 GPM up into the hundreds of gallons per minute, scaled to acreage and irrigation scheduling. We assess your grove size, irrigation method, and storage before settling on a yield target and pump selection.

Frost Protection and Peak Flow

Cold winter nights settle into the valley floor, and frost protection is a genuine concern for East Hemet and Valle Vista groves. Sprinkler-based frost protection can require on the order of 50 or more GPM per acre, sustained through the coldest hours, an enormous instantaneous draw next to ordinary summer irrigation. A well and pump sized only for average use will not protect trees on a freeze night, so we engineer pumping and storage around that peak event rather than the average day.

Sizing Well, Pump, and Storage as One System

A reliable citrus water supply is three coordinated pieces working together.

  • Well yield: We measure static level, drawdown under pumping, and recovery so the equipment is matched to what the well can actually sustain, not an optimistic guess.
  • High-capacity pumps: Citrus irrigation generally uses a high-capacity submersible pump or a vertical turbine pump, selected to the well's sustainable yield and the pressure your drip or micro-sprinkler design requires.
  • Storage ponds and tanks: Storage is what lets a steady well meet spiky demand. A lined pond or tank bank fills around the clock while irrigation and frost pumps draw on the reservoir for short, intense peaks, and it provides backup if the pump needs service mid-season.

Get the balance wrong and you either stress trees in August or oversize a pump that hammers the well. We design the whole package to your grove so neither happens.

Water Quality for East Hemet Citrus

Citrus ranks among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, and valley groundwater can carry minerals that injure a grove gradually. Three constituents top our watch list.

  • Salinity (EC): High total salts limit a tree's water uptake and erode yield over time. Because citrus tolerance is modest, we recommend testing electrical conductivity (EC) before committing to a source.
  • Chloride: Citrus accumulates chloride in its leaves, producing the familiar tip and margin leaf burn. Managing source water and choosing the right rootstock keeps it in check.
  • Boron: Boron turns toxic to citrus at very low concentrations, often cited near the 0.5 to 1 ppm range, making it a priority item on any irrigation water test.

We sample your water, interpret it against citrus tolerances, and recommend treatment, blending, or rootstock-aware strategies when results call for it.

Drought and Groundwater Stewardship

Groundwater beneath the Hemet-San Jacinto Valley is a shared resource that Southern California's droughts steadily draw down. In dry cycles, water levels fall, pumping lifts rise, and weaker wells lose capacity. California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) has heightened the focus on long-term basin health across Riverside County, so efficiency now matters as much as raw capacity. Efficient drip and micro-sprinkler irrigation, soil-moisture-based scheduling, and right-sized pumping help East Hemet growers make the most of every acre-foot, which protects both the grove's economics and the basin.

Common Citrus Well Issues We Encounter

  • Slow yield decline as a well ages and the screen or casing builds mineral encrustation.
  • Pump and motor failures from sand, sediment, or the heavy irrigation-season duty cycle.
  • Pressure and flow loss in drip and micro-sprinkler systems, usually clogged emitters, failing filtration, or a mismatched pump.
  • Creeping salinity or chloride through extended dry spells.
  • Frost-night shortfalls where an older system cannot reach the peak flow a freeze demands.

Maintenance That Pays Off

Citrus wells last longer and perform better with preventive care. We recommend periodic well performance testing to track yield and drawdown, regular pump and motor inspection, monitoring of pressure and flow at the irrigation manifold, and clean filtration to keep emitters open. Seasonal water testing catches salinity or chloride drift before it shows on the foliage. Replacing a worn pump on your terms is far cheaper than an emergency failure in peak summer or during a frost.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much well capacity does an East Hemet citrus grove need?

It depends on acreage and irrigation method, but groves commonly run on wells producing 50 GPM up into the hundreds of GPM. Frost protection can demand 50 or more GPM per acre, so we size to the peak rather than the average.

How much water does mature citrus use per year?

Plan on roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year for healthy bearing citrus in the hot Hemet-San Jacinto Valley, with the high end during long, dry summers.

Why is storage important for a citrus well?

Peak demand, especially frost protection, can far exceed steady well output. A pond or tank bank lets a steady well fill a reservoir continuously while pumps draw on it for short, heavy peaks.

What water quality problems hurt citrus most in East Hemet?

Salinity, chloride, and boron. Citrus is salt-sensitive, chloride causes leaf burn, and boron is toxic at low levels (often cited around 0.5 to 1 ppm). A water test flags these early.

What does a new agricultural citrus well cost?

A turnkey agricultural well typically runs about $25,000 to $60,000 or more depending on depth and equipment, with high-capacity pumps around $3,000 to $8,000 and hydrofracturing about $3,000 to $8,000.

Can you help with both small and commercial citrus plantings?

Yes. We serve everything from backyard and rural-residential groves to full commercial blocks throughout East Hemet and Riverside County.
