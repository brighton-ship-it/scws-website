Sand in Your Big Bear Lake Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Big Bear Lake well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Big Bear Lake well.

Sand and Sediment in Big Bear Lake Well Water: A Local Guide

Big Bear Lake sits at roughly 6,750 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, a high alpine resort community wrapped around its namesake reservoir in San Bernardino County. Property owners here who depend on a private well draw their water from a very specific kind of aquifer, and that geology shapes nearly every sand and sediment problem we see. If you have started noticing grit settling at the bottom of a glass, sandy residue in the bathtub, or a sputtering faucet, the cause almost always traces back to what is happening hundreds of feet below your property in Bear Valley.

The good news is that sandy water is a well-understood, fixable problem. The important news is that it should not be ignored. Sand is abrasive, and a well that is producing it will quietly destroy an expensive submersible pump, scar plumbing fixtures, and clog your pressure tank long before you connect the dots. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years servicing wells across the mountain communities, and the pages below walk through why Big Bear wells produce sand, what it damages, how we diagnose it, and what it costs to put it right.

Why Big Bear Wells Produce Sand

To understand sand in your water, it helps to understand where that water comes from. Domestic wells in the Big Bear area draw from the Big Bear Valley groundwater basin. This basin is made up of alluvial valley-fill material, sand and gravel that has eroded off the surrounding ridges and washed down into the mountain valley over thousands of years, including glacial and erosional fan deposits shed off Sugarloaf and the high peaks ringing Bear Valley. Beneath and around that loose valley fill sits fractured granitic and crystalline bedrock of the San Bernardino Mountains. Water moves through the cracks and seams in that hard rock as well as through the sandy sediments above it.

This combination matters because both formations can shed fine material into a well. The valley-fill alluvium is, by its nature, sandy and unconsolidated, so an aging or improperly built well screen sitting in that material can let sand migrate inside the casing. The fractured bedrock contributes its own grit, including weathered granitic fines and decomposed rock that work loose from fracture faces. A well in Big Bear City, Fawnskin, Moonridge, Sugarloaf, Erwin Lake, or out toward Baldwin Lake may be completed in mostly alluvium, mostly fractured rock, or a mix of both, which is exactly why a local technician who knows these formations diagnoses the problem faster than a generic crew.

Within that geology, the specific triggers we see most often include:

  • A worn or failing pump pulling sand and fines. As a submersible pump ages, its impellers and bearings wear, the unit churns harder, and it begins lifting sediment it once left behind.
  • An oversized pump over-drawing a limited mountain well. Many Big Bear wells are modest producers. Install a pump that is too powerful and it will overpump the formation, creating excessive suction that drags sand off the alluvium and bedrock faces.
  • A failed or corroded well screen. The screen is the barrier that admits water while holding back the formation. When it corrodes through, splits, or pulls apart, sand pours in unchecked.
  • The wrong screen slot size for the formation. A screen built for coarse gravel will pass the finer sand common in valley-fill alluvium. Mismatched slot sizing is a frequent culprit in older wells.
  • Excessive drawdown. Drawing the water level down too far, especially during a drought year or peak summer use, increases velocity into the well and mobilizes sediment that otherwise stays put.
  • A new, undeveloped well. A freshly drilled well naturally produces some sand for the first days or weeks until it is properly developed and the fines around the borehole are cleared.
  • Naturally sandy alluvium. Some Bear Valley wells simply sit in fine, sandy sediment, and a small amount of sand production is part of life there unless filtration is added.

Sorting out which of these applies to your well is the heart of a good diagnosis. A sudden onset of sand often points to a mechanical failure such as a cracked screen or a worn pump, while a slow, seasonal increase more often points to drawdown, an oversized pump, or the formation itself.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is hard, angular, and relentless, and it does real damage as it moves through your water system. The most expensive victim is usually the submersible pump itself. Sand grinds away at the impellers and seals, and a pump that should last 10 to 15 years can fail in a fraction of that time when it is constantly moving abrasive sediment. Because the pump may sit several hundred feet down in the well, replacing it is never a small bill.

Beyond the pump, sand settles in the pressure tank and reduces its effective capacity, causing the pump to cycle on and off more often, which shortens its life further. It scores valve seats so they no longer seal, leading to pressure loss and dripping. It packs into water heaters, where it bakes into a hard layer that steals efficiency and capacity. It clogs the tiny passages in faucet aerators, toilet fill valves, washing machines, dishwashers, and irrigation emitters. Left long enough, sand can also signal a structural problem such as a casing breach that, if ignored, threatens the integrity of the entire well. In a cold-climate community like Big Bear, where wellheads and exposed plumbing already contend with hard winter freezes, a sediment problem layered on top of freeze stress can take a system out of service at the worst possible time.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Big Bear

Guessing is expensive, so we diagnose methodically before recommending any repair. Our diagnostic visit typically includes:

  • A jar test. We collect water and let the sediment settle so we can measure how much sand is present and judge its grain size and character, which hints at whether it is alluvial sand or fractured-rock grit.
  • Sand concentration measurement. We quantify how heavily the well is producing sediment, which tells us whether filtration alone will suffice or the well itself needs attention.
  • Amp draw and pump depth checks. Reading the pump's electrical draw and confirming its set depth reveals whether the pump is straining, oversized, or hung too low in the sediment zone.
  • Pulling and inspecting the pump and screen. When warranted, we pull the pump to inspect it and the screen directly for wear, corrosion, and cracks.
  • A downhole camera survey. A camera lowered into the well shows us the casing, the screen, and the producing zones, often pinpointing the exact breach or failed section.
  • Flow and drawdown testing. Measuring yield against how far the water level drops shows whether overpumping or excessive drawdown is mobilizing the sand.

How We Fix It

Because sandy water has several possible causes, it has several possible fixes, and the right one depends entirely on the diagnosis. Common solutions include:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters. These spin sediment out of the water stream using centrifugal force, with no filter media to replace, an excellent first line of defense for steady, moderate sand production.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration. Spin-down filters and cartridge-style sediment filters polish out finer particles that a separator passes, protecting fixtures and appliances downstream.
  • Pump resizing and repositioning. If an oversized pump is overdrawing the well, we install a correctly sized unit; if the pump hangs too low in the sediment, we raise it above the accumulation zone.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment. For wells choked with fines or never properly developed, we clean and redevelop the well to stabilize the formation and restore clean production.
  • Screen repair or relining. When the screen has failed, we repair, replace, or reline it so it once again holds back the formation while admitting water.

In many Big Bear cases the best result comes from combining approaches, for example resizing the pump to stop overpumping the alluvium and adding a centrifugal separator to handle the modest sand the formation naturally yields.

Preventing Sand Problems

Some sand prevention happens at the well, and some happens through maintenance. Sizing the pump correctly for the actual yield of your mountain well is the single most effective preventive step, since an overpowered pump is a common cause of self-inflicted sand. Setting the pump at the proper depth, neither too low in the sediment nor so high it loses prime, matters just as much. Beyond that, avoid pushing the well past its sustainable yield during peak summer demand, have your system inspected periodically so a corroding screen is caught before it fails, and in our cold climate make sure the wellhead and exposed plumbing are protected against winter freezes that can crack components and worsen sediment intrusion. A sediment filter sized to your water's character provides ongoing insurance for fixtures and appliances even when the well is behaving well.

When to Call a Professional

A little fine sand in a brand-new well that clears within a couple of weeks is usually nothing to worry about. Call us promptly, though, if sand appears suddenly in a well that was previously clean, if the amount of grit is increasing over time, if you see sand alongside dropping water pressure or a pump that short-cycles, or if the water turns cloudy or gritty after years of running clear. These patterns point to mechanical failure or formation problems that get more expensive the longer they run. Because a pump grinding sand can fail with little warning, and because Big Bear winters make an emergency well failure especially miserable, it is far cheaper to diagnose early than to replace a destroyed pump later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Big Bear well water normal?

In a brand-new well, a small amount of sand for the first days or weeks is normal as the well develops. In an established well that previously ran clear, new sand is not normal and usually signals a worn pump, a failed screen, or overpumping that should be diagnosed.

Why does my well produce sand only in summer?

Seasonal sand usually means drawdown. Heavier summer demand pulls the water level down farther, which increases the velocity of water entering the well and mobilizes sand from the valley-fill alluvium and fractured rock. Correct pump sizing and managing peak demand often resolve it.

Will sand ruin my pump?

Yes, over time. Sand is abrasive and grinds away pump impellers and seals, so a pump that should last well over a decade can fail much sooner. That is the main reason to address sandy water rather than live with it.

Can I just install a filter and ignore the well?

A filter or separator protects your fixtures and appliances, and for naturally sandy alluvial wells it may be the right long-term answer. But if the sand comes from a failing screen, casing breach, or worn pump, a filter only masks a problem that is still damaging the pump and well, so a proper diagnosis comes first.

Does Big Bear's cold climate affect well problems?

It can. At roughly 6,750 feet, Big Bear sees hard winter freezes that can crack exposed wellhead components and plumbing. Freeze damage combined with a sediment problem can knock a system offline in the dead of winter, so protecting the wellhead and addressing sand early both matter here.
