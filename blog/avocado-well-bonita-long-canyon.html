Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Bonita Long Canyon well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

A Suburban Community on Municipal Water, Not an Avocado Belt

Honesty matters, so here it is: Bonita Long Canyon is a master-planned suburban neighborhood, and almost every home in it is connected to municipal water through the Sweetwater Authority and Otay system. It is not a commercial avocado district, and it would be misleading to frame it as one. The wider Bonita and Sweetwater Valley area does have a real rural and equestrian past, with horse properties, citrus, and the occasional fruit tree, but the genuine San Diego avocado belt lies inland and north, in places like Fallbrook and Valley Center. The practical reason a Bonita-area owner reads a page like this is usually one specific thing: they have, or are considering, a private well on a larger estate lot or an older Sweetwater Valley parcel.

For those properties, geology and water economics both matter. The Bonita area sits in the lower Sweetwater River valley, with alluvial valley-floor deposits along the river and harder rock on the surrounding slopes and mesas. A private well's behavior depends on where the parcel sits relative to that valley fill. Domestic and large-lot irrigation wells in this part of southwestern San Diego County vary widely in depth and yield, and water quality can include hardness and, in places, salinity from the coastal-influenced lower valley. Because most neighbors are on city water, well owners here are a small minority, which makes experienced, honest service all the more important.

How a Large-Lot Well System Works

A private well on a Bonita-area estate lot follows the standard pattern: a submersible pump in the casing feeds a pressure tank that stores water under air pressure so the pump does not run with every faucet or sprinkler zone. A pressure switch controls the pump. On larger parcels irrigating lawn, citrus, ornamental landscape, or pasture, owners often add a storage tank and a booster or constant-pressure pump so multiple zones can run without pressure dropping out across the property.

Because so many properties here can fall back on municipal water, a common and sensible setup is a well used primarily for irrigation while the home stays on city water, or a well kept as a backup against outages and rate increases. Either way, the system needs the same care: a properly charged pressure tank, a reliable pressure switch, sediment filtration to protect emitters, and water treatment matched to a current water test if hardness or salinity is present. For any avocado or citrus hobby planting, we manage chloride sensitivity and salt buildup through testing and leaching irrigation, the same approach we would use on a true grove, just at a backyard scale.

Common Local Scenarios We See in the Bonita Area

  • Idle or backup wells that need recommissioning. Wells kept as standby behind city water often need cleaning, a pump, or rehabilitation before they perform.
  • Pressure-tank and switch failures. Waterlogged tanks and worn switches cause short-cycling, the fastest way to ruin a pump.
  • Hardness and scale. Mineral-rich water clogs emitters and scales water heaters and fixtures.
  • Salinity in the lower valley. Coastal-influenced groundwater can carry elevated salts, which stress sensitive plants and corrode plumbing.
  • Undersized irrigation supply. A well fine for a house can fall short when asked to run several large irrigation zones at once.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Read the pressure gauge. Note cut-in and cut-out pressures; an erratic band usually means the pressure tank lost its air charge.
  2. Listen for short-cycling. Rapid pump on/off points to a tank or switch problem rather than the pump itself.
  3. Check the breaker. A tripped dedicated circuit can look like a dead pump.
  4. Watch for stains or salt. Scale, rust, or a salty taste signals a water-quality issue we can test and treat.
  5. Run one zone at a time. If pressure holds alone but collapses with several zones, the supply may be undersized for current use.

Is a Well Worth It When City Water Is Available?

This is the honest question many Bonita-area owners should ask, and we will give you a straight answer. For a typical single-family lot already on municipal water, a private well rarely pays for itself. But for larger estate lots with significant irrigation, pasture, or a hobby orchard, a well can meaningfully cut water bills and provide independence during outages and drought restrictions. The math depends on your lot size, your irrigation demand, the depth and yield a well can achieve on your parcel, and current water rates. We evaluate all of that and tell you whether drilling, reviving an existing well, or simply staying on city water is the smart move. We would rather lose a sale than sell you a well you do not need.

If you do have an existing well, keeping it healthy is usually far cheaper than letting it fail and starting over. An idle well that sits for years can be rehabilitated, but neglect eventually wins. A modest annual checkup of the pump, tank, switch, and water quality protects the investment and keeps the option open.

The Sweetwater Valley Has Real Rural Roots

It is worth acknowledging the genuine agricultural history that gives the Bonita area its character. The Sweetwater Valley was once dotted with citrus groves and small farms, and to this day Bonita retains an equestrian identity, with horse trails, large lots, and a rural feel that sets it apart from the surrounding suburbs of Chula Vista. A handful of those older properties still have wells that date back to that era, and some owners keep horses, citrus, or a few fruit trees that benefit from an independent water source. If you own one of these legacy parcels, your well is part of that heritage, and keeping it serviceable preserves both a practical asset and a piece of the valley's working past. We respect that, and we treat these older systems with the care they deserve, repairing rather than replacing wherever it is sound to do so.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really private wells in Bonita Long Canyon?

Very few. Bonita Long Canyon is a suburban neighborhood on municipal water. Private wells in the broader Bonita and Sweetwater Valley area are mostly on older or larger estate parcels. If you own one, you are in a small minority and benefit from experienced local service.

Can I grow avocados here?

You can keep a backyard avocado tree, but this is not commercial avocado terrain like inland north county. If you do irrigate avocados, the main concerns are chloride sensitivity and salt buildup, which we manage with water testing and leaching irrigation.

My well has been sitting as a backup. Can it be revived?

Usually yes. Idle wells often need cleaning, a new pump, or rehabilitation to restore flow. We test the well and tell you honestly whether reviving it makes sense versus staying on city water.

Why is my well water hard or salty?

Groundwater in the lower Sweetwater Valley can carry hardness and, in coastal-influenced areas, elevated salinity. A water test pinpoints the issue, and a properly sized softener or treatment system addresses it.

Can a well handle all my irrigation zones at once?

Only if it is sized for that demand. Many home wells fall short when running several large zones simultaneously. A storage tank with a booster or constant-pressure pump solves the problem.
