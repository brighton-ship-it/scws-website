Low Water Pressure on a Running Springs Well: What's Really Going On

Running Springs sits high in the San Bernardino Mountains along Highway 18, between Lake Arrowhead and Big Bear Lake, at roughly 6,100 feet of elevation. Properties along Live Oak Drive, the Smiley Park area, and the neighborhoods feeding off Hunsaker Drive and Pioneer Drive are largely served by private wells and small mountain water systems because the terrain here is steep, forested, and well outside any large municipal grid. Wells in this part of San Bernardino County are typically drilled into fractured granite and decomposed granite (locally called "DG"), so water often comes from fracture zones rather than a deep, uniform sand-and-gravel aquifer. That geology is the single biggest reason weak water pressure behaves differently up here than it does on the valley floor in Redlands or San Bernardino proper.

When a Running Springs homeowner tells us "my pressure dropped," the cause is rarely the city main — there usually isn't one. It is almost always something in your own well, pump, pressure tank, or pressure switch. The good news is that those are exactly the components a licensed C-57 well contractor can diagnose and repair. The challenge is that elevation, freezing winters, and fractured-rock yield all stack the deck against a quick guess, which is why a methodical local diagnosis pays off.

How Your Running Springs Well Pressure System Actually Works

A private well does not push water straight to your faucet on demand. Instead, a submersible pump sits down in the borehole, often several hundred feet deep in this mountain granite, and pushes water up to a pressure tank near the house. That tank holds a cushion of compressed air over a rubber bladder. As water fills the tank, the air compresses and builds pressure; as you use water, the air pushes it back out. A pressure switch watches the tank and tells the pump when to kick on (commonly at 40 psi) and shut off (commonly at 60 psi). When every part of that chain is healthy, you get steady 40–60 psi at the tap. When one link weakens — a waterlogged tank, a worn switch, a tired pump, or a fracture that simply isn't yielding much water that season — pressure sags.

Mountain wells add wrinkles a valley well never sees. Long vertical lifts mean the pump works harder to overcome elevation just to reach the tank. Cold winters mean exposed lines, pressure tanks in unheated crawlspaces, and well houses are all at risk of freezing, which can throttle flow overnight and then "magically" recover by afternoon. And fractured-granite wells can have genuinely low natural yield, so a system that was borderline for years can tip into chronic low pressure after a dry winter.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Running Springs

From decades of work across San Bernardino County's high country, these are the culprits we find most often in Running Springs:

  • Waterlogged pressure tank — the most frequent single cause. When the bladder fails or the air charge bleeds off, the pump short-cycles, pressure swings wildly, and overall delivery drops.
  • Failing pressure switch — corroded or pitted contacts, or a switch set too low. Cheap to replace and a common quick win.
  • Worn or undersized submersible pump — after years of lifting water hundreds of feet, impellers wear and output falls. Mountain lifts are hard on pumps.
  • Low well yield from fractured granite — the well draws down faster than the fractures recharge, so pressure fades during heavy use or late in a dry year.
  • Sediment, sand, or mineral scale — DG aquifers shed fine sand, and hard mountain water leaves scale that clogs screens, filters, and aerator heads.
  • Frozen or partially frozen lines — a winter-only pressure problem that points straight at insulation and freeze protection.
  • Clogged whole-house or sediment filters — a neglected cartridge can strangle flow to the entire home.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks can help you describe the problem and sometimes solve it outright:

  1. Is it the whole house or one fixture? If only one faucet is weak, suspect a clogged aerator or fixture, not the well. If every tap is weak, the problem is system-wide.
  2. Watch your pressure gauge. Most systems have a gauge near the pressure tank. Healthy systems cycle between roughly 40 and 60 psi. If the needle barely moves, sits low, or bounces rapidly, note that — it tells us a lot.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off (short-cycling) almost always means a waterlogged tank or bad switch.
  4. Tap the pressure tank. The top should sound hollow and the bottom solid. If it sounds full of water all the way up, the bladder has likely failed.
  5. Check your filter. If you have a sediment or whole-house filter, a dark, clogged cartridge can be the whole story. Swap it and re-test.
  6. Rule out freezing in winter. If pressure is fine by afternoon but gone on cold mornings, you have a freeze issue, not a pump failure.

Please don't open the well cap, pull wiring, or adjust the pressure switch yourself — well systems run on 230 volts and the components are heavy and under pressure. The checks above are all you need to do before calling a pro.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Running Springs well pressure drop in winter but recover in the afternoon?

That pattern almost always means partial freezing of an exposed line, pressure tank, or wellhead overnight, which then thaws as temperatures rise. At 6,100 feet this is common. The fix is proper insulation, heat tape, or relocating vulnerable components — not a new pump.

How deep are wells in Running Springs?

Mountain wells here are typically drilled into fractured granite and often run several hundred feet deep to intersect productive water-bearing fractures. Depth varies a lot block to block because it depends on where the fractures are, which is exactly why local experience matters.

My pump runs constantly. Is that bad?

Yes. A pump that never shuts off is usually fighting a waterlogged pressure tank, a stuck switch, or a well that can't keep up with demand. Running constantly wears the pump out fast, so it is worth a prompt diagnosis.

Can low yield from a fractured-granite well be fixed without drilling a new one?

Often, yes. Hydrofracturing uses high-pressure water to open existing fractures and can meaningfully boost yield. Pairing that with a properly sized pressure tank or a constant-pressure system frequently solves chronic low pressure at a fraction of the cost of a new well.

Is sandy or gritty water a pressure problem?

It can become one. Decomposed-granite aquifers shed fine sand that clogs screens, filters, and fixtures, gradually choking flow. Proper sediment filtration protects your pump and your pressure.
