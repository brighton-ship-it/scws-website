What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Calimesa Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Wells in Calimesa

Calimesa sits at the northwestern edge of Riverside County, where the Yucaipa Valley landform steps down toward the Beaumont plain and the San Gorgonio Pass opens to the east. Many properties on the hillsides and rural fringes of Calimesa rely on private wells, and the geology beneath them is more complex than in a simple valley basin. If your Calimesa well has lost production, hydrofracturing is often the most economical way to restore it—provided your well draws from the right formation.

The groundwater system here is part of the Yucaipa subbasin, which geologists describe in layers: a crystalline basement at the bottom, overlain by consolidated sedimentary rock, then unconsolidated alluvial-fan sediment, and finally surficial deposits at the top. The valley fill—those middle-Pleistocene alluvial-fan and braidplain deposits—is the primary aquifer in the basin's center. But on the slopes and where wells reach down into the fractured crystalline basement, the water moves through cracks in hard rock. That fractured bedrock is exactly what hydrofracturing is built to improve. The area is also cut by the Banning fault, which has been mapped west from Calimesa, and faulting locally influences how water-bearing fractures connect.

How Hydrofracturing Works

The technique is purely mechanical. We lower an inflatable packer into your well casing to seal off a target section of the borehole, then inject clean water into that isolated zone at pressures usually between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. The high pressure flushes silt, clay, and mineral scale out of the existing fractures and forces those fractures to open wider and reach farther into the surrounding rock—creating fresh pathways for groundwater to flow toward your well.

By sealing one zone at a time, we direct the full force of the water exactly where it will help instead of losing it up the casing. Deeper wells may be treated in several stages. The whole process generally runs four to eight hours, and only clean water ever enters the well.

Why Calimesa Wells Lose Yield

Several local factors contribute to declining production:

  • Fracture clogging in basement rock: Over years of pumping, fine sediment and mineral scale narrow the cracks that feed a hard-rock well.
  • Regional groundwater decline: Pumping demand across the Yucaipa subbasin and dry-year drawdown lower water levels, leaving marginal wells short.
  • Complex, faulted geology: The basin's steep slopes and fault structure mean some wells were completed in rock with only limited fracture connection from the start.
  • Growing demand: Added landscaping, livestock, or a second dwelling can outrun a well that was adequate decades ago.

What to Check and Signs You Need It

Look for these indicators before the well fails completely:

  • The pump runs much longer than it used to in order to refill the pressure tank.
  • Pressure falls off in the afternoon or whenever multiple fixtures run at once.
  • Faucets spit air, which means the water level is dropping to the pump intake.
  • The well recovers slowly and needs hours to bounce back after heavy use.
  • Overall production is clearly lower than in past years.

A simple test: time how long the pump runs to refill the tank when the system is idle, then again during peak demand. A big difference signals a recovery problem in the formation, which is precisely what hydrofracturing addresses.

When to Call a Professional

Low water is not always a formation issue. A failing submersible pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, a stuck pressure switch, or a leaking drop pipe can all look like a dying well. We start every job with a $125 diagnostic—credited toward any work we perform—to identify the real cause first. If an inexpensive part solves the problem, that is what we will recommend. When the borehole is sound but the formation simply cannot keep up, hydrofracturing is the right next step before considering a costly new well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing a good fit for Calimesa's geology?

It can be, but it depends on what your well is finished in. Wells that reach the fractured crystalline basement beneath the Yucaipa subbasin respond well to hydrofracturing. Wells producing from the loose unconsolidated alluvial-fan sediments that fill much of the valley are not good candidates, because that material will not hold open fractures. Our assessment determines which applies to your well.

How is this different from oil-field fracking?

Completely different. Water-well hydrofracturing injects only clean water under pressure to clean and open natural rock fractures. There are no chemicals, no proppant sand, and no gels. We disinfect the well afterward and recommend a bacteria test before it goes back into full service.

What does hydrofracturing cost in the Calimesa area?

Typically $3,000-$8,000 depending on well depth and the number of zones treated. By comparison, a new turnkey well in Riverside County usually runs $18,000-$42,000. Rehabilitating your existing well is the cost-effective first option.

How long does the treatment take?

Most Calimesa jobs take four to eight hours from setup through the final flow test. We measure your baseline yield first, treat the target zones, and re-test before leaving so you can see the change.

Will my water be cleaner after hydrofracturing?

The goal is more water, not necessarily different water chemistry, but flushing silt and scale out of the fractures often reduces sediment and turbidity issues as a side benefit. If you have separate water-quality concerns, those are addressed with treatment equipment, which we can also advise on.

Do I need to be on-site during the work?

It helps to have someone available at the start so we can confirm access and discuss findings, but you do not have to stand by all day. We will walk you through the before-and-after results and document the improvement when the job is complete.

Can you treat a well that has lost yield gradually over many years?

Yes, and those are often the best candidates. A well that has slowly declined over a decade or more has usually not run out of groundwater; the fractures feeding it have simply narrowed with accumulated silt and mineral scale. Reopening and extending that fracture network frequently recovers much of the lost capacity, and the documented before-and-after flow test shows you exactly how much you regained.
