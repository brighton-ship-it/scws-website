Understanding Short Cycling

Short cycling occurs when your pump turns on and off more frequently than normal—sometimes every few seconds or minutes instead of running for several minutes per cycle. A healthy well system should cycle perhaps 1-2 times per hour during normal use, running for several minutes each cycle. Frequent cycling wastes electricity, wears out pump components prematurely, and can burn out your motor. If you notice your pressure gauge rapidly fluctuating or hear the pump constantly clicking on and off, you have a short cycling problem that needs immediate attention.

Pressure Tank Problems

The most common cause of short cycling is a failed or waterlogged pressure tank. Bladder tanks have an air chamber separated from water by a rubber bladder. Over time, bladders can rupture, or air can slowly leak out through the valve. Without that air cushion, there's no reserve to maintain pressure when you open a faucet—pressure drops instantly, triggering the pump. Older galvanized tanks can also become waterlogged when internal corrosion destroys the air/water separation. A tank that feels completely solid when knocked on, or one that sprays water from its air valve, needs replacement.

Pressure Switch Issues

Your pressure switch controls when the pump turns on (usually 30-40 PSI) and off (usually 50-60 PSI). Corroded contacts can cause intermittent connections—the pump starts, contacts bounce, pump stops, contacts reconnect. Debris or insects inside the switch can also interfere. Sometimes the pressure adjustment nuts work loose, narrowing the on/off gap. If your cut-in and cut-out pressures are too close together (less than 15 PSI apart), the pump will cycle frequently. Try cleaning contacts with fine sandpaper or replace the switch entirely.

Other Causes and Solutions

Check for leaks anywhere in your system—a running toilet, dripping faucet, or leaky irrigation valve will cause continuous small pressure drops and frequent cycling. A check valve that's stuck partially open lets water flow backward down the well, dropping pressure and triggering the pump. Undersized pressure tanks for your household demand can also cause frequent cycling even when working properly. For homes with high water usage, upgrading to a larger tank (85+ gallons) reduces cycling stress on your pump. If troubleshooting these issues doesn't help, your pump motor itself may be failing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my well pump turn on and off every few seconds?

Rapid cycling every few seconds almost always indicates a waterlogged pressure tank. When the tank's air bladder fails or loses its air charge, there's no cushion to maintain pressure between pump cycles. The pump turns on, pressure rises instantly, pump shuts off, pressure drops immediately, and the cycle repeats. This is hard on your pump motor and can burn it out.

How do I check if my pressure tank is waterlogged?

With the pump off and pressure relieved, push the air valve on top of the tank. If water sprays out, your tank is waterlogged. You can also tap the tank from top to bottom—a good tank sounds hollow at top (air) and solid at bottom (water). A waterlogged tank sounds solid throughout or has the water/air levels reversed.

Can a bad pressure switch cause short cycling?

Yes. A failing pressure switch may read pressure incorrectly, causing erratic on/off behavior. Contacts can become pitted or corroded, creating inconsistent connections. The pressure settings may also have drifted. Try adjusting or replacing the switch—it's a $20-40 part and simple repair before assuming the tank has failed.
