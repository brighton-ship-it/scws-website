What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mead Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Mead Valley

Mead Valley is an unincorporated community in Riverside County, just west of Perris and south of the city of Riverside, where the rolling granitic hills of the Perris block meet the valley floor. Many properties here, the larger rural lots, ranchettes, and parcels tucked into the hills, rely on private wells rather than a municipal connection. The good news for Mead Valley well owners is that the local geology is well suited to hydrofracturing: much of the area is underlain by hard granitic rock, exactly the kind of fractured bedrock the technique is designed to improve.

Hydrofracturing, sometimes spelled hydrofracking, injects high-pressure water into a low-yield bedrock well to reopen the natural fractures that carry water to it. On the granitic terrain around Mead Valley and Perris, it has a strong record of restoring lost yield, and it costs a small fraction of what a new well would.

How the Process Works

A well in granite produces from cracks, not from an underground pool. Water moves through fracture seams in the rock to reach the borehole, and the well yields only as much as those seams allow. With years of pumping, the fractures closest to the well clog with sediment and mineral scale, and production falls off. Hydrofracturing clears that bottleneck. We set an inflatable packer to seal off a section of the well and inject clean water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI, flushing the clogged fractures and opening tight cracks nearby so the borehole reconnects to water it had lost. No chemicals are used, and the field work is typically completed in four to eight hours.

Mead Valley Geology: Granite That Favors Hydrofracturing

Mead Valley sits in the Perris block of the northern Peninsular Ranges, an area dominated by granitic bedrock, including the biotite-hornblende tonalite of the Val Verde pluton, overlain in places by older alluvial fan deposits. For hydrofracturing, that is favorable ground. Wells drilled into the granitic rock of the surrounding hills produce through fractures, and those fractures are what a hydrofracture cleans and opens. Where a well is finished in the shallower alluvial fan material rather than the underlying granite, the picture is more mixed, and a well drawing purely from loose sediment is a weaker candidate. We read your well log and run a downhole camera when needed to confirm you are tapping the fractured granite before recommending treatment, but in the Mead Valley area, more often than not, the geology is on your side.

Signs Your Mead Valley Well May Need Treatment

  • Declining yield compared with previous years.
  • Running dry during irrigation or peak household demand.
  • Slow recovery after the pump shuts off.
  • Frequent pump cycling from a borehole that cannot keep up.
  • A new granite well that never produced the gallons-per-minute you expected.

Realistic Results in Riverside County

In the granitic rock typical of Mead Valley and the broader Perris area, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of treated bedrock wells, with successful jobs commonly raising flow two to five times above baseline. These are honest ranges, because the exact fracture pattern underground is never fully visible. We safeguard the outcome with measurement, baselining your flow before treatment and re-testing afterward, so you receive a documented before-and-after rather than a promise.

Our Step-by-Step Process

  1. Diagnostic and flow test ($125, credited) establishing your current yield.
  2. Camera inspection to confirm casing and pinpoint productive fracture zones.
  3. Hydrofracturing staged high-pressure injection, usually a single day on site.
  4. Post-treatment flow test after the well settles over 24 to 48 hours.
  5. Documented report a clear before-and-after comparison.

Cost Versus a New Well

Hydrofracturing typically costs $3,000-$8,000. A new turnkey well in Riverside County generally runs $18,000-$42,000 depending on depth, casing, and pump, and a replacement pump, if yours is also failing, is about $2,500-$5,500. Given the granitic geology here and that price gap, trying hydrofracturing first on a sound bedrock well is almost always the smart financial move.

When to Consider Deepening or Redrilling Instead

Even in favorable granite, hydrofracturing is not right for every well, and we will say so. If a camera shows failed casing, a collapsing borehole, or a well that never reached a producing fracture, deepening or drilling new is the honest recommendation. A well drawing only from shallow alluvial fan sediment, similarly, has little for the process to open. We would rather give you that straight guidance than charge for a treatment unlikely to help.

Why the Perris Block Responds Well

The reason wells around Mead Valley tend to respond to hydrofracturing is the character of the rock. The granitic tonalite of the Perris block is naturally jointed and fractured, a product of the same geologic forces that shaped the Peninsular Ranges, so a well drilled into it intersects a network of cracks that a hydrofracture can clean and extend. That is a meaningful advantage over deep-basin settings elsewhere in the region, where wells draw from sediment with no fractures to open. When you call us about a Mead Valley well, the first thing we confirm is that you are in that fractured granite, because that is where the technique earns its keep.

Recovery Rate and Summer Reliability

Owners often describe a struggling well purely in gallons per minute, but how fast the well recovers between draws frequently matters more for daily life. A well can show a fair instantaneous flow yet still run a property short if it draws down quickly and refills slowly. By widening the fracture network feeding the borehole, hydrofracturing tends to improve both the peak flow and the recovery rate, and that faster rebound is often what carries a Mead Valley household, garden, and any livestock through the hot Riverside County summers without rationing.

Catching Decline Early on an Older Well

Many of the wells we service around Mead Valley and Perris were drilled years ago, and owners sometimes wait until a well nearly quits before calling. That is usually a costly delay. A granite well that is still structurally sound can typically be revived with a single hydrofracture, pushing the much larger expense of a new well well into the future, but only if the casing and borehole are still in good shape. Once those degrade, rehabilitation may no longer be an option. Calling at the first signs of declining yield keeps the cheaper, less disruptive fix available and gives the treatment its best chance of a lasting result.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Mead Valley area good for hydrofracturing?

Generally yes. Much of Mead Valley sits on granitic bedrock of the Perris block, which is exactly the fractured rock hydrofracturing is designed to improve. We confirm your well is in that granite before recommending treatment.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. It relies on clean water under pressure only, so it does not affect your water quality the way petroleum fracking can.

How much more water can I expect?

Successful bedrock treatments commonly raise yield two to five times, though the rock determines the result. We measure your well before and after so the gain is documented.

How does the cost compare to a new well?

Hydrofracturing runs about $3,000-$8,000 versus $18,000-$42,000 for a new turnkey well, which is why we recommend trying it first on a sound granite well.

Will the improvement last?

Often for many years. Minerals slowly re-accumulate with pumping, so some wells benefit from a repeat treatment later, but a single hydrofracture frequently lasts the better part of a decade.

How quickly can you come out?

We provide same-day emergency service for non-producing wells and schedule routine diagnostics promptly across Riverside County.
