Well Water Has a Salty Taste – Understanding the Causes

A salty or brackish taste in your well water is more than just unpleasant – it can indicate equipment problems, environmental changes, or contamination issues that need attention. Understanding why your water tastes salty helps you choose the right solution and protect your health.

The causes of salty well water range from simple equipment malfunctions to serious aquifer contamination. Let's explore each possibility and the appropriate response.

Common Causes of Salty Well Water

Water Softener Problems

The most common cause of suddenly salty water is a malfunctioning water softener. Water softeners use salt (sodium chloride or potassium chloride) to remove hardness minerals. When something goes wrong, excess salt enters your water supply.

Common softener issues:

  • Stuck valve: The softener fails to rinse properly after regeneration
  • Incorrect settings: Regeneration cycle set too long or too frequently
  • Salt bridging: Salt forms a crust in the brine tank, causing irregular regeneration
  • Drain line blockage: Brine can't drain properly during regeneration
  • Control valve failure: Valve doesn't switch between cycles correctly

Quick test: Bypass your water softener temporarily. If the salty taste disappears, the softener is the culprit.

Seawater Intrusion

For coastal areas, including much of Southern California, seawater intrusion into freshwater aquifers is a serious and growing concern. This occurs when:

  • Excessive groundwater pumping lowers the water table
  • Seawater migrates inland to fill the void
  • Wells draw from the contaminated zone

Seawater intrusion is typically gradual, with saltiness increasing over months or years. It's often worse during dry periods when groundwater levels are lowest.

Road Salt Contamination

In areas where road salt is used for winter ice control, contamination can occur when:

  • Salt-laden runoff infiltrates near the well
  • Salt storage areas contaminate groundwater
  • Improper well construction allows surface water entry

This type of contamination often shows seasonal patterns, worsening in late winter and spring as snow melts.

Natural Mineral Content

Some aquifers naturally contain elevated levels of sodium, chloride, or other minerals that create a salty taste. This is common in:

  • Areas with ancient marine sediments
  • Deeper aquifers with long water residence times
  • Regions with evaporite mineral deposits

Natural salt content is usually consistent over time, unlike contamination or equipment issues which cause sudden changes.

Testing Your Water

To determine the cause of salty water, testing is essential:

Basic chloride test: Measures chloride ions, the primary contributor to salty taste. Normal drinking water contains less than 250 mg/L chloride.

Sodium test: Important for health concerns. EPA recommends below 20 mg/L for those on sodium-restricted diets.

Total Dissolved Solids (TDS): Measures overall mineral content. TDS above 500 mg/L often tastes salty or brackish.

Conductivity: Quick screening test that correlates with salinity levels.

For suspected seawater intrusion, additional tests may include specific ion ratios that distinguish seawater from other salt sources.

Treatment Options

Fix the Water Softener

If your softener is the problem:

  • Check and clear salt bridges in the brine tank
  • Verify timer and regeneration settings
  • Inspect and clean the drain line
  • Have the control valve serviced or replaced
  • Consider switching to potassium chloride for lower sodium

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

RO systems effectively remove salt and other dissolved minerals. Options include:

  • Point-of-use RO: Under-sink system for drinking water
  • Whole-house RO: Complete treatment but expensive and water-intensive

Distillation

Distillation removes all dissolved minerals including salt. Practical for drinking water only due to slow production rates.

Blending

If you have access to a lower-salinity water source, blending can reduce overall salt content to acceptable levels.

New Well

In cases of seawater intrusion or natural salinity, drilling a new well to a different aquifer may be the only long-term solution.

Health Implications

While salty water is unpleasant, health effects depend on the concentration and individual sensitivity:

General population: Most people can tolerate higher sodium levels without immediate effects, though long-term consumption of very high levels isn't recommended.

Sodium-restricted diets: Those with hypertension, heart disease, or kidney problems should avoid water with sodium above 20 mg/L. This includes water softened with sodium chloride.

Infants: High sodium water shouldn't be used for formula preparation.

Plants and landscaping: Salt damages most plants. Use unsoftened water for gardens and avoid irrigating with high-sodium water.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my well water suddenly become salty?

Sudden salt taste is usually caused by water softener malfunction. Check if your softener is cycling properly and try bypassing it. If that's not the cause, contamination or aquifer changes may be responsible.

Can I drink water that tastes salty?

Mild saltiness is generally safe for most people, but those on sodium-restricted diets should avoid it. Have your water tested to determine actual sodium levels before making decisions.

Will a water filter remove salt?

Standard carbon filters don't remove salt. You need reverse osmosis, distillation, or specialized ion exchange systems to remove dissolved sodium and chloride.

Can seawater intrusion be reversed?

Seawater intrusion is difficult to reverse and typically requires regional groundwater management efforts. For individual wells, deepening, relocating, or treating the water are usually the options.

Should I use potassium chloride instead of sodium chloride in my softener?

Potassium chloride produces softened water with potassium instead of sodium, which is better for those on sodium-restricted diets. However, it costs more and those with kidney problems should consult their doctor about potassium intake.
