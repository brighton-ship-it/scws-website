What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your March Air Reserve Base Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells Around March Air Reserve Base

If a well that serves a property near March Air Reserve Base is producing less water than it once did, hydrofracturing (also spelled hydrofracking) is one of the first options worth weighing before you commit to the cost of a new well. The technique injects high-pressure water into the borehole to clear and widen the natural fractures in bedrock, opening fresh pathways for groundwater to reach your pump. But March sits in a part of Riverside County where the geology changes quickly from one parcel to the next, so honest candidacy screening matters here more than almost anywhere else we work.

Where March Air Reserve Base Sits, Geologically

March Air Reserve Base lies in northwestern Riverside County, wedged between the cities of Riverside, Moreno Valley, and Perris on the broad lowland known as the Perris Block. Much of the immediate area is flat valley floor filled with deep alluvium — layered sand, silt, and gravel washed down from the surrounding ranges. Toward the edges, near the Box Springs Mountains to the north and the rising ground toward Reche Canyon and the Lakeview Mountains to the east and south, that alluvium thins and you reach fractured granitic and crystalline bedrock. That difference decides whether hydrofracturing will help you.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

The method is straightforward. After a camera inspection, we lower an inflatable packer into the well and seal off a chosen section of the borehole. We then inject clean water above that seal at pressures that commonly reach 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The pressurized water pushes into the rock's existing hairline fractures, flushes out the silt and mineral scale plugging them, and widens the connected network so water moves toward the well more freely. We may treat several zones in sequence down the hole. The whole job uses nothing but water, and it typically takes four to eight hours.

Why a March-Area Well Loses Yield

Low production around the base usually traces back to one of a handful of causes, and not all of them are solved by hydrofracturing:

  • A dropping regional water table. Years of drought and heavy pumping across the Perris and San Jacinto groundwater areas have lowered static levels. If your well is simply chasing a falling table, fracturing the rock will not put water back into the basin.
  • Plugged fractures in bedrock. Wells finished in the granitic rock along the margins often lose yield because the fine fissures feeding them have silted up or scaled over. This is the classic case hydrofracturing fixes.
  • Encrusted screen or perforations. Iron, manganese, and calcium scale can choke the intake even when the aquifer is healthy.
  • A worn pump or undersized equipment. Sometimes the well is fine and the pump is the problem.

Honest Candidacy: Valley Floor vs. Bedrock Margin

Here is the part many contractors skip. If your well is bottomed entirely in the loose alluvial sand and gravel of the valley floor, hydrofracturing is a weak option. High-pressure water does not create lasting fractures in unconsolidated sediment — there is no rock to crack. For those wells, the better path is usually deepening the existing borehole to reach a more productive layer, or drilling a new well sited and screened correctly. We would rather tell you that up front than take your money on a treatment unlikely to last.

On the other hand, wells that reach fractured granite or crystalline rock along the foothill margins are excellent candidates. Those are exactly the wells where we routinely see two to five times the flow after treatment. If you are not sure which type you have, your driller's well completion report and our camera survey will tell us within an hour.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of looking can save a service call or sharpen the one you make:

  • Find your well log. The State of California keeps well completion reports, and you may have a copy in your closing paperwork. It tells us the depth and whether you are in rock or sediment.
  • Note when the trouble shows up. Sputtering air at the tap or a well that recovers slowly after heavy use points to a supply problem. Steady low pressure everywhere points more often to the pump, pressure tank, or pressure switch.
  • Check the pressure tank and switch. A waterlogged tank or failing switch (a $150–$350 part) causes rapid pump cycling that mimics a failing well.
  • Watch for sediment or staining. Sandy or rust-colored water suggests screen or scale issues that filtration ($300–$900) or iron/manganese treatment ($1,500–$3,500) may address.

The Perris Block and What It Means for Your Well

The ground under March Air Reserve Base belongs to a geologic structure called the Perris Block, a relatively stable wedge of crust bounded by major fault zones. Across the block, basement granitic rock lies below a cover of alluvium that ranges from very deep near the valley centers to nearly absent along the ridges. For well owners this matters in a practical way: two properties only a mile apart can have completely different wells — one drawing from sand and gravel, the next bottomed in fractured granite. Because hydrofracturing only rewards the second kind, we never quote it sight unseen. Pulling your well completion report and running a quick camera survey tells us in minutes whether you sit on the kind of rock that responds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing safe for a well near March Air Reserve Base?

Yes. The process uses clean water only — no chemicals — and has been used on residential and agricultural wells for decades. It will not contaminate the aquifer, and on a sound steel casing it does no harm. We run a camera survey first so we never pressurize a section of casing that is corroded or cracked.

My well is in the flat ground near the base. Is it a good candidate?

Maybe not. Wells finished entirely in the loose alluvial sand and gravel of the valley floor around March do not fracture well, because there is no solid rock to open. Hydrofracturing shines when your well reaches the fractured granitic rock along the Box Springs and Reche Canyon margins. We check your well log before we recommend it.

How much can hydrofracturing improve my yield?

On a well bottomed in fractured bedrock, a typical result is a two to five times increase in flow. Wells in pure sand or that are simply pumping the water table down see little benefit, and in those cases we will tell you so and discuss deepening or a new well instead.

How long does the job take?

Most treatments are completed in a single day, usually four to eight hours on site, followed by a verifying flow test within 24 to 48 hours so you can see the before-and-after numbers.

Is hydrofracturing cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always, when it is the right tool. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000–$8,000, while a new turnkey well in the Riverside County inland area runs $18,000–$42,000. Trying it first on a good bedrock candidate can save you tens of thousands of dollars.
