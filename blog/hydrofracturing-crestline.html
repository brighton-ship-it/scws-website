What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Crestline Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Crestline Mountain Wells

Crestline sits high in the San Bernardino Mountains of San Bernardino County, a forested community around Lake Gregory at roughly a mile of elevation. Up here, the ground is granite and fractured crystalline bedrock—the hard rock of the San Bernardino range—and many mountain properties in and around Crestline, Valley of Enchantment, and the ridges toward Lake Arrowhead and Rim of the World depend on private wells drilled into that rock. Mountain wells live and die by their fracture network, which is exactly why hydrofracturing (also written hydrofracking) is such a valuable tool here. When a Crestline well starts to fade, fracturing is usually the most cost-effective way to bring it back without the expense and difficulty of drilling a new well on a steep, forested lot.

Southern California Well Service has worked on mountain and foothill wells across San Bernardino County since 2020. We understand how Crestline's granite behaves, how seasonal snowmelt and dry summers swing the water table, and why a well that produced well for years can slow to a trickle—and what it takes to restore it.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates a well using clean, high-pressure water and nothing else. Despite sharing a name with oil-and-gas fracking, it has nothing in common with it: there are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no additives—only water pressure, applied where it is needed.

Our crew lowers inflatable packers into the well casing and inflates them to seal off a chosen section of the borehole. We then pump clean water into that isolated interval at pressures typically between 1,500 and 3,000+ PSI. That pressure forces water out into the existing fractures and joints in the granite, scouring out the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have clogged them and widening the natural passages so groundwater can flow into the well again. We treat one zone at a time and follow with a flow test to measure the improvement.

This is the ideal technique for Crestline's geology. A granite well is essentially a borehole intersecting a network of cracks; the more open and connected those cracks are, the more water the well produces. Hydrofracturing reopens and extends that fracture network, which is why mountain hard-rock wells are often the strongest candidates for the treatment.

Crestline's elevation adds another wrinkle worth understanding. Recharge here comes largely from winter precipitation and snowmelt soaking into the fractured rock, so a mountain well is sensitive to both the season and the rock's ability to transmit water. When fractures clog, the well cannot capture the recharge that is actually present in the formation. Clearing those fractures lets the well make full use of the water the mountain provides, which is often the difference between a marginal well and a dependable one.

Why Crestline Wells Lose Yield

Around Crestline and the surrounding San Bernardino Mountain communities, declining wells usually come down to a few causes:

  • Fractures sealed by mineral deposits. Over years of use, minerals precipitate inside the granite fractures and on the well screen, slowly closing the pathways that supply the well.
  • Sediment and fines clogging the borehole. Decomposed granite produces fine grit that can pack into fractures and around the casing.
  • Marginal fracture connectivity. Some mountain wells were always modest producers because they intersected only a limited set of water-bearing fractures.
  • Seasonal water-table swings. Mountain water levels rise with winter snowmelt and fall through dry summers and drought years, leaving some wells short in late season.
  • Old wells that have never been rehabilitated. Many Crestline-area wells are decades old and have never been cleaned or redeveloped.

Hydrofracturing directly addresses sealed fractures, sediment, and marginal connectivity. Where the underlying problem is a seasonal or drought-driven drop in the water table, we will tell you honestly and discuss deepening or other options rather than overselling a fracture job.

Signs Your Crestline Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Mountain wells often fade most noticeably in late summer and fall, after the snowmelt recharge has drained away. Watch for:

  • The well produces clearly less water than it did a few years ago.
  • It draws down fast and the pump short-cycles or runs dry during heavy use.
  • Water pressure at the cabin or home has weakened.
  • Recovery is slow—the well takes much longer to refill after pumping.
  • You notice more grit or cloudiness in the water.
  • A newly drilled mountain well came in weaker than expected.

What to Check Before You Call

A few details speed the diagnosis. Note when the decline started and whether it tracks with the dry season. Watch for pump short-cycling. Find any records of the well's depth, original yield, or completion report. And be ready to describe your usage—a weekend cabin and a full-time mountain home place very different demands on a well. Sharing how the well has behaved across recent seasons helps us target the treatment and restore reliable flow more quickly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean, pressurized water. There are no chemicals, sand proppants, or additives. It is a safe, long-established method for rehabilitating water wells.

Will it work on a granite mountain well in Crestline?

Often very well. Crestline wells are drilled into fractured granite, the formation where hydrofracturing is most effective, because the well's yield depends directly on its fracture network. We run a flow test and assess construction first to confirm your well is a good candidate.

How much can my yield improve?

Results vary by well, but many granite wells see a meaningful increase in flow once the plugged fractures are reopened. We document before-and-after yield with flow tests so you see the real improvement.

How long does the process take?

A typical job takes about four to eight hours on site, and we usually verify the improved yield with a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well on a mountain lot?

Almost always, and the gap is often wider in the mountains because drilling and access on a steep forested lot add cost. Hydrofracturing typically runs $3,000–$8,000 versus $18,000–$42,000 for a new turnkey well.

My well only struggles in late summer—can you help?

Often, yes. If your well draws down hard after the snowmelt recharge fades, reopening the fracture network can improve both yield and recovery. If the cause is a seasonal water-table drop, we will tell you honestly and discuss deepening.
