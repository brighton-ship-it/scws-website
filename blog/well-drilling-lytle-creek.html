The Complete Turnkey Well Drilling Process

A properly constructed water well is a multi-step project that begins well before the drill bit touches the ground and ends with final inspections and state-required documentation. Here is what the full process looks like when Southern California Well Service handles your Lytle Creek well project from start to finish.

Step 1: Site Assessment and Geology Review

Before we schedule drilling, our crew visits the property to assess access, topography, proximity to potential contamination sources (septic systems, fuel storage, animal areas), and local geology indicators. In Lytle Creek's canyon environment, site access is a real consideration: steep driveways, narrow roads, and hillside lots require our rigs to be positioned carefully. We review California DWR well completion records for neighboring properties, examine surface geology, and advise on the most likely aquifer target for your specific parcel. This assessment informs everything that follows, from the permit application to the choice of drilling method.

Step 2: San Bernardino County Well Permit

Because Lytle Creek is unincorporated territory in San Bernardino County, well permits are issued by San Bernardino County Environmental Health Services (EHS), which is the county's local enforcing agency under California's Water Well Standards (Bulletin 74-81 and 74-90). The permit application requires a plot plan showing the well location within a 500-foot radius, the proposed well depth, casing material, annular seal depth, intended use, and estimated pump rate. We prepare and submit the entire permit package on your behalf. Permit approval typically takes two to four weeks, though timelines can vary with workload at the county office. Permit fees range from $300 to $1,200 depending on well type.

Step 3: Drilling Method Selection — Air Rotary vs. Mud Rotary

The right drilling method depends on what is underground. Lytle Creek's geology presents two primary conditions, and we match our approach to the actual site:

  • Air rotary drilling is used for hard bedrock conditions, specifically the fractured granodiorite and granite of the San Gabriel Mountains crystalline basement. Air rotary uses compressed air to lift cuttings out of the borehole and is ideal for penetrating and sampling fractured rock aquifers. It produces a dry borehole where formation water naturally flows from fractures when they are intersected.
  • Mud rotary drilling is preferred for alluvial sediment zones near the Lytle Creek wash floor, where loose sand, gravel, and silt require a bentonite drilling fluid to stabilize the borehole walls during drilling and until the casing and gravel pack are installed.

Our Gefco rotary drill rig is capable of reaching depths beyond 1,000 feet, giving us the flexibility to chase productive water-bearing fractures in hard rock or fully case through deep alluvial sequences.

Step 4: Casing and Well Construction

Once the borehole is drilled to the target depth, we install the well casing to protect the water-bearing zone and prevent surface contamination from entering the well. In rock wells, steel casing is set through the overburden layer and grouted in place with a sanitary seal; the open rock section below may be left uncased where the formation is competent. In alluvial wells, perforated PVC or steel casing is set opposite the water-bearing sand and gravel layers, and a gravel pack is placed in the annular space around the perforated section to filter fine sediment and maximize yield. A continuous concrete grout seal is placed from the surface down to the top of the gravel pack to prevent shallow water intrusion.

Step 5: Well Development

After casing and cementing are complete, the well must be developed to remove drilling fluid, fine material, and filter cake from the formation, and to restore the natural permeability of the aquifer immediately adjacent to the well screen. We use surge blocks, air surging, and high-volume pumping to clean the well and measure stabilized yield. Proper development is critical for long-term productivity and water quality.

Step 6: Pump and Pressure System Installation

Once the well is developed and tested, we install a submersible pump sized to match the well's yield, your household demand, and the depth to water. The pump assembly includes the pump motor, drop pipe, wiring, and pitless adapter. Above ground, we install a pressure tank, pressure switch, electrical controls, and connect to your home's plumbing. The pressure tank maintains consistent water pressure and protects the pump from short-cycling. We select only quality pumps from established manufacturers and size the system to provide adequate flow for your home, irrigation, or livestock use.

Step 7: Final Inspection and California DWR Completion Report

California law requires that the licensed well driller file a Well Completion Report (DWR Form 188) with the California Department of Water Resources within 60 days of completing any new well. This report documents the well's construction details, geology encountered, static water level, yield test results, and pump information. A copy goes to San Bernardino County EHS as well. We handle filing the completion report as part of every project. The county may conduct a final inspection before the well is placed in service. Once approved, you have a legal, properly documented water well that will appear in the state's groundwater database.

Lytle Creek Geology and Expected Well Depths

Understanding the geology beneath Lytle Creek is essential for setting realistic expectations about drilling depth, well yield, and cost. The community occupies a narrow canyon cut through the western Transverse Ranges, and the rock record shifts significantly depending on where on the canyon floor or walls your property sits.

Alluvial Deposits of the Lytle Creek Wash

The Lytle Creek stream has deposited thick sequences of sand, gravel, cobbles, and finer sediments along the canyon floor over thousands of years. These alluvial materials form a productive shallow aquifer that connects downcanyon to the broader Rialto-Colton groundwater basin, one of the most important water storage units in the Inland Empire. Wells drilled into the alluvial zone near the creek can encounter water at relatively shallow depths. California DWR well completion records for Lytle Creek show an average well depth of approximately 119 feet, with the shallowest wells on record completed at around 39 feet. These shallow alluvial completions reflect productive zones in the gravel and sand layers deposited by the creek.

Fractured Crystalline Bedrock

Away from the canyon floor and up the canyon walls, the geology transitions quickly to the fractured granodiorite and granite of the San Gabriel Mountains crystalline basement. These Transverse Ranges basement rocks underlie the entire region. Water in bedrock wells occurs in fractures, joints, and fault zones rather than in pore spaces, so yield depends entirely on intersecting productive fractures during drilling. Bedrock wells in Lytle Creek and surrounding mountain terrain typically range from 150 to 500 feet or more, and some may go deeper to reach fractures with adequate yield. The range of 39 to 500 feet documented in the DWR records reflects this dual geology: shallow alluvial wells on the canyon floor and deeper bedrock wells on hillside and ridge parcels.

The distinction matters because it affects which drilling method we use, how much casing we set, and ultimately what your well will cost. Our pre-drilling site assessment is specifically designed to evaluate which geological environment your parcel sits in before we ever start the rig.

San Bernardino County Well Permitting: What to Expect

All water wells in Lytle Creek require a permit from San Bernardino County Environmental Health Services before drilling begins. EHS is the designated local enforcing agency under California's well construction laws and enforces the state's Water Well Standards as well as the county's own groundwater ordinance. Here is a summary of the permitting process and what the timeline looks like:

  • Permit application: We prepare and submit the application to EHS, which must include a site plan showing the proposed well location, setback distances from property lines and potential contamination sources, proposed casing and annular seal design, and a description of intended use.
  • County review: EHS reviews the application for compliance with well siting standards, including minimum setback requirements from septic systems (100 feet), fuel tanks, and other contamination sources. Processing time typically runs two to four weeks but can vary.
  • Permit issuance: Once approved, the permit is issued and drilling may proceed. Permit fees in San Bernardino County generally range from $300 to $1,200 depending on the scope of work.
  • Completion report: After drilling and completion, we file the California DWR Form 188 completion report, which becomes part of the permanent state groundwater record.

Cost to Drill a Well in Lytle Creek

One of the most common questions we receive is: how much does it cost to drill a well in Lytle Creek? Cost depends on several factors specific to your parcel, but we can give you realistic ranges based on what we see in this part of San Bernardino County.

Turnkey Well Project Cost

A complete turnkey well project in Lytle Creek, including drilling, casing, development, pump, pressure system, and all connections, typically ranges from $18,000 to $42,000. Factors that push costs toward the higher end include:

  • Greater depth required to reach a productive aquifer, particularly in bedrock formations
  • Hard fractured granite that slows drilling progress and increases rig time
  • Remote or difficult-to-access sites requiring extra equipment mobilization
  • Larger pump systems for higher-demand properties (livestock, irrigation, multi-unit)
  • Deeper static water levels requiring a longer pump drop pipe

Wells in very hard rock terrain or requiring depths beyond 400 feet can exceed the upper end of this range. We provide a detailed written estimate after the site assessment so you know what to expect before any work begins.
