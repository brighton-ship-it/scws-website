Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your San Marcos well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance

Avocados on the San Marcos Hillsides

San Marcos is a city of granite hills and tucked-away valleys, and that topography is exactly why avocados took hold here. The slopes around Twin Oaks Valley and Coronado Hills drain cold air downhill on still winter nights, sparing the trees the frost that would damage them on flatter ground, and the decomposed-granite soils drain freely enough to keep avocado roots from drowning. Drive past Cal State San Marcos and Lake San Marcos out into the rural fringes and Discovery Hills, and you'll still find producing groves leaning on private wells for the water their trees can't get any other way.

Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years keeping wells like these running. We're a licensed C-57 contractor (CSLB #1086994) with a 4.9-star reputation and offices in Ramona and Anza, and we work the full San Marcos–Escondido–Vista corridor. On San Marcos's steep parcels, one issue comes up more than anywhere else: pressure. When your grove climbs a hillside, getting even water to both the bottom row and the ridgeline is a real engineering problem, and it's one we solve constantly.

Pressure, Elevation, and Why Hillside Groves Are Different

Every foot of vertical rise costs you water pressure — roughly 0.43 psi per foot of elevation. On a San Marcos grove that spans 60 or 80 feet of vertical climb, the trees at the top can be starved while the trees at the bottom are over-watered, all from the same pump. The fixes that make a hillside grove water evenly include:

  • A constant-pressure (booster) system that holds a steady delivery pressure regardless of how many zones are running.
  • Zoning the grove by elevation so each band of trees irrigates at a pressure suited to its height on the slope.
  • Pressure-compensating emitters and regulators that put out the same flow at the top and bottom of a line.
  • A storage tank set thoughtfully on the property so the booster has a reliable supply to draw from.

Get this right and the whole grove greens up uniformly; get it wrong and you'll chase dry, stressed trees at the ridgeline year after year no matter how much you pump.

How the Well System Comes Together

Behind the pressure system is the well itself. In San Marcos's decomposed granite, productive wells draw from fractures in the rock, and a submersible pump is matched to whatever the well can sustainably yield. Because many fractured-granite wells produce a steady but moderate flow, a storage tank is usually the linchpin: the well trickles water into the tank around the clock, and the grove draws hard from the tank during its daily irrigation window. Mature trees run on micro-sprinklers that wet the broad, shallow root zone; young trees often start on drip. Good filtration keeps fine granite sediment from plugging emitters.

Avocado Water Demand and Salinity in San Marcos

Avocados are thirsty and fussy. A mature tree can take 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and a producing acre commonly needs about four to six acre-feet a year, with the heaviest pull during summer fruit sizing. They're also among the most chloride- and sodium-sensitive crops grown in San Diego County — salts that wouldn't bother most plants will burn avocado leaf margins and erode your yield.

San Marcos groundwater drawn from granite tends to be hard and mineral-rich, and in dry years salinity can rise as the water table drops. Growers manage this by applying a leaching fraction to push salts past the roots, by testing water for chloride, sodium, EC, and hardness each season, by blending with a cleaner source when one is available, and by choosing salt-tolerant rootstocks at replant. We can test your well water for the parameters that matter to avocados so you're managing by data rather than by the first scorched leaf.

What Goes Wrong on San Marcos Groves

  • Uneven irrigation on the slope. Ridgeline trees stressed while lower trees thrive — almost always a pressure or zoning problem.
  • Pump scale and wear. Hard water deposits mineral scale on impellers and screens, slowly cutting output.
  • Pressure-tank failure. A waterlogged tank short-cycles the pump and wears out switches and motors.
  • Late-summer shortfall. The grove keeps up until sizing season, then runs dry — a sign the well, pump, or storage is undersized for true peak demand.
  • Leaf burn from chloride, worse after dry winters.
  • Sediment from granite sand clogging filters and micro-sprinklers.

What You Can Check, and When to Call

It's safe to confirm the well breaker is on and the controller is fault-free, to watch the pressure gauge for rapid cycling, and to walk the lines for breaks or a blown filter. If only the top of the grove is dry, suspect pressure or a zone valve rather than the well. Beyond that, bring in a licensed contractor — especially when there's no water at all, when the pump runs but won't build pressure, when you smell a hot motor, or when leaf burn means it's time to test the water. Pulling a deep submersible pump and judging well yield on a steep parcel is specialized work, and doing it wrong can be expensive.

Young Trees Versus Mature Groves: Different Water, Same Well

A newly planted San Marcos grove and a mature one make very different demands on the same well, and matching the irrigation to the tree's stage protects both your trees and your equipment. Young trees have small root systems and want light, frequent water delivered close to the trunk — drip lines or a single micro-emitter per tree are ideal, and over-watering at this stage drowns roots and invites root rot in the granite soils. As trees mature and their canopies spread, the wetted area has to grow with them; that's when growers transition to micro-sprinklers that throw water across the whole shallow root mat. A well designed only for today's young trees will fall short in five years when those same trees are full producers, so it's worth planning storage and pump capacity for the grove's mature demand from the start.

This staging also affects scheduling. Mixed-age groves — common as San Marcos owners replant blocks over time — benefit from separate irrigation zones so the young and mature sections can run on different timers and pressures off the same source. Building that flexibility in early saves a costly retrofit later.

Drought Years and Protecting Your Aquifer

Southern California's dry cycles put real strain on granite wells. When rainfall is scant, the water table beneath a San Marcos hillside drops, the well's sustainable yield falls, and the salts already in the groundwater concentrate — a double hit of less water and saltier water, exactly when the grove needs the most. Growers who come through drought years in good shape tend to do a few things consistently: they irrigate efficiently with well-maintained, unclogged emitters so no water is wasted; they monitor pump run-time and pressure so they notice a declining well early; and they keep storage capacity generous so a slowing well still fills the tank overnight. If a well's yield has genuinely fallen, hydrofracturing can sometimes reopen tight fractures and restore flow without the cost of a whole new well — a worthwhile first option to evaluate before drilling fresh.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do the trees at the top of my San Marcos grove always look stressed?

Elevation costs pressure — about 0.43 psi for every foot of climb — so the highest trees get the least water from a pump that isn't compensating. A constant-pressure system, elevation zoning, and pressure-compensating emitters usually even it out.

Do I really need a booster pump for a hillside grove?

On a grove with significant vertical rise, almost always. A booster or constant-pressure system holds steady delivery pressure across the whole slope so the ridgeline and the base get matched flow. Without it you tend to chase dry top rows indefinitely.

What's burning the margins of my avocado leaves?

That's the classic look of chloride or sodium accumulation in the root zone, common in San Marcos's mineral-rich granite water and worse in dry years. Water testing plus a deliberate leaching fraction is the standard remedy.

How deep are San Marcos grove wells?

It depends on where the productive fractures sit in the granite under your parcel. A local driller's knowledge of the Twin Oaks Valley and Coronado Hills areas helps target the right depth rather than drilling blind.

Can you handle everything, or just the pump?

Everything. We drill, hydrofracture, repair and replace pumps, install filtration and softening, and design irrigation pressure systems — all under one C-57 license, so you deal with one accountable crew.
