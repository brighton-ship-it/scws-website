How a Well System Supplies an Avocado Grove

A grove irrigation system is a chain of components, and a weakness anywhere along that chain shows up as stressed trees, uneven blocks, or a sudden loss of pressure during the hottest week of the year. Understanding the pieces helps you spot trouble before it becomes a crop emergency.

The Well and the Pump

The well itself is the cased borehole drilled into the aquifer. In San Diego County backcountry, that often means fractured crystalline rock and decomposed granite rather than deep sandy aquifers, so yield can vary tree-block to tree-block. A submersible pump set down in the casing lifts water to the surface. Avocado groves are thirsty — a mature tree can demand 40 to 70 gallons a day in summer, and an acre may need four to six acre-feet a year — so the pump has to be correctly sized for both depth and peak flow. An undersized or worn pump short-cycles, overheats, and fails early.

Pressure, Storage, and Delivery

Because grove demand surges during midday irrigation windows, most operations buffer the well with storage tanks. The well fills the tank steadily, and the irrigation system draws from the tank at the higher rate the trees need. A pressure tank and pressure switch maintain steady delivery pressure, while a constant-pressure (variable-frequency) controller can hold pressure rock-steady across changing demand. From there, water moves through filtration, then into drip lines for established trees or micro-sprinklers for young plantings. Uniform pressure across the grove is what gives you uniform fruit set and sizing — pressure drift at the far rows is one of the most common causes of patchy production.

Water Quality for Avocados: Why Coastal Chemistry Matters

Avocados are among the most chloride- and salinity-sensitive commercial tree crops grown. They show leaf-tip burn, defoliation, and reduced yield at salt levels many other crops tolerate without complaint. That sensitivity makes water quality testing every bit as important as water quantity for anyone in the Point Loma region.

Chloride and Salinity

Near the coast, seawater intrusion is a real concern: when groundwater is pumped faster than it recharges, saltwater can migrate inland into the aquifer, driving up chloride and overall salinity. Even modest chloride increases stress avocado roots and accumulate in leaves over a season. If your well shows rising salinity, options include blending with a lower-chloride source, applying extra leaching irrigation to flush salts below the root zone, selecting salt-tolerant rootstocks, and committing to regular soil and leaf-tissue testing so problems are caught while they are still reversible.

Hardness, Iron, and Manganese

Beyond salinity, San Diego County wells frequently carry hard, mineral-rich water. Hardness drives scale that clogs drip emitters and shortens pump life. Iron and manganese cause staining, feed bacterial slime that plugs irrigation lines, and can throw off nutrient availability in the soil. Sediment from fractured-rock formations abrades pump components and fills filters. Each of these has a treatment path — sediment filtration, iron and manganese removal, or softening — but the right fix depends on a real water analysis, not a guess.

Common Local Scenarios and Their Causes

Across our years serving San Diego County groves and rural properties, the same patterns recur. Recognizing yours can save a service call — or tell you exactly when to make one.

  • Leaf-tip burn that worsens through summer — usually chloride or salinity accumulation, especially where irrigation has been light and salts have not been leached past the roots.
  • Clogged or uneven drip emitters — typically hardness scale, iron bacteria, or unfiltered sediment building up in the lines.
  • Pump short-cycling or running constantly — often a waterlogged pressure tank, a failing pressure switch, or a pump that no longer matches the grove's demand.
  • Pressure that fades at the far rows — undersized delivery, partially blocked lines, or a pump losing capacity as it wears.
  • Declining yield during drought years — lower static water levels in fractured-rock wells, sometimes correctable with hydrofracturing or a deepened well.
  • New staining, odor, or cloudiness — a shift in water chemistry that warrants testing before it reaches the trees.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick observations help you describe the problem accurately and often narrow down the cause:

  1. Note your pressure gauge reading and whether the pump cycles on and off rapidly (a sign of tank or switch trouble).
  2. Check the pressure tank — tap it; a healthy tank sounds hollow on top and solid at the bottom. An all-solid tank is waterlogged.
  3. Confirm the breaker and any control panel are not tripped, and look for scorching or loose wiring around the pressure switch.
  4. Inspect filters for sediment, rust color, or slime, and flush a sample of drip emitters to see if flow is even.
  5. Walk the grove for uneven wetting patterns or dry rows that point to pressure or clogging issues.
  6. Keep a basic water test on file — chloride, salinity (TDS or EC), hardness, iron, and manganese — so changes are easy to spot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are avocados so sensitive to well water quality?

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride and overall salinity, far more than most crops. Salts accumulate in the leaves over a growing season and cause tip burn, defoliation, and lower yields. Because of this, water testing for chloride, salinity, hardness, iron, and manganese is just as important as having enough water volume.

Should I worry about seawater intrusion near Point Loma?

Coastal aquifers can experience seawater intrusion when groundwater is pumped faster than it recharges, raising chloride and salinity levels. For salt-sensitive avocados, even modest increases matter. If you irrigate near the coast, periodic testing and strategies like blending, leaching, and salt-tolerant rootstocks help protect your trees.

How much water does an avocado grove need?

A mature avocado tree can use 40 to 70 gallons per day in summer, and an acre may require roughly four to six acre-feet per year. Demand peaks during fruit set and sizing, so your well, pump, and storage must be sized for those high-demand windows, not just average use.
