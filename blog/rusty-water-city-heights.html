What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your City Heights water.

Rusty Water in City Heights: An Older San Diego Neighborhood's Real Causes

City Heights is one of San Diego's densest and most historic urban neighborhoods, an inner-city Mid-City community in San Diego County packed with bungalows, apartments, and houses dating from the early to mid twentieth century. It is fully served by the City of San Diego's municipal water system, which means private wells inside City Heights are essentially nonexistent. When residents here see rusty, orange, or brown water, the cause is almost never the source water and almost always the aging plumbing inside the building. Because this is a neighborhood of older housing stock, corroding galvanized-steel pipe is far and away the leading explanation. This article focuses on that reality, while also covering well-sourced iron for readers who own rural property elsewhere in the county.

Why Galvanized Plumbing Is the Usual Suspect Here

A large share of City Heights homes were built in the 1920s through the 1950s, decades when galvanized steel was the standard plumbing material. Galvanized pipe is steel protected by a zinc coating, and that coating is sacrificial: it slowly corrodes away to protect the steel, and once it is gone, the steel itself begins to rust from the inside. Rust accumulates on the pipe walls and periodically breaks free, traveling to your faucets as orange flakes and tinting the water. This is why an older City Heights home receiving clean municipal water can still deliver rusty water at the tap. The most recognizable sign is discoloration that is heaviest first thing in the morning, after water has been sitting still in the pipes overnight, and that clears after the tap runs for a minute or two.

The Hidden Side Effect: Pressure Loss

Galvanized corrosion does more than discolor water. As rust and mineral scale build up on the inside of the pipes, the usable diameter shrinks, sometimes dramatically in pipes that are seventy or more years old. The result is steadily declining water pressure, weak showers, and slow-filling tubs, often alongside the rusty water. Many City Heights homeowners notice both problems together, and both share the same root cause and the same solution. Treating only the color with a filter leaves the pressure problem and the ongoing corrosion untouched.

How Iron Appears in Well Water Elsewhere

For readers who own rural or East County parcels on private wells, the source of rust is geological rather than plumbing-related. Groundwater dissolves naturally occurring iron as it moves through soil and rock, holding it invisibly in solution until it reaches air at the tap and oxidizes into rusty particles. Wells can also host iron bacteria, which leave a slimy reddish residue and a musty smell. Well-sourced iron is removed by oxidizing it and filtering it out, and iron bacteria are cleared with shock chlorination plus filtration. These are well-service solutions, distinct from the repiping that solves a City Heights plumbing problem.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Run cold water at multiple faucets. Rust everywhere points to the main line into the home or the building's risers; rust at only some fixtures points to the branches serving them.
  2. Compare hot and cold at one faucet. Rust only on the hot side usually means the water heater.
  3. Watch the timing. Morning-heavy rust that clears with running is the classic galvanized-pipe pattern.
  4. Consider the home's age. Pre-1960 San Diego homes very likely have original galvanized lines unless repiped.
  5. If on a private well, check for orange slime in the toilet tank and any musty odor, both signs of iron bacteria.

How Treatment and Repiping Actually Work

It helps to understand the mechanics behind each fix. When the cause is galvanized corrosion, repiping replaces the rusting steel with PEX (a flexible cross-linked polyethylene) or copper, neither of which sheds rust. A repipe can be staged: a plumber maps the runs, opens limited sections of wall, swaps the lines, and restores the surfaces, often completing a single-family home in a few days. When the cause is well-sourced iron, treatment instead oxidizes the dissolved iron into particles and filters them out, with the filter media backwashing periodically to clear the captured rust. The two approaches share nothing in common, which is exactly why an accurate diagnosis up front saves City Heights homeowners from spending on equipment that cannot solve their particular problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does City Heights have private wells?

No, the neighborhood is entirely on City of San Diego municipal water. Rusty water in City Heights is almost always caused by corroding galvanized plumbing in older homes and buildings, not by the source water.

Why is my water rusty only in the morning?

This is the hallmark of galvanized pipe corrosion. Overnight, standing water absorbs rust from the pipe walls; running the tap for a minute flushes it out, and the water clears.

Is rusty water dangerous to drink?

Iron is not a recognized health hazard at common levels, but it tastes metallic and stains. The bigger concern is what the corrosion is doing to your plumbing and pressure over time.

Can a water filter fix this?

A sediment filter can reduce visible rust particles, but it does not stop the pipes from corroding. The permanent solution for galvanized plumbing is repiping with PEX or copper.

My pressure is dropping along with the rust. Are they related?

Yes. The same internal corrosion that releases rust also narrows the pipes, lowering pressure. Repiping addresses both problems at once.
