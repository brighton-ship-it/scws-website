Water Treatment for Wells Near Loma Portal and the Greater Point Loma Area

Loma Portal is a residential neighborhood in the Point Loma area of the City of San Diego, in San Diego County, California. The vast majority of homes here are served by treated municipal water from the City of San Diego rather than private wells, so for most Loma Portal residents the main "treatment" question is softening or polishing already-treated city water. The picture changes, however, once you move into the more rural and semi-rural pockets of greater San Diego County that surround the coastal city — the inland valleys, ranch parcels, and agricultural properties where private groundwater wells are still common and where Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years solving water quality problems.

This guide is written for that audience: the minority of Loma Portal-area property owners on private wells, anyone evaluating a coastal or rural parcel served by groundwater, and well owners throughout San Diego County who want to understand how raw well water is treated and made safe. As a licensed C-57 well-drilling contractor with a 4.9-star reputation, we install and service every major category of well water treatment equipment, and the right system always starts with one thing: knowing exactly what is in your water.

Why You Should Test Before You Treat

Treatment equipment is matched to specific contaminants. Buying a softener when your real problem is bacteria, or an iron filter when your real problem is low pH, wastes money and leaves the actual issue unsolved. That is why we never recommend equipment before a water test. A laboratory analysis tells you the hardness in grains per gallon, the pH, and the levels of iron, manganese, nitrate, sulfur (hydrogen sulfide), total dissolved solids (TDS), and bacteria such as coliform and E. coli.

San Diego County groundwater has well-documented tendencies that make testing especially worthwhile. Most wells in the county produce hard to very hard water, frequently in the 15–30 grains-per-gallon range, well above the 7 gpg threshold where scale and soap problems begin. Nitrate can be elevated near agricultural land and older septic systems, and some inland and East County areas show naturally occurring arsenic. A test priced at roughly $100–$300 is the cheapest insurance you can buy before investing in equipment, and it also gives you a baseline to compare against in future years.

Common Local Water Problems and the Right Treatment for Each

Every well is different, but raw groundwater in this region tends to produce a recognizable set of issues. Here is how each one is correctly treated.

Hard Water → Water Softener

Hardness is by far the most common complaint we see across San Diego County. Calcium and magnesium dissolved from local rock leave chalky scale on fixtures, cloud glassware, stiffen laundry, and shorten the life of water heaters, dishwashers, and the well pump itself. The proven fix is an ion-exchange water softener, which swaps the hardness minerals for sodium (or potassium) as water passes through a resin bed. A properly sized softener protects your entire plumbing system and typically runs $1,500–$3,500 installed, depending on capacity and features.

Iron and Manganese Staining → Oxidizing / Iron Filter

Reddish-brown stains in sinks and toilets, or black specks and dark staining, point to dissolved iron and manganese. These are best removed with an oxidizing iron filter that converts the dissolved metals into solid particles and then traps them in a filter media bed, often using air injection or a catalytic media. Iron filters generally cost $1,500–$3,500 and are frequently paired with a softener when both hardness and iron are present.

Sediment and Grit → Sediment Filtration

Sand, silt, and fine rock particles are common in fractured-rock and decomposed-granite aquifers throughout the region. Sediment scours pump components, clogs valves, and fouls downstream treatment equipment. A sediment filtration stage — a spin-down separator or a graded cartridge filter installed at the point of entry — protects everything behind it. Sediment filtration is usually the most affordable component and is a standard part of a $300–$900 whole-house filtration package.

Sulfur / Rotten-Egg Smell → Aeration, Carbon, or Chlorination

A rotten-egg odor is hydrogen sulfide gas. Depending on concentration, it is treated with aeration (mixing air to off-gas the sulfur), activated carbon filtration for lighter cases, or chlorination followed by filtration for heavier cases. Because sulfur odor sometimes signals sulfur-reducing bacteria, this problem is one where a professional diagnosis matters before choosing the method.

Bacteria → UV Sterilization or Chlorination

Any private well that tests positive for coliform or E. coli needs disinfection before the water is safe to drink. The cleanest modern solution is an ultraviolet (UV) sterilization system, which passes water past a UV lamp that inactivates bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens without adding any chemicals or taste. UV systems typically cost $800–$1,800. For some situations, shock chlorination of the well plus continuous chlorination is used instead or in combination. UV only works on clear water, so it is always installed downstream of sediment and iron filtration.

Nitrates and Dissolved Contaminants → Reverse Osmosis

Nitrate — a real concern near agricultural areas and older septic systems in San Diego County — cannot be removed by a softener or a standard filter. The correct treatment is reverse osmosis (RO), which forces water through a semipermeable membrane that rejects nitrate, arsenic, dissolved salts, and many other contaminants. RO is most often installed as a point-of-use system under the kitchen sink for drinking and cooking water, priced around $300–$1,200. Whole-house RO is available for severe cases but costs considerably more.

Acidic / Low-pH Water → Acid Neutralizer

Water with a low pH is corrosive. Tell-tale signs include blue-green staining from copper pipes, pinhole leaks, and premature corrosion of fittings and the water heater. The fix is an acid neutralizer — a tank of calcite or a calcite/magnesia blend that raises pH as water flows through it. Neutralizers run roughly $1,500–$3,000 and protect both your plumbing and any downstream equipment from acid damage.

Coastal Saltwater Intrusion → Reverse Osmosis

Wells located close to the coast can be vulnerable to saltwater intrusion, where over-pumping draws seawater inland into a freshwater aquifer, raising chloride and total dissolved solids. If a coastal parcel near the Point Loma peninsula relies on groundwater and a test shows elevated TDS or chloride, reverse osmosis is the primary treatment capable of producing usable water. Because intrusion can worsen over time, ongoing TDS monitoring is wise for any near-coast well.

Sizing and Whole-House vs. Point-of-Use

Choosing the right size matters as much as choosing the right type. A softener or filter that is too small will exhaust quickly and let untreated water through; one that is too large wastes salt and water. Sizing is based on your household size, peak flow demand, and the measured concentration of each contaminant — another reason the water test comes first.

Whole-house (point-of-entry) systems treat all the water coming into the building and are the right choice for hardness, iron, sediment, sulfur, bacteria, and pH, where you want every tap, shower, and appliance protected. Point-of-use systems, such as an under-sink reverse osmosis unit, treat water at a single tap and are ideal for drinking-water concerns like nitrate and TDS, where treating the whole house would be unnecessarily expensive. Many San Diego County well owners end up with a sensible combination: whole-house softening and filtration plus a point-of-use RO unit at the kitchen sink.

Maintenance Keeps Your System Working

Treatment equipment is not install-and-forget. Softeners need salt and an occasional resin check. Sediment and carbon cartridges need periodic replacement. Iron filters and neutralizers need media inspection and eventual recharge or refill. UV systems need an annual lamp replacement and a clean quartz sleeve to stay effective. RO membranes and pre-filters need scheduled changes. Skipping maintenance is the single most common reason a treatment system stops performing — and because well water has no municipal monitoring, we recommend retesting your water at least once a year, and any time the taste, color, or smell changes.

When to Call a Professional

You can change a cartridge or add salt yourself, but you should call a licensed professional when your water tests positive for bacteria or nitrate, when you smell sulfur, when you need to size or install whole-house equipment, when staining or corrosion appears, or any time you are not sure what is causing a problem. A C-57 licensed contractor can interpret your lab results, recommend equipment that actually matches your water chemistry, and install it correctly so it performs as intended. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Loma Portal use well water or city water?

Most of Loma Portal is served by treated municipal water from the City of San Diego. Private wells are uncommon within the neighborhood itself but are still found on rural and semi-rural parcels across the surrounding parts of San Diego County, which is where well water treatment is most relevant.

How hard is the water in the San Diego area?

Both municipal and well water in San Diego County tend to be hard to very hard, often in the 15–30 grains-per-gallon range. That is well above the level where scale, spotting, and soap problems begin, which is why ion-exchange water softeners are so common throughout the region.

How often should I test my well water?

At minimum once a year, and immediately any time the taste, color, smell, or clarity changes. Annual testing for bacteria, nitrate, and basic chemistry is the standard recommendation for private wells because no public agency monitors them for you.

Can one system fix every water problem?

No single device handles everything. Hardness, iron, sulfur, bacteria, nitrate, and low pH each require a different technology. Most well owners need a tailored combination — for example a softener plus a sediment filter, with a point-of-use reverse osmosis unit for drinking water.

Is reverse osmosis necessary if I have a softener?

They do different jobs. A softener removes hardness minerals but does not remove nitrate, arsenic, or dissolved salts. If your test shows those contaminants — or elevated TDS from coastal saltwater intrusion — you need reverse osmosis in addition to, not instead of, a softener.
