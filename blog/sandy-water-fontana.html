Sand in Your Fontana Well Water?

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Fontana well.

Sand and Sediment in Fontana Well Water

If you have started seeing sand, grit, or silty sediment settling in the bottom of a glass, collecting in your toilet tank, or clogging faucet aerators on your Fontana property, you are dealing with one of the most common—and most damaging—problems a private well owner can face in San Bernardino County. Fontana sits on the alluvial floor of the Inland Empire, directly below the San Gabriel and San Bernardino Mountains, and the very geology that gives the area its abundant groundwater is also what makes sand intrusion a genuine local risk.

At Southern California Well Service, we have spent more than 30 years pulling pumps, inspecting screens, and rehabilitating wells across Fontana and the surrounding communities of Rialto, Bloomington, Rancho Cucamonga, Ontario, and San Bernardino. Sandy water is rarely a cosmetic nuisance you can simply filter and forget. It is almost always a signal that something has changed below ground or inside your well, and the longer abrasive sand cycles through your pump and plumbing, the more expensive the eventual repair becomes. This guide explains why Fontana wells produce sand, what that sand quietly destroys, how we diagnose the true source, and what it costs to fix it correctly the first time.

Why Fontana Wells Produce Sand

To understand sand problems in Fontana, you have to understand what your well is actually drawing from. Fontana draws its groundwater from the Chino Basin and the Rialto-Colton groundwater subbasins—deep aquifers made up of alluvial sand-and-gravel fan deposits washed down from the mountains over hundreds of thousands of years. The Lytle Creek and Cucamonga alluvial fans carried sand, gravel, and finer sediment off the San Gabriel and San Bernardino Mountains and spread them across the valley floor where Fontana now sits. Northern Fontana, climbing toward the foothills around Hunters Ridge, sits on coarser, steeper fan material, while the southern and central parts of the city overlie finer, deeper basin sediments.

This matters because, unlike a well drilled into solid bedrock, a well screened in a loose sand-and-gravel aquifer is in constant contact with fine, mobile sediment. The well screen and the gravel pack around it are specifically engineered to hold that fine sand back while letting clean water in. When any part of that system fails or is overwhelmed, the aquifer's natural sand starts coming up the casing with your water. Because Fontana's aquifer is a sandy alluvial formation rather than fractured rock, sand and sediment intrusion is a real, recurring local concern rather than a rare event.

The specific causes we encounter most often on Fontana wells include the following:

  • A worn or failing pump: A submersible pump that is already pulling sand will wear its impellers and wear rings, which in turn lets it draw even more sand—an accelerating cycle.
  • An oversized pump over-drawing the well: A pump rated for more flow than the aquifer can comfortably deliver pulls water too aggressively, creating high velocity at the screen that drags fine sand in with it.
  • A failed or corroded well screen: Decades of service in mineral-rich Inland Empire groundwater can corrode or split a screen, opening gaps that let aquifer sand pour through.
  • The wrong screen slot size for fine sand: If the original screen slots or gravel pack were sized for coarser material than the formation actually contains, fine Fontana sand passes straight through.
  • Excessive drawdown: Pumping the water level down too far—common during drought years or heavy irrigation demand—increases the suction velocity at the screen and mobilizes sediment.
  • A new, undeveloped well: A freshly drilled well naturally produces some sand for the first days or weeks until it is properly developed; if development was rushed or skipped, sand keeps coming.
  • The naturally sandy aquifer itself: Even a healthy well in the Chino Basin alluvium will produce trace sediment, so any increase deserves prompt attention.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is abrasive, and inside a pressurized water system it behaves like liquid sandpaper. The damage is cumulative and often invisible until something fails. The submersible pump is usually the first and most expensive casualty: sand grinds away the impellers, wear rings, and bearings, robbing the pump of pressure and eventually seizing or burning out the motor. A pump that should last 12 to 15 years can fail in a fraction of that time when it is constantly moving grit.

Downstream, sand settles into your pressure tank, reducing its effective capacity and causing the pump to short-cycle. It scours the seats of valves and the cartridge of your pressure switch, packs into water heaters where it bakes into a hard crust on the bottom, and erodes the seals and solenoids in dishwashers, washing machines, irrigation valves, and toilets. Aerators and showerheads clog, fixtures wear out early, and any whole-house filtration you install plugs up far faster than it should. Left unaddressed, sandy water turns a single repairable issue into a chain of failing equipment across the entire property.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Fontana

Guessing at a sand problem wastes money, because the fix for a worn pump is completely different from the fix for a torn screen. Our diagnostic process is methodical and built around Fontana's specific conditions:

  • Jar and settling test: We capture water samples and measure how much sediment settles out, and how quickly, which tells us whether you have fine suspended silt or coarser grit.
  • Sand concentration measurement: We quantify the actual sand content—often in parts per million by volume—so we can track whether it is improving or worsening.
  • Amp draw and pump depth check: We read the motor's amperage and confirm the pump's set depth, since a pump hanging too low in the casing sits right in the sediment zone.
  • Pull and inspect the pump and screen: When the evidence points downhole, we physically pull the pump to inspect the impellers, the check valve, the drop pipe, and the condition of the screen and gravel pack.
  • Downhole camera inspection: A camera survey lets us see corrosion, splits, holes, or encrustation in the casing and screen without guesswork.
  • Flow and drawdown testing: We measure yield against how far the water level drops, revealing whether over-pumping or excessive drawdown is dragging sand in.

How We Fix It

Once we know the source, we match the solution to the cause rather than masking the symptom. Depending on what your Fontana well needs, the fix may involve one or several of the following:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters: A centrifugal sand separator spins sediment out of the water before it reaches your pressure tank and plumbing, protecting the whole system.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration: Spin-down filters and graded sediment cartridges catch finer material for point-of-entry protection, ideal when sand levels are low to moderate.
  • Correct pump sizing and placement: We re-size the pump to match your well's real yield and reset it at the proper depth—above the sediment zone—so it stops drawing sand in the first place.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment: We clean, surge, and redevelop the well to restore the gravel pack and remove fine material that has migrated into the screen area.
  • Screen repair or relining: Where the screen itself has failed, we repair, reline, or replace it so the aquifer's sand is held back the way it was designed to be.

In many cases the right answer is a combination—for example, resetting a properly sized pump and adding a centrifugal separator as insurance against the trace sediment that any Chino Basin well will always carry.

Preventing Sand Problems

The best sand problem is the one that never starts. A few habits go a long way for Fontana well owners. Avoid over-pumping by spreading heavy irrigation demand across the day rather than running everything at once, which keeps drawdown and screen velocity in check. Have your well's static and pumping water levels checked periodically, especially after dry years when the Inland Empire water table drops. Schedule a professional well inspection every few years so a corroding screen or a pump that has crept down in the casing is caught before it starts producing sand. If your well is new, insist on proper development before it is put into regular service. And keep an eye—literally—on your water: a glass left to settle on the counter is the cheapest sand monitor you own.

When to Call a Professional

Some sand situations warrant an immediate call. If sand appears suddenly and heavily where there was none before, if your water pressure is dropping along with the grit, if the pump is short-cycling or running constantly, or if you are finding sand in multiple fixtures at once, those are signs of an active downhole failure that will only worsen. Trace sediment that has been stable for years may simply need a separator, but a sudden change almost always means a screen, casing, or pump problem that requires pulling equipment and inspecting the well. Well work involves heavy equipment, electrical components, and confined downhole conditions—it is not safe DIY territory. As a licensed C-57 contractor, we have the rigs, cameras, and experience to diagnose and repair Fontana wells correctly and safely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Fontana well water normal?

A trace of fine sediment is common because Fontana's aquifer is a sand-and-gravel alluvial formation. A small, stable amount can often be handled with a sediment filter. But any noticeable increase, or sand you can feel grit between your fingers, signals a change in the well that should be inspected.

Will a sand filter alone solve the problem?

Sometimes, but not always. A filter or separator protects your plumbing and appliances, which is valuable, but it does not stop sand from wearing out your pump downhole. If the source is a failed screen or a pump set too low, you need to address the well itself, not just filter the water coming out of it.

Can sand really ruin my well pump?

Yes. Sand is highly abrasive and grinds away the impellers, wear rings, and bearings inside a submersible pump. It is one of the leading causes of premature pump failure, which is why a sudden increase in sand should never be ignored.

Why did my well suddenly start producing sand?

Common triggers in Fontana include a corroding or split well screen, a pump that has worn or slipped lower in the casing, and excessive drawdown during drought or heavy irrigation. A downhole camera inspection usually pinpoints the exact cause quickly.

How long does a sand repair take?

Adding a separator or sediment filter is typically a same-day job. Pulling and resetting a pump is usually completed in a day. Full well rehabilitation or screen repair can take longer depending on depth and condition, and we will give you a realistic timeline after the diagnostic.
