Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Del Mar well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Private Wells and Landscape Irrigation in Del Mar's Coastal Setting

Del Mar sits on the northern edge of San Diego County's coastline, a compact beach city of roughly two square miles wrapped around bluffs, the San Dieguito lagoon, and the famous Del Mar Racetrack and fairgrounds. Most homes here draw their household water from municipal supply rather than a private well, so it is worth being straightforward: Del Mar is not a commercial avocado district. What it does have is a strong culture of estate gardens, ornamental landscaping, mature lawns, and the occasional backyard avocado or citrus tree on the larger lots that climb toward Carmel Valley, Rancho Santa Fe, and Solana Beach. Where private wells exist in and around Del Mar, they are almost always landscape and irrigation wells, or legacy domestic wells on bigger parcels, used to keep gardens green without leaning entirely on metered city water.

That distinction matters because a landscape irrigation well near the coast faces a very different set of conditions than an inland agricultural well. The geology beneath Del Mar is marine sedimentary rock and coastal terrace deposits laid down over millions of years, not the fractured granite found further east in Ramona or Anza. Combined with proximity to the ocean and the San Dieguito lagoon system, the defining water-quality concern here is salinity. Brackish or salt-influenced groundwater is the issue that most often surprises property owners, and it has direct consequences for the salt-sensitive plants Del Mar gardeners love, including avocado and citrus.

How a Private Well and Landscape Irrigation System Works

A residential well and irrigation setup in Del Mar is a chain of components, and a weak link anywhere shows up at the sprinkler heads. Understanding the pieces helps you describe a problem accurately when you call for help.

  • The well and submersible pump draw groundwater up from the saturated zone. The pump sits deep in the casing and pushes water toward the surface on demand.
  • The pressure tank stores a cushion of pressurized water so the pump is not forced to cycle on every time a valve opens. A waterlogged or failed tank is one of the most common reasons for rapid, short pump cycling.
  • The pressure switch tells the pump when to start and stop based on system pressure. It is an inexpensive part that causes outsized headaches when it sticks or burns out.
  • Filtration and treatment may include a sediment filter and, where salinity or minerals are a concern, additional conditioning before the water reaches the irrigation zones.
  • The irrigation distribution ties into valves, drip lines, and micro-sprinklers that water beds, hedges, ornamental trees, and any backyard fruit trees.

For estate gardens spread across uneven coastal lots, a constant-pressure or booster system is often added so that the furthest zone gets the same coverage as the one nearest the pump.

Common Local Scenarios and Their Causes

The calls we field from coastal North County properties tend to cluster around a handful of recurring situations:

  • Salty or brackish water showing up as white crusting on soil, leaf-tip burn on avocado and citrus, and declining ornamental plants. Near the lagoon and the coast, this often traces back to salinity in the groundwater itself.
  • Weak or uneven irrigation pressure where the back of the garden never gets watered properly, frequently a tank, pressure-switch, or aging-pump issue rather than the sprinklers themselves.
  • Short cycling, where the pump clicks on and off rapidly, which usually points to a waterlogged pressure tank or a misadjusted switch and will shorten pump life if ignored.
  • Sediment and cloudiness from coastal terrace fines working into the system, clogging drip emitters and micro-sprinklers.
  • Iron or manganese staining that leaves rust-colored marks on hardscape, walls, and light-colored stucco common in Del Mar architecture.
  • A legacy well that has simply aged out, where a decades-old domestic well on a larger lot is no longer reliable and the owner is weighing repair against replacement or proper decommissioning.

A Practical What-to-Check List

Before you call, a few quick observations help us diagnose faster and may even point to something you can resolve yourself:

  1. Look at the pressure gauge on the tank. Is it holding steady, or swinging wildly when irrigation runs?
  2. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off is a classic short-cycling symptom.
  3. Check the breaker for the well or irrigation pump. Rule out a simple tripped circuit first.
  4. Inspect emitters and sprinkler heads for crusty mineral or salt buildup.
  5. Note any change in taste, smell, color, or a salty edge to the water, and roughly when it started.
  6. Watch your salt-sensitive plants. Tip burn on avocado and citrus leaves is an early warning of rising chloride or salinity.
  7. Confirm whether the problem affects every zone or just the ones farthest from the pump.

When to Call a Professional

Some things are simple homeowner checks. Others should not be handled without a licensed contractor. Call a professional when you see water-quality changes that point to salinity intrusion, when the pump runs but pressure stays low, when you suspect the pump itself is failing, or when a treatment system needs to be sized for brackish or mineral-heavy water. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Del Mar have private wells if most homes are on city water?

Yes, though they are less common than inland. The wells you find in and around Del Mar are typically landscape and irrigation wells used to water estate gardens and ornamental landscaping, plus older legacy domestic wells on larger lots. They supplement or reduce reliance on metered municipal water rather than serving as a property's only supply.

Why does my well water near the coast taste or feel salty?

Proximity to the ocean and the San Dieguito lagoon means coastal groundwater can be brackish or salt-influenced. Salinity is the single most common water-quality issue near the Del Mar coast, and it is what makes avocado, citrus, and other salt-sensitive plants struggle with leaf-tip burn.

Can I still grow a backyard avocado or citrus tree on well water here?

Often yes, but water chemistry matters. Avocado and citrus are notably salt-sensitive, so we recommend testing for salinity and chloride first. Where levels are elevated, blending, additional leaching, and appropriate filtration can make the difference between thriving trees and steady decline.

Should I repair my old well or replace it?

That depends on the well's depth, condition, yield, and water quality. A diagnostic visit lets us evaluate the casing, pump, and chemistry before recommending repair, rehabilitation such as hydrofracturing, full replacement, or proper decommissioning. We give an honest assessment rather than defaulting to the most expensive option.
