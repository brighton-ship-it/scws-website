What a Booster Pump Actually Does

A booster pump is a second pump that raises the water pressure after water has already left your well. Your submersible well pump lifts water up out of the ground and into a pressure tank; the booster pump takes it from there and pushes it through the house at the pressure you actually want at the fixtures. On many Fontana properties the well pump does its job perfectly well, yet the water still arrives at the house weak. That is almost always a pressure problem, not a supply problem, and it is exactly what a booster is built to solve.

Most household plumbing is designed to run comfortably somewhere in the 45 to 65 psi range. When your system is delivering 25 or 30 psi, you feel it immediately: a shower that turns to a trickle the moment someone starts the dishwasher, upstairs faucets that barely run, and irrigation zones that will not pop up. A booster pump closes that gap, holding pressure steady even when several fixtures are running at once.

Constant-Pressure (VFD) Systems Explained

The modern version of a booster is a constant-pressure system driven by a variable frequency drive, or VFD. A traditional pump runs at a single speed and cycles on and off between a low and high pressure setpoint, which is why older systems surge and sag as demand changes. A VFD-controlled booster instead varies the motor speed continuously to match how much water you are using, holding a nearly constant pressure at the tap whether one faucet or five are open. For Fontana homes with multiple bathrooms, large landscapes, or several people using water at the same time, a constant-pressure system delivers a noticeably smoother, city-like experience. It also runs the pump more gently, which tends to extend equipment life and can reduce the water hammer that stresses pipes and joints.

Booster Pumps and Pressure Tanks Work Together

A booster rarely works alone. It is paired with a pressure tank that absorbs small demands and keeps the pump from starting every time you run a little water. If your pressure tank has become waterlogged, the pump will short-cycle, which wears it out fast. Any time we install or upgrade a booster in Fontana, we check the tank, the pressure switch, and the overall system together so all the parts are matched. Bolting a powerful booster onto a failing tank or an undersized well simply moves the problem downstream.

Common Fontana Pressure Scenarios We Solve

Because Fontana runs from the flats near Bloomington and the Lytle Creek wash up toward the elevated neighborhoods closer to the foothills, we see a predictable set of pressure situations across the area:

  • Chronic low household pressure. The well produces plenty of water, but every fixture in the house feels weak. This is the classic case for a booster or constant-pressure upgrade.
  • Hilltop and elevated properties. Homes on the higher ground toward the San Gabriel and San Bernardino foothills, and near Rancho Cucamonga and Rialto, fight gravity. Every foot of elevation from the tank to an upstairs bathroom costs pressure, and a booster is often the only practical fix.
  • Long pipe runs from well to house. On larger Inland Empire parcels the wellhead can sit hundreds of feet from the home. Friction over a long line drops pressure before the water ever reaches you; a booster compensates for that loss.
  • Irrigation and outbuildings that starve the house. When the sprinklers or a barn draw water, the house pressure collapses. A properly sized constant-pressure system holds steady across all those demands at once.
  • Two-story homes and additions. Adding a second floor or an ADU raises the pressure demand your original single-story system was never designed for.

What to Check Before You Call

A few simple checks help you describe the problem accurately and sometimes reveal an easy fix:

  1. Read your pressure gauge. Most systems have a gauge at the pressure tank. Note the pressure when no water is running and again while a faucet is open. A big drop under demand points to a pressure or tank problem rather than the well itself.
  2. Check whether it is the whole house or one fixture. If only one faucet is weak, the issue is likely a clogged aerator or a local valve, not your pump. If every fixture is weak, the system pressure is the culprit.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow near the top and solid near the bottom. If it sounds solid all the way up, it is likely waterlogged and short-cycling the pump.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off, or a pump that runs constantly, both signal trouble worth a professional look.
  5. Note recent changes. New irrigation, a room addition, or a drop in pressure that came on suddenly all help us pinpoint the cause faster.

Please leave the electrical side of the system, pressure-switch adjustments, and any wellhead work to a licensed technician. Well pump circuits carry serious voltage, and an incorrectly set switch can quickly destroy a pump.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I need a booster pump or a new well pump?

If your well produces plenty of water but the pressure at your fixtures is weak, you almost certainly need a booster or constant-pressure system rather than a new well pump. If the well itself struggles to keep up with demand or runs dry, that is a well-supply issue. Our diagnostic visit measures both pressure and flow so we can tell you exactly which problem you have before you spend money.

What is the difference between a booster pump and a constant-pressure system?

A basic booster raises pressure but still cycles on and off, so pressure can fluctuate. A constant-pressure system uses a variable frequency drive to adjust the pump speed continuously, holding steady pressure no matter how many fixtures are running. Most Fontana homeowners who want a smooth, city-like feel choose the constant-pressure option.

Will a booster pump fix low pressure on my hilltop Fontana property?

Yes. Elevated properties near the foothills lose pressure to gravity as water climbs to the house and to upper floors. A correctly sized booster pump compensates for that elevation change and restores strong, even pressure throughout the home.
