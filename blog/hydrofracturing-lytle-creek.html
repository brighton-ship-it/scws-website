What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lytle Creek Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Lytle Creek

In Lytle Creek, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Lytle Creek wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing Bedrock Wells in Lytle Creek

Lytle Creek is a canyon community in the San Gabriel Mountains of San Bernardino County, tucked northwest of Rialto and Fontana where Lytle Creek Road climbs from the valley toward the high country near the Cajon corridor. At roughly 2,700 feet, it's a place of cabins, small ranches, and rural homes strung along the creek, many of them served by private wells. The geology here is mixed: the lower canyon mouth is built from deep alluvial wash deposits, while properties higher in the canyon and on the slopes draw from fractured granitic and gneissic bedrock. Hydrofracturing is the right tool for those hard-rock wells — and we'll be honest with you about whether your well qualifies before recommending it.

Is Your Lytle Creek Well in Rock or Wash?

This distinction matters more in Lytle Creek than in most communities. Wells near the canyon mouth and along the broad Lytle Creek wash often draw from loose sand, gravel, and boulders — alluvium that fracturing cannot improve, because there's no solid rock to fracture. Wells set higher in the canyon or into the bedrock walls tap water through cracks in granite and gneiss, and those are excellent fracturing candidates. The first thing we determine is which kind of well you own. A look at your completion report or a downhole camera survey answers the question quickly, and if you're in wash material we'll steer you toward a more appropriate solution rather than sell you a treatment that won't work.

How the Process Restores a Hard-Rock Well

For a bedrock well, we seat inflatable packers in the open-hole section to isolate a target zone, then inject clean water — never chemicals — at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure scours mineral scale and fine sediment out of existing fractures and pushes them open deeper into the granite or gneiss, frequently connecting the borehole to water-bearing seams just beyond its original reach. On the competent crystalline rock of the upper canyon, those reopened fractures tend to hold and keep producing. A typical job runs four to eight hours, and we test flow before and after so the gain is documented on paper.

Why Canyon Wells Lose Yield

Lytle Creek's groundwater is recharged by winter storms and snowmelt funneling down the San Gabriel front, while demand peaks through the hot, dry summer. A bedrock well with a limited fracture network can draw down faster than the rock refills it, leaving owners short in late summer and fall. Over the years, mineral deposits common in granitic groundwater also accumulate in the tightest, most productive cracks nearest the bore, slowly choking the flow. Fracturing tackles both problems by clearing clogged fractures and opening new ones, which gives the well stronger inflow and more storage to ride out the dry months between storms.

Signs Your Well Might Benefit

  • The well produced noticeably more water a few years ago than it does now.
  • The pump short-cycles or sputters air during back-to-back household use.
  • Recovery is slow — it takes many hours for the well to refill after heavy use.
  • You ration water every late summer until the winter rains return.
  • A recently drilled bedrock well never reached a satisfying flow rate.

What to Have Ready

If you can find your well completion report, note the total depth, casing depth, and the original measured yield — these tell us a lot about whether fracturing will help, and they confirm whether your well is in rock or wash. Watch your system over a week of normal use: when does pressure fade, and how long does the well take to recover? Rule out the easy culprits too, since a waterlogged pressure tank, a worn pump, or a stuck check valve can all mimic a low-yield well. We check those during the assessment so you never pay to fracture a well that simply needed a pump.

Why This Requires a Professional

Hydrofracturing uses high pressure and specialized down-hole equipment and is never a DIY project. We begin with a pre-treatment flow test and a downhole video inspection to confirm your casing is sound, to verify you're in bedrock, and to map the fracture zones worth targeting. That inspection also flags problems — casing damage or a collapsed section — that would need to be handled first. A licensed contractor's judgment ensures your money goes toward the fix that will actually solve the shortage.

Lytle Creek Cost Expectations

A new well in this canyon terrain commonly runs $18,000 to $42,000 once drilling and a pump are included, and difficult access can push it higher. Hydrofracturing an existing bedrock well typically costs $3,000 to $8,000 — far less — which is why it's almost always worth trying first. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward the work if you proceed. If your system also needs equipment, expect roughly $2,500 to $5,500 for a submersible pump and $600 to $1,500 for a pressure tank.

Lytle Creek's narrow, winding road and steep, wooded lots can make staging equipment a challenge, and the canyon's flash-flood history means timing matters. We plan each job around your specific site so the rig and high-pressure gear can be positioned safely and the work finished in a single visit. Knowing the canyon's access points and seasonal conditions is part of what lets us complete these treatments efficiently without surprises.

A Common Lytle Creek Situation

A frequent case is the weekend cabin that becomes a full-time residence. The original bedrock well was drilled for occasional use, and once a household lives there year-round, daily demand outpaces it. Before assuming the well is finished, it's worth testing whether fracturing can lift its capacity to support full-time living — in sound rock it often does, sparing the owner the cost and permitting delay of a new well.

The Rock Beneath the San Gabriel Front

The San Gabriel Mountains that wall in Lytle Creek are among the most rapidly rising and heavily faulted ranges in North America, squeezed up along the San Andreas and related faults that run through the nearby Cajon Pass. That intense tectonic activity has thoroughly shattered the granite and gneiss that form the canyon walls, and it is precisely this shattering that gives bedrock wells here their water-bearing fractures. The flip side is that the same forces grind enormous volumes of rock into the sand, gravel, and boulders that fill the canyon mouth and the Lytle Creek wash below — material famous historically for placer gold. For the well owner, the practical takeaway is simple: water in the upper-canyon bedrock moves through fractures that fracturing can open and extend, while water in the lower wash moves through pore space between loose grains, where fracturing has nothing to act on. Identifying exactly where your well sits in that picture is the foundation of any honest recommendation, and it's the first thing our inspection establishes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well is in bedrock or wash?

Your completion report or a downhole camera survey tells us immediately. Wells higher in the canyon and into the rock walls are usually bedrock and good candidates; wells near the wash often draw from loose alluvium, which fracturing can't help.

How much can yield improve?

For sound bedrock wells, successful treatments often raise flow two to five times. We measure before-and-after flow so the gain is documented.

Are chemicals involved?

No. Only clean water is used, so nothing is introduced into the ground that could affect your water quality or the creek.

How long does the improvement last?

In sound rock, gains commonly last many years. Some wells benefit from a later second treatment, but most owners get a long, stable run of improved flow.

Will it affect the creek or my neighbors' wells?

No. Treatment works only the fractures immediately around your borehole and doesn't deplete the broader groundwater system.

What if my well isn't a candidate?

We'll tell you honestly and recommend a better option. We never fracture wells in loose wash material or with damaged casing.
