What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Bankers Hill well.

Rusty water is almost always fixable once you identify the true source — and the source is rarely a mystery if you know what to look for.

Why Well Water Turns Rusty, Orange, or Brown

Discolored water has a handful of common culprits. Telling them apart matters, because the fix for dissolved iron is completely different from the fix for iron bacteria or a corroding casing.

Dissolved Iron

Iron is one of the most abundant minerals in San Diego County groundwater. When iron dissolves into water deep underground where there is no oxygen, it stays invisible — the water often looks perfectly clear at the tap. The moment it hits air, the iron oxidizes and turns orange or rust-colored, then settles as a reddish-brown sediment. This is the classic "clear when poured, rusty after it sits" pattern.

Manganese

Manganese behaves much like iron but stains black, dark brown, or purple-gray instead of orange. It often appears alongside iron and is responsible for the dark specks and slippery black film on fixtures, dishwashers, and inside toilet tanks.

Corroding Steel Well Casing

Older wells were built with steel casing that slowly rusts from the inside out after decades underground. As the casing corrodes, flakes and rust particles wash into the water column. This produces gritty, reddish water that may worsen over time and is often accompanied by sediment.

Corroding Galvanized or Iron Pipes

In older Bankers Hill homes and throughout uptown San Diego, the original plumbing is frequently galvanized steel. Over 40 to 70 years, the zinc coating wears away and the steel underneath rusts, shedding orange particles into your water. This is one of the most common reasons a home on clean city water still gets rusty water from one or two faucets.

Oxidized Sediment

Sometimes the rust is simply accumulated iron sediment that has built up in a pressure tank, water heater, or a low spot in the pipes, then breaks loose when flow changes — for example when a pump kicks on or a fire hydrant is used nearby. The water runs dirty for a few minutes, then clears.

Iron Bacteria

Iron bacteria are harmless-to-health but troublesome organisms that feed on iron in the water. They leave behind a tell-tale reddish-brown slime, an oily rainbow sheen on standing water, and a musty, swampy, or sewage-like smell. You will often find the slime clogging toilet tanks, screens, and the inside of the well. This is a biological problem, not just a mineral one, and it needs a different fix.

How to Tell the Causes Apart

You can narrow down the source before anyone ever visits, just by paying attention to a few patterns:

  • Clear at first, then turns rusty as it sits in a glass: dissolved iron oxidizing in the air.
  • Already rust-colored the instant it leaves the tap: oxidized sediment, corroding casing, or corroding pipes.
  • Black or dark-brown staining: manganese, often with iron.
  • Slimy reddish gunk, rainbow sheen, and a musty smell: iron bacteria.
  • Rusty at only one faucet: a localized pipe or fixture problem, not your well or main supply.
  • Rusty only on the hot side: almost always a failing water heater anode rod or a rusting tank.
  • Rusty across the whole house, all taps: the source is the well, the casing, the main service line, or the incoming water.
  • Worse after the water sits unused (first thing in the morning, or after a weekend away): corroding pipes or sediment settling and re-suspending.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of simple investigation can save a service call or help us solve your problem faster. Work through these in order:

  1. Hot or cold? Run only cold water at a tap. If the cold runs clear and only the hot is rusty, the problem is your water heater, not your well.
  2. One tap or every tap? Check several faucets around the house. If only one is affected, the issue is the pipe or fixture serving that location.
  3. After sitting unused: Note whether the rust is worst first thing in the morning and clears after running for a minute or two — a sign of corroding pipes or settled sediment.
  4. Inspect the water heater: If your heater is more than 8 to 12 years old, a corroded anode rod or rusting tank is a likely suspect. Draining a bucket from the tank's bottom valve will show heavy rust.
  5. Look for slime: Lift the toilet tank lid. Reddish slime or a rainbow film points to iron bacteria.
  6. Check the toilet and tub: Orange rings and stains that keep coming back confirm an ongoing iron source rather than a one-time event.

Treatment and Repair Options

The right fix depends entirely on the cause. Here is how we approach each one.

Sediment Filtration

For oxidized rust particles, casing flakes, and grit, a whole-house sediment filter traps the particles before they reach your fixtures. This is the most affordable first line of defense, typically running 300 to 900 dollars installed. It will not remove dissolved iron, only the particles you can already see.

Iron and Manganese Filters

For dissolved iron and manganese, a dedicated oxidizing filter (air-injection or a media-based system) converts the dissolved metal into a filterable solid and removes it. These systems generally run 1,500 to 3,500 dollars depending on water chemistry and household size.

Water Softeners

A water softener can remove modest amounts of dissolved iron along with the hardness minerals that are common in San Diego County groundwater. Softeners fall in the same 1,500 to 3,500 dollar range and are often paired with an iron filter when iron levels are high.

Chlorination and Shock Chlorination for Iron Bacteria

Iron bacteria cannot be filtered away — the colony has to be killed. Shock chlorination floods the well and plumbing with a strong chlorine solution to destroy the bacteria, followed by a thorough flush. In persistent cases we install a continuous chlorination system. Knocking out iron bacteria also removes the musty smell and the slime.

Casing and Pipe Repair or Replacement

When the rust comes from a corroded steel casing, the casing may need to be cleaned, lined, or in severe cases replaced. Inside the home, replacing aging galvanized pipe with PEX or copper permanently eliminates the household source of rust. We can also address related well-system parts that fail in mineral-rich water: a pressure switch runs 150 to 350 dollars, a pressure tank 600 to 1,500 dollars, a submersible pump 2,500 to 5,500 dollars, and a constant-pressure or booster system 2,000 to 4,500 dollars.

Major Well Work

If a well is failing beyond repair, a new turnkey well runs 18,000 to 42,000 dollars, hydrofracturing to improve a low-yield well runs 3,000 to 8,000 dollars, and properly abandoning and decommissioning an old well runs 1,500 to 5,000 dollars.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rusty well water safe to drink?

Iron and manganese at the levels typically found in well water are not considered a health hazard, so rusty water is usually safe to drink, though it tastes metallic and unpleasant. The bigger concern is what the rust may signal — a corroding casing, iron bacteria, or other water-quality issues — which is why testing is worthwhile. If you ever see rust appear suddenly along with a change in smell or taste, stop drinking it and have the water tested.

Why is only my hot water rusty?

When only the hot water is discolored and the cold runs clear, the culprit is almost always your water heater. The sacrificial anode rod inside the tank corrodes over time, and an aging tank can rust internally, tinting the hot water orange. Flushing the tank and replacing the anode rod — or the heater itself if it is old — resolves it without touching your well.

My water is clear from the tap but turns orange after sitting. Why?

That is the signature of dissolved iron. Underground, iron stays dissolved and invisible because there is no oxygen. Once the water meets air in your glass, the iron oxidizes and turns orange, then settles as rust-colored sediment. An iron-removal filter is the standard fix.

What is the reddish slime in my toilet tank?

Reddish-brown slime, an oily rainbow sheen, and a musty smell are classic signs of iron bacteria. These organisms feed on iron in the water and leave slimy deposits that clog fixtures. They are not a direct health threat, but they cause odors and staining and require shock chlorination to eliminate — filtration alone will not remove them.

Can a Bankers Hill home on city water still get rusty water?

Yes. Many older uptown San Diego homes were built with galvanized steel plumbing that corrodes from the inside after several decades, shedding rust into otherwise clean municipal water. If the rust shows up at only certain taps or worsens after the water sits overnight, aging interior pipe is the likely cause, and repiping with PEX or copper is the lasting fix.

How much does it cost to fix rusty well water?

It depends on the cause. A basic sediment filter runs 300 to 900 dollars, while an iron or manganese filter or a softener runs 1,500 to 3,500 dollars. Shock chlorination for iron bacteria and pipe or casing repairs vary by situation.
