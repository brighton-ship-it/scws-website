What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Santee Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Despite the shared name, water-well hydrofracturing has nothing to do with the chemical-laden oil and gas operations you may have read about. We use nothing but clean water. No additives, no proppants, no chemicals go down the borehole.

The procedure is straightforward. A crew lowers an inflatable device called a packer into your well casing and seats it at a chosen depth. Inflating the packer seals off the section below it so that all the force we apply is concentrated where it counts. Then we pump clean water into that isolated zone at 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure does two useful things at once: it widens the natural fractures already threading through the granite, and it flushes out the silt, fine clay, and mineral scale that have been quietly choking those cracks for years. Sometimes a second or third zone is treated higher up the bore so the entire water-bearing interval gets attention.

Because the rock around Santee is fractured crystalline bedrock rather than a porous aquifer, those fractures are the plumbing. Open and clean them, and groundwater that was creeping toward your well now has wider, clearer channels to travel through. Start to finish, a typical job runs about four to eight hours, and most wells can be put back into normal service the same day or the next.

Common Low-Yield Scenarios in Santee-Area Wells

We see a handful of patterns over and over on the bedrock wells ringing Santee. If any of these sound familiar, hydrofracturing is worth a serious look.

  • The slow decline. A well that produced comfortably a decade ago now sputters by late summer. Mineral scale and fine sediment have gradually sealed the fractures. This is the classic case where reopening those pathways restores much of the lost capacity.
  • The disappointing new well. A freshly drilled hole in foothill granite came in weaker than hoped. The driller hit rock with tight, poorly connected fractures. Fracturing frequently links those isolated seams into a more productive network.
  • The recovery crawl. You can draw water, but after the pump runs the well takes hours to refill. That slow recovery points to restricted flow through the surrounding rock, which is exactly what fracturing addresses.
  • The seasonal squeeze. The well keeps up in spring but falls behind during the dry San Diego County summer when the regional water table drops. Improving the connection to deeper fractures often buys back the margin you need.

There is one important exception worth stating plainly. A handful of parcels sit right on the valley floor along the San Diego River, where the well may be finished in loose alluvium, sand, and gravel rather than rock. Hydrofracturing does not help those wells, because there are no rock fractures to open, and high pressure in loose material can actually cause problems. Alluvial valley wells are usually better served by deepening, mechanical or chemical rehabilitation, a new screen, or treatment for sediment. We will tell you honestly which category your well falls into before anyone fires up a pump.

What to Check Before You Call

A little homework helps us help you faster, and sometimes it points to a simpler fix than fracturing. Take a few minutes to note the following:

  • Your well log or drilling report. The total depth and the formation noted by the driller tell us immediately whether you are in bedrock or alluvium. Hard-rock domestic wells around here typically run 200 to 600 feet deep.
  • When and how the flow changed. A gradual fade over years suggests scaling and is a strong fracturing candidate. A sudden drop overnight more often signals a pump, pressure switch, or electrical fault rather than the well itself.
  • Pump behavior. If the pump rapidly cycles on and off, or runs and then loses prime, jot that down. It steers the diagnosis.
  • Storage and pressure equipment. A waterlogged pressure tank or a failing pressure switch can mimic a low-yield well. These are inexpensive to rule out.

None of this requires special tools. A clear picture of the symptoms lets our technician arrive prepared and keeps your diagnostic visit efficient.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing is not a do-it-yourself project. It involves pressures high enough to injure a person and equipment that must be operated correctly to protect your casing. Beyond safety, California requires that this work be performed by a licensed contractor, and a botched attempt can damage a well that was otherwise salvageable.

Call a professional when production has clearly declined, when a new well underperformed, when recovery after pumping has slowed, or simply when you are weighing fracturing against the much larger expense of drilling a replacement. A proper assessment includes measuring your current yield, and where useful a downhole camera survey to inspect the casing and locate the productive fracture zones. That diagnosis tells us, and you, whether fracturing is the right move or whether your money is better spent elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing work on my well near Santee?

If your well is drilled into the granitic or fractured crystalline rock common in the Santee-area foothills and back-country, it is a good candidate. Roughly seven to eight of every ten such wells we treat see meaningful improvement. Wells finished in the loose alluvium of the valley floor along the San Diego River are the exception and are better addressed by other methods.

Is hydrofracturing safe for my drinking water?

Yes. The process uses only clean water under pressure. No chemicals or additives are introduced into your well, so there is nothing to contaminate your drinking supply.

How much will my well's flow improve?

Results vary with the geology, but a common outcome on bedrock wells is a two to five times increase over the pre-treatment flow rate. We measure your yield before and after so the change is documented, not guessed.

How long does the job take?

Most residential hydrofracturing jobs take about four to eight hours on site. Many wells can return to normal use the same day, and we typically verify the improved yield within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Fracturing an existing well generally costs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000. That is why we recommend exploring fracturing before committing to a replacement.

What if hydrofracturing is not right for my well?

We will tell you. If your well sits in alluvium or the assessment points to a pump, pressure, or treatment issue instead, we will recommend the fix that actually solves your problem rather than selling you a service you do not need.
