What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Fairmount Village Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing uses high-pressure clean water — and only water — to rejuvenate a well. We seal a section of the open bedrock borehole with an inflatable packer and inject water at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure clears out the silt, scale, and fine debris that have plugged the rock's natural fractures, and it opens and links those cracks to a wider water-bearing zone, so more groundwater can reach the well.

In the fractured rock beneath San Diego's mesas and canyons, water moves through fractures rather than through solid stone. A well that intersected a poorly connected fracture set can be dramatically improved simply by connecting it to better ones — which is exactly what fracturing does. Treatment usually runs four to eight hours on site, with the well back to normal within a day or two.

Why Wells in This Terrain Lose Yield

  • Fracture plugging: Years of mineral buildup and sediment seal the fractures feeding a bedrock well.
  • Canyon-edge variability: Fracture density changes sharply near the canyons that cut through City Heights, so two nearby wells can yield very differently.
  • Marginal original completion: Some wells caught only a thin fracture from the start and were modest producers from day one.
  • Dry-season decline: When the regional water table drops, a marginal well is the first to run short under summer demand.

Signs Your Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Yield has fallen off compared with past years.
  • The pump short-cycles or runs much longer than it used to.
  • Air, sputtering, or sediment appears at the taps under heavy use.
  • The well recovers slowly after a long draw.
  • You've gotten a new-well quote and want to try a cheaper fix first.

The technique is meant for wells finished in hard, fractured rock. Wells set in loose alluvium or terrace sand respond better to redevelopment or pump service. Our diagnostic determines which applies to your well before any pressure equipment is deployed.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Find the well log — depth, casing, static water level. San Diego County and the state DWR keep records if you don't have the driller's report.
  2. Rule out cheap failures — a pressure switch ($150–$350) or a waterlogged tank ($600–$1,500) can mimic a failing well.
  3. Observe the pump — rapid cycling signals the well is being drawn down faster than it refills.
  4. Note the pattern — gradual decline points to the formation; a sudden loss points to equipment.

Urban-Edge Wells and Why They Matter

People are sometimes surprised that hydrofracturing is relevant anywhere near a built-up neighborhood like Fairmount Village. The reality is that San Diego's canyon-cut topography leaves pockets of property — on canyon rims, in unincorporated islands, and on the larger lots that predate dense subdivision — where a private well is still the water source or a valuable backup. For those owners, the same fractured granitic and metavolcanic rock that defines the wider region sits just below the surface, and the same yield problems and solutions apply.

Wells in this kind of terrain tend to be older, which means decades of mineral scale and sediment have had time to seal off the fractures. That accumulation is reversible. A high-pressure flush clears the plugged cracks and reconnects the borehole to the surrounding water-bearing rock. We frequently find that a well written off as "tapped out" was really just choked, and a single treatment brings it back to a useful flow.

Because urban-edge lots are tighter, we pay particular attention to access and to protecting your property during the work. Our crews set up efficiently, contain the discharge water, and leave the site clean — the same standard we hold on rural ranch wells, scaled to a residential setting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really private wells around Fairmount Village?

While much of City Heights is on municipal water, properties on the canyon edges and in nearby unincorporated pockets do use private wells, and we service wells throughout the surrounding San Diego County area.

Does fracturing use any chemicals?

No. The process uses only clean water under pressure. We disinfect the well afterward and you can have the water tested.

Will it harm my casing?

No — the pressure is applied to the rock below the casing, which is isolated with packers. We camera-inspect the casing first to confirm it's sound.

How quickly will I know if it worked?

We measure flow before treatment and again within 24–48 hours afterward, so you get a documented comparison.

How long do results last?

Often many years, depending on the rock and how hard the well is pumped.

What if my well isn't a good candidate?

We'll tell you before treating. Wells in loose sediment respond better to redevelopment or pump work, which we also handle.
