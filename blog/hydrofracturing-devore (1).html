What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Devore Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Devore

In Devore, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Devore wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Bedrock Wells in Devore

Devore sits at the mouth of Cajon Pass in San Bernardino County, around 2,000 feet, where the San Gabriel and San Bernardino mountain ranges come together near the junction of Interstate 15 and Interstate 215. Known to many for Glen Helen Regional Park and the amphitheater, Devore is also a community of foothill homes, horse properties, and rural parcels — many on private wells. The local geology is split between the deep alluvial fans that spill out of Cajon Wash and the fractured granitic and metamorphic bedrock of the slopes above, including the Devore Heights area. Hydrofracturing can substantially boost a hard-rock well's yield, and the honest first step is determining whether your well draws from that bedrock or from loose wash material.

Bedrock or Alluvial Fan? Why It Decides Everything

In Devore this question is central. Wells set into the alluvial fans near the wash draw water from pore space between sand, gravel, and cobbles — there's no solid rock to fracture, so hydrofracturing can't help them. Wells drilled into the granitic and metamorphic bedrock on the surrounding slopes produce water through cracks and joints, and those are exactly the wells fracturing improves. Before we recommend anything, we confirm which type you have. Your well completion report or a downhole camera survey settles it quickly, and if you're in fan deposits we'll point you toward a better-suited solution instead of selling a treatment that won't deliver.

How Hydrofracturing Works on Hard Rock

For a bedrock well, we lower inflatable packers into the open-hole section to isolate a target zone, then inject clean water — never chemicals — at pressures between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. That pressure flushes mineral scale and silt out of existing fractures and propagates them deeper into the rock, often linking the borehole to water-bearing seams just beyond its original reach. On the competent crystalline rock of the Devore slopes, those reopened fractures tend to hold and keep producing reliably. A typical treatment takes four to eight hours, and we measure flow before and after so the improvement is documented.

Why Foothill Wells Slow Down

Cajon Pass funnels winter storms through Devore, and that precipitation, along with mountain runoff, recharges the local groundwater; demand then climbs through the long, dry, often windy summer. A bedrock well with a limited fracture network can draw down faster than the rock refills it, leaving owners short in late summer and fall. Over time, mineral deposits in granitic groundwater also settle into the narrowest, most productive cracks near the bore, gradually throttling the flow. Fracturing addresses both by clearing the clogged fractures and opening new ones, giving the well stronger inflow and more storage to bridge the dry season.

Signs Your Devore Well May Benefit

  • The well produced more water a few years ago than it does today.
  • Filling stock troughs and watering the property leaves the house short.
  • Recovery is slow — the well takes many hours to refill after heavy use.
  • You ration water every late summer until the winter storms return.
  • A recently drilled bedrock well never reached a satisfying flow rate.

What to Gather Before the Assessment

Locate your well completion report if possible and note the total depth, casing depth, and the original yield — these reveal a great deal about whether fracturing will help and whether you're in bedrock or fan. Track your system over a week of normal use: when does pressure fade, and how long until it recovers? Rule out the simple causes too, since a waterlogged pressure tank, a tired pump, or a failed check valve can all imitate a low-yield well. We confirm those during the visit so you never pay to fracture a well that only needed a pump.

Why a Licensed Contractor Is Essential

Hydrofracturing involves high pressure and specialized down-hole equipment and is never a do-it-yourself project. We start with a pre-treatment flow test and a downhole video inspection to verify your casing is sound, confirm you're in bedrock, and map the fracture zones worth targeting. That same inspection often catches problems — casing corrosion, a collapsed section, or an equipment fault — that need attention first. A professional assessment ensures your money goes toward the fix that will actually restore your water.

The San Andreas Fault and Devore's Fractured Rock

Cajon Pass exists because the San Andreas fault slices directly through it, and the relentless movement along that fault and its many splays has fractured the bedrock of the surrounding ranges over millions of years. For the well owner on Devore's slopes, that geologic violence is actually helpful: it created the dense fracture networks through which groundwater travels and from which a well draws. A well that sits near such a fractured zone but only catches its margin can frequently be improved dramatically by hydrofracturing, which extends the borehole's connection into the more heavily broken rock nearby. Our downhole inspection is designed to find those productive zones so the high-pressure injection is aimed where it will do the most good, rather than applied blindly along the length of the hole. This targeted approach is a big part of why fracturing succeeds so often in faulted terrain like Devore's.

A Common Devore Scenario

One pattern we see is the horse property that grows beyond what its well was built for. The original bedrock well comfortably handled a couple of animals and a modest household, but as the owner adds livestock, a garden, or a second dwelling, the well falls behind. Before drilling a new bore, it's worth testing whether fracturing can raise the existing well's capacity to match the load — in sound rock it often does, sparing the cost and the permitting timeline of a new well.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Devore well is in bedrock?

Your completion report or a downhole camera survey tells us right away. Slope and Devore Heights wells are usually in fractured bedrock and good candidates; wells near the wash often draw from loose fan deposits, which fracturing can't help.

How much can my yield improve?

For sound bedrock wells, successful treatments often raise flow two to five times over the pre-treatment rate. We document before-and-after flow so the gain is clear.

Are any chemicals used?

No. Only clean water is injected, so nothing is introduced into the ground that could affect your water quality.

How long does the improvement last?

In sound rock, gains commonly last many years. Some wells benefit from a later second treatment, but most owners get a long, stable run of improved flow.

Will it affect nearby wells?

No. Treatment works only the fractures immediately around your borehole and doesn't deplete the broader aquifer your neighbors share.

What if my well isn't a candidate?

We'll tell you honestly and recommend a better option. We never fracture wells in loose fan material or with damaged casing.
