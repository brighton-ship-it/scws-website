What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Garner Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Garner Valley wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Ranch and Home Wells in Garner Valley

Garner Valley stretches along Highway 74 in the southern San Jacinto Mountains of Riverside County, a long, high meadow at roughly 4,300 to 4,600 feet bordered by the jagged Desert Divide to the north and Thomas Mountain to the south. It is ranch and equestrian country — sagebrush flats, Jeffrey pines, and properties large enough for horses, pasture, and a home or two. Almost all of Garner Valley relies on private wells, and most of those wells draw from fractures in the granitic bedrock that underlies the San Jacinto range. When a Garner Valley well falls behind the heavy demand of livestock, pasture irrigation, and a household, hydrofracturing is usually the most economical way to restore its output without the cost of drilling a new well.

The San Jacinto Granite Beneath Your Property

The San Jacinto Mountains are a great fault-bounded block of granitic rock — chiefly granodiorite and quartz diorite — that has been pushed up along the San Jacinto fault zone. Beneath Garner Valley's meadow soils, that hard crystalline rock carries water only in its fractures: joints, shear zones, and the cracks associated with the faults that define the valley. A well's yield is determined by how many of those water-bearing fractures the borehole intersects. This is why neighboring ranches can have very different well performance, and why a deeper hole isn't always a better one. Hydrofracturing improves a well by reopening sediment-clogged fractures and extending the borehole's reach into the surrounding fracture system.

How the Process Restores Flow

We position inflatable packers in the open-hole section of the well to isolate a target zone, then inject clean water — never chemicals — at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure scours mineral scale and silt from existing fractures and pushes them open farther into the rock, frequently connecting the well to productive seams just beyond its original reach. On the competent granite of the San Jacinto range, those reopened fractures tend to hold and keep producing well. A typical Garner Valley treatment runs four to eight hours, and we test flow before and after to document the change.

The Water Demands of Equestrian Properties

Garner Valley wells often serve unusually thirsty properties. Horses drink heavily, especially through the hot, dry months; pastures and paddocks may need irrigation; and many parcels also support a household, guest quarters, or a second residence. A well that was adequate when a property had a couple of animals can fall short once a small operation grows into a working ranch. Rather than drill a new bore, owners frequently find that fracturing the existing well lifts its capacity enough to carry the whole place — keeping troughs full and pastures green through the dry season.

Signs Your Garner Valley Well Needs Attention

  • Declining yield: the well produced more water a few years ago than it does now.
  • Can't run everything at once: filling troughs and watering pasture leaves the house short.
  • Slow recovery: the well draws down quickly and refills slowly after heavy use.
  • Seasonal shortfalls: the well runs low every late summer and early fall before the winter rains.
  • A new well that underperformed: drilled into rock but never reached a satisfying flow.

What to Have Ready Before We Visit

Find your well completion report if you can, and note the total depth, casing depth, and the original measured yield. Watch your system over a week of normal ranch use: when does pressure drop, and how long does the well take to recover? Rule out the simple culprits, too — a waterlogged pressure tank, a failing pump, or a bad check valve can all mimic a low-yield well. We verify those during the assessment so you never pay to fracture a well that just needed a new pump.

Why This Isn't a DIY Job

Hydrofracturing uses high pressure and specialized down-hole equipment and must be done by a licensed contractor. We start with a pre-treatment flow test and a downhole video inspection to confirm your casing is sound and to map the fracture zones worth targeting. That inspection also flags problems — casing damage or a collapsed section — that would need to be addressed first. A professional assessment makes sure you're paying for the fix that will actually solve your water shortage.

What It Costs in Garner Valley

A new well in this hard-rock mountain terrain commonly runs $18,000 to $42,000 with drilling and a pump, and ranch wells can run higher. Hydrofracturing an existing well typically costs $3,000 to $8,000 — far less — which is why it's almost always the right first step. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward the work if you proceed. If your system also needs equipment, expect roughly $2,500 to $5,500 for a submersible pump and $600 to $1,500 for a pressure tank.

How Garner Valley's Climate Drives Well Behavior

Garner Valley sits in the rain shadow transition between the wetter west slope of the San Jacintos and the desert beyond, so it sees real winter precipitation — including snow — but long, dry summers. Groundwater is recharged mainly during the wet season, and a well with a limited fracture network can draw down faster than the rock refills it, leaving owners short in late summer and fall. Opening additional fractures gives the well both stronger inflow and more effective storage to ride out the dry months, which is exactly what a horse property or small ranch needs when demand peaks under the summer sun.

A Pattern We See Along Highway 74

A common Garner Valley situation is the property that changes hands and takes on more animals or a larger garden than the previous owner kept. The well that quietly served a part-time resident suddenly has to support full-time ranch life, and it can't keep up. Before assuming the well is finished, it's worth testing whether fracturing can raise its capacity to match the new use — in sound rock it frequently does, sparing the owner a five-figure drilling bill and the permitting delay that comes with a new well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hydrofracturing supply enough water for my horses and pasture?

Often, yes, when the well is in sound fractured rock. Many Garner Valley wells see yield rise two to five times after treatment — frequently enough for livestock, irrigation, and household use. We measure before-and-after flow so you can plan around real numbers.

Is it safe for livestock and land?

Yes. The process uses only clean water — no chemicals — so there is nothing introduced into the ground that could affect your animals, soil, or pasture.

How long will the improvement last?

In sound granite, gains commonly last many years. A well may eventually benefit from a second treatment, but most owners get a long, stable run of improved flow.

Will it interfere with my neighbor's well?

No. Treatment works only the fractures immediately around your borehole; it doesn't deplete the shared aquifer or affect nearby wells.

How soon will I know if it worked?

We run a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours, so you see the result almost immediately rather than waiting weeks.

What if my well isn't a good candidate?

We'll tell you plainly and recommend a better option. We don't sell fracturing for wells in loose formations or with damaged casing.
