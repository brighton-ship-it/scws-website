Rusty Water from Your Kensington Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Kensington tap? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding pipes. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Tap Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron affecting your Kensington home.

Solutions for Rusty Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Rusty Water Solutions for Kensington's Historic Homes

Kensington is one of the most distinctive neighborhoods in the City of San Diego, in San Diego County — a small, leafy enclave above Mission Valley, organized around Adams Avenue and the Kensington Heights area, full of carefully preserved Spanish Revival and Tudor homes from the 1910s through the 1940s. That architectural charm is exactly why rusty water is a recurring topic here: the same vintage homes that give Kensington its character were often plumbed with galvanized steel that is now nearing or past a century old. Virtually every home in Kensington is served by the City of San Diego's municipal water, so discolored water almost never comes from the source — it comes from the aging metal between the meter and your faucet.

For homeowners who love these older houses, that is reassuring. Rusty water in Kensington is usually a plumbing-condition issue, not a contaminated-supply issue, and it is very fixable once you identify the specific corroding component. The trick is separating a one-time disturbance in the city mains from genuine pipe corrosion that needs attention.

How Corrosion Produces Rusty Water

The orange or brown tint is rust — iron oxide — and in a city-served historic home it has a short list of likely origins:

  • Aging galvanized steel pipe. The protective zinc lining inside galvanized pipe erodes over decades. Once the bare steel is exposed, it rusts from the inside, releasing particles into your water and gradually narrowing the pipe. In homes of Kensington's age, this is the single most common cause.
  • Water-heater corrosion. A tank heater's sacrificial anode rod is designed to corrode first. When it is exhausted, the tank rusts and pushes brown water out the hot side.
  • Municipal main flushing or repairs. The City flushes hydrants and fixes mains routinely. Either can stir settled iron-and-mineral sediment in the distribution system, briefly tinting water before it clears.
  • Corroded valves and fittings. Original steel shut-offs and fittings at a single fixture can rust and color the water at just that tap.
  • Stagnant water in little-used lines. A guest bathroom or outdoor line that sits unused can hold rusty water that flushes clear quickly.

True iron-in-the-water treatment — oxidizing filters, air injection, or a softener that also captures light iron — is the right tool when a property actually draws iron-bearing groundwater, which in this part of San Diego is the rare exception rather than the rule. For nearly all Kensington homes the durable solution is repairing or replacing the corroding pipe, fitting, or water heater.

Why Galvanized Pipe Fails in Older Homes

Galvanized steel was the standard residential supply material for much of the early and mid-20th century, and it was genuinely durable for its era. The problem is time. The internal zinc coating that protects the steel slowly sacrifices itself, and once it is gone the exposed steel begins to oxidize wherever water touches it. Rust builds up as a rough, scaly layer that does two things at once: it breaks off in flakes that discolor your water, and it constricts the inside diameter of the pipe so flow drops over the years. In a Kensington home with original plumbing, you may notice the hot water discoloring first, the upstairs fixtures weakening before the downstairs ones, or rust that is worst first thing in the morning after water has sat in the pipes overnight. None of this means the house is failing; it simply means a building system has reached the end of its service life, the same way a roof or a furnace eventually does. Replacing the worst runs, or repiping in copper or PEX, restores both clean water and full pressure.

Common Rusty-Water Scenarios in Kensington

Across this historic neighborhood, the same situations come up again and again:

  • The 1930s home with rust at every tap. Original galvanized supply lines reaching end of life, often accompanied by gradually weakening pressure throughout the house.
  • Brown water only when the hot tap runs. A textbook sign of a water heater whose anode rod is spent and whose tank has begun to corrode.
  • Several neighbors see it the same morning. A hydrant flush or main repair on Adams Avenue or a side street; this clears on its own within hours.
  • One rusty fixture in a remodeled bath. A leftover corroded galvanized stub or steel valve behind an otherwise updated bathroom.
  • Rust after a renovation. Construction vibration or a pressure change during a remodel can dislodge scale inside old pipe, causing a temporary flush of color.

What to Check Before You Call

Run through these quick checks first:

  1. Hot, cold, or both? Hot-only points to the water heater. Both means the source side or shared house plumbing.
  2. One fixture or whole house? A single rusty tap is usually a local fitting; whole-house color points to the main supply pipe or the city main.
  3. Check with neighbors. If the block is affected together, it is a municipal event, not your house.
  4. Flush a cold tap. Several minutes of cold water that runs clear and stays clear indicates settled sediment, not active corrosion.
  5. Note the pressure. Rust combined with weak, declining flow strongly suggests galvanized pipe failing internally.
  6. Date the water heater. A unit past 8–10 years old with rusty hot water is very likely the cause.

When to Call a Professional

Call when the discoloration is constant, comes back after flushing, affects the whole house, or arrives with falling pressure — those are the fingerprints of corroding interior pipe in an older home. Persistent brown hot water means the water heater needs service or replacement. Because Kensington homes are valuable and historic, it is worth having a licensed professional confirm the exact source before any work begins, so you replace only what truly needs replacing. A C-57 contractor can test the water at multiple points, evaluate your supply line and fixtures, and lay out whether a targeted repair, a water-heater swap, or a repipe is the right call.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Kensington home is on city water — why is it rusty?

In an older neighborhood like Kensington, the rust almost always comes from inside the home: corroding galvanized pipe or a failing water heater. The City's treated supply is not the source. A recent hydrant flush or main repair can also cause brief discoloration that clears on its own.

Is the rusty water harmful to my family?

Iron at typical levels is an aesthetic problem, not an acute health hazard, but it tastes metallic, stains fixtures and laundry, and can indicate deteriorating pipe. If it persists or the taste or smell changes, get it tested to be sure nothing else is going on.

Why is only the hot water brown?

That is the signature of a water heater problem. The anode rod has likely worn out and the tank is corroding. Flushing the tank and replacing the anode often resolves it; a badly rusted tank should be replaced.

Does rusty water mean I need to repipe my historic home?

Not always. If only one fixture is affected, it may be a single corroded fitting. Whole-house rust with weak pressure, however, usually points to failing galvanized pipe, where a copper or PEX repipe is the lasting solution. We confirm the extent before recommending anything.

Can a renovation cause rusty water?

Yes. Vibration and pressure changes during construction can knock loose scale inside old pipes, producing a temporary flush of rust that typically clears after running the taps. If it lingers, the underlying pipe may need attention.
