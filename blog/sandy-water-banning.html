Sand in Your Banning Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Banning well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Banning well.

Sandy Well Water Solutions for Banning Properties

Banning sits in the San Gorgonio Pass, the dramatic gap in Riverside County where the San Bernardino Mountains to the north and the San Jacinto Mountains to the south nearly meet at roughly 2,300 feet of elevation. For generations, the aquifers beneath this pass have supplied private wells across Banning, Cabazon, Cherry Valley, and the surrounding foothills. The catch is that the water-bearing formations here are largely alluvial fan deposits — decomposed granite, gravel, and sand washed down off both mountain ranges and layered across the valley floor over thousands of years. When you find sand or grit in your Banning well water, that loose, sandy geology is usually part of the story. Understanding why it happens, and acting quickly, is what protects your pump and your household water supply.

How Sand Actually Gets Into a Banning Well

A properly built well is designed to deliver clean water while leaving the sand and fine sediment outside the casing. That job falls to the well screen — a slotted or perforated section of casing set across the water-bearing zone — and to the gravel or sand pack placed around it. When you suddenly notice grit at the bottom of a glass, in the toilet tank, or spraying from an aerator, one of those barriers has usually broken down. In the San Gorgonio Pass, several specific causes come up again and again:

  • Worn or corroded well screen. The pass carries mineral-rich water, and over years the slots in a steel screen can corrode and widen, or the screen can simply wear through. Once the openings are larger than the surrounding formation grains, sand pours straight in.
  • An oversized or aging pump. A submersible pump that moves more water than the aquifer can gently supply creates a strong suction that tears fines loose from the formation. As pumps age and homeowners "upgrade" to bigger units to chase pressure, this over-pumping problem gets worse.
  • A dropping water table. During the long dry stretches common to Riverside County, the standing water level in a Banning well can fall. When it does, the pump sits closer to the bottom of the well and starts drawing from a zone loaded with settled fines and sediment.
  • A pump set too deep. If a pump was originally hung near the bottom of the borehole, every start-up stirs the sediment that naturally accumulates there and pulls it into the system.
  • A cracked or breached casing. Ground movement is a real factor here — the San Andreas Fault runs directly through the San Gorgonio Pass. Shifting ground, corrosion, or an old casing joint can open a gap that lets unfiltered, sandy water bypass the screen entirely.

Common Sandy-Water Scenarios We See Around Banning

After decades of well work across the pass and the wider county, certain patterns repeat. One is the older Cherry Valley or foothill well, twenty or thirty years into its life, where the steel screen has finally corroded enough to let sand through — the homeowner notices grit slowly building in the pressure tank and toilet tanks over a season. Another is the property that added irrigation, a pool, or a second dwelling and put in a bigger pump: within weeks the water turns cloudy and gritty because the new pump is over-drawing the formation. A third is the drought-year surprise, where a well that ran clean for years suddenly spits sand after a dry winter drops the water table and the pump begins pulling from the sediment layer. Each scenario points to a different fix, which is exactly why guessing is expensive.

What to Check Before You Call

You can gather useful information safely from the surface without ever pulling equipment. Run an outside spigot into a clean clear jar and let it settle — note whether you get fine tan silt, coarse sharp sand, or reddish sediment, and roughly how much. Check whether the grit is constant or only appears when the pump kicks on or after heavy use. Look at your pressure gauge: sand problems often ride alongside pressure that swings or drops. Inspect and, if present, clean any existing sediment filter or spin-down screen so you can gauge how fast it re-loads. Write down your well's age, depth, and pump horsepower if you have the records. What you should not do is keep running the system hard — every gritty gallon is sandblasting the impellers inside your pump.

When to Call a Professional

Sand is one of those problems that quietly turns catastrophic. Abrasive grit chews through pump impellers and seals, scores control valves, packs the bottom of the pressure tank, and can burn out a pump motor that is straining against the load. If your water has turned gritty and stayed that way, if pressure is falling, or if you are already replacing sediment cartridges every few days, it is time to bring in a licensed contractor. Because the root cause could be anything from a $300 filter fix to a screen or casing problem hundreds of feet down, a proper diagnosis is the only way to avoid throwing money at the wrong solution.

How the Problem Gets Fixed

The right repair depends entirely on where the sand is entering. Common solutions, roughly in order of scope, include:

  • Sediment and spin-down filtration. A spin-down or cartridge sediment filter installed at the wellhead is often the fastest relief and runs about $300 to $900 installed. It protects the house, but it treats the symptom, not the source.
  • Centrifugal sand separators. For steady sand loads, a centrifugal separator spins grit out of the water before it reaches the tank and appliances, sparing your pump downstream wear.
  • Proper pump sizing and resetting. Frequently the cure is matching pump output to what the well can actually yield and raising the pump to hang above the sediment zone. This alone resolves many over-pumping and set-too-deep cases.
  • Screen replacement or well rehabilitation. When the screen has failed, we can clean and redevelop the well or install a new screen or liner to restore the sand barrier.
  • Pump or component replacement. If sand has already damaged the equipment, a submersible pump runs roughly $2,500 to $5,500, a pressure tank $600 to $1,500, and a pressure switch $150 to $350. A control box or capacitor repair typically falls between $400 and $900.

We start every sandy-water call with a diagnostic visit — $125, credited toward the repair — and a full well inspection runs $150 to $400. If a well has reached the end of its life, a new turnkey well ranges from about $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth and the pass's rocky drilling conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Banning well water normal?

A brand-new or freshly serviced well can shed a small amount of sand for a few weeks as it settles in. But an established well that suddenly produces sand is not normal — it signals a screen, pump, or water-table change that needs attention before it damages equipment.

Can sandy water ruin my well pump?

Yes. Sand is highly abrasive and grinds down the impellers, seals, and bearings inside a submersible pump. Running a pump on sandy water is one of the fastest ways to shorten its life, which is why we recommend addressing it quickly rather than living with it.

Why did my well start producing sand after a dry winter?

Drought and heavy demand can lower the water table in the San Gorgonio Pass. When the level drops, the pump sits closer to the sediment at the bottom of the well and begins pulling in fines it never touched at normal water levels.

Will a sediment filter fix the problem?

A sediment or spin-down filter (about $300 to $900) protects your home's plumbing and appliances and is often the right first step. However, it does not stop sand from entering the well itself, so if the cause is a failed screen or worn pump, the underlying issue still needs repair.

How much does it cost to fix sandy well water in Banning?

It ranges widely with the cause. A filtration fix may run $300 to $900, while pump repairs or replacement fall between $150 and $5,500 depending on the component. Screen or casing repairs and well rehabilitation cost more. Our $125 diagnostic (credited toward the work) pinpoints the real cause first.
