Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Understanding a Citrus Grove's Water Demand

Citrus is a year-round, water-hungry crop, and the numbers add up fast. In the warm inland climate around Romoland, a mature, producing grove commonly uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre each year. Demand peaks from late spring through early fall, when heat and long days push evapotranspiration to its highest. Since one acre-foot is roughly 326,000 gallons, a 10-acre grove can easily consume several million gallons across a single growing season.

From Acre-Feet to Gallons Per Minute

Translating that annual demand into pumping capacity is where planning matters. To keep pace at peak, many citrus operations aim for a well and pump that can sustain roughly 15 to 25 GPM per acre, depending on soil, irrigation method, and storage. Efficient drip and micro-sprinkler systems lower the live flow you need compared with older flood methods, and they make a real difference in a basin where groundwater is shared and regulated.

Frost Protection Is a Different Animal

Citrus is cold-sensitive, and the Perris Valley sees occasional hard freezes. Growers who protect their trees with overhead sprinklers face a sudden, intense demand: frost protection can call for 50 or more GPM per acre, running for hours, usually in the coldest pre-dawn window. That spike dwarfs routine irrigation. A well sized only for summer watering simply cannot deliver it live, which is why frost-protected groves rely so heavily on stored water.

Building the Right Well, Pump, and Storage

A dependable citrus water system comes down to three elements working together: the well, the pump, and storage.

The Well

An agricultural well is engineered for sustained volume and a long service life. Casing, screen placement, and gravel pack are matched to the local formation so the well produces steadily and resists sand intrusion and collapse through decades of heavy seasonal pumping.

The Pump

High-capacity citrus wells typically use a submersible pump set deep in the bore or a vertical lineshaft turbine driven from the surface. Both move large volumes against real lift. Sizing is everything: a pump that is too large can overdraw and damage the well, while one that is too small leaves trees thirsty in peak heat. We size the pump to the well's tested yield, not to a catalog guess.

Storage Ponds and Tanks

Storage is what lets a sensible-sized well serve a real grove. Filling a lined pond or a bank of steel or poly tanks during off-peak hours, then irrigating from that reserve, lets you smooth out demand, ride through short power outages, and meet the enormous frost-protection surge without drilling an oversized well. In rural Romoland, where electrical service can flicker, that buffer is genuine insurance.

Water Quality and Citrus: Salinity, Chloride, and Boron

Citrus ranks among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so testing the water is as important as measuring the flow. Before planting or rehabilitating a grove, have the well water analyzed.

  • Salinity (EC): Citrus handles only modest salinity. Elevated total dissolved solids stress the roots, slow growth, and chip away at yield over the years.
  • Chloride: Citrus is notably chloride-sensitive. Chloride builds up in the foliage and produces the familiar tip and margin leaf burn. Rootstock selection shifts the threshold somewhat, but chloride-heavy water remains a long-term problem.
  • Boron: A micronutrient with a narrow safe range. Citrus can show toxicity when boron rises much past roughly 0.5 to 1 ppm, so even small amounts warrant testing.

When a test raises a flag, the response can range from blending water sources and leaching accumulated salts through careful irrigation, to treatment and rootstock choices. We test Romoland well water and translate the results into practical decisions for your grove.

Drought, Groundwater, and SGMA in the Perris Valley

Romoland sits over the San Jacinto Groundwater Basin, the alluvial basin underlying the San Jacinto, Perris, Moreno, and Menifee Valleys in western Riverside County. That basin is managed under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA). It is rated a high-priority basin, a Groundwater Sustainability Plan covering the area has been adopted and is being implemented, and Eastern Municipal Water District serves as the groundwater sustainability agency across much of it. Parts of the larger basin are additionally adjudicated under the Hemet-San Jacinto Watermaster.

For a grower, the bottom line is that groundwater here is a shared, regulated resource. Efficient irrigation, accurate metering, and well-kept equipment are not only smart economics, they also keep you aligned with long-term basin management. In drought years, static water levels can fall and a pump set too high in the casing may start to break suction or draw air, so it pays to review well performance periodically.

Citrus Well Problems We Commonly See Around Romoland

  • Falling yield and pump cavitation when seasonal water levels drop, particularly in late summer and through droughts.
  • Mineral scaling on screens, columns, and pump parts in mineral-rich groundwater, slowly strangling flow.
  • Plugged drip emitters and micro-sprinklers from sediment, iron, or biological growth where filtration is undersized.
  • Worn or undersized pumps that cannot reach peak summer demand, let alone a frost-protection surge.
  • Pressure-tank and control faults that trigger short-cycling and wear equipment out early.

Maintenance That Protects Your Grove

Citrus wells reward steady upkeep. We recommend periodic flow and water-level testing to catch a slipping well before it becomes an emergency, regular checks of the pump, motor, and electrical controls, and disciplined cleaning of filters and emitters so the irrigation system keeps delivering. When a well loses output to mineral encrustation or fine sediment, well rehabilitation and hydrofracturing can frequently bring back lost yield for a fraction of the cost of a new well. Recurring water testing also lets you watch salinity and chloride trends so they never sneak up on the trees.

When to Call a Professional

Reach out when you see dropping pressure or yield, air sputtering through the lines, sand or cloudy water, climbing power bills from a laboring pump, or any hint that the well cannot keep up during heat or a freeze. Frequently Asked Questions

How much well capacity does a Romoland citrus grove need?

It depends on acreage, soil, irrigation method, and whether you protect against frost. A common planning target is enough sustained capacity to meet peak summer use, backed by storage. We test the well and size the pump and storage to your specific grove instead of applying a generic figure.

What causes burned tips and edges on my citrus leaves?

That tip and margin leaf burn usually traces back to chloride or overall salinity in the irrigation water, which citrus is especially sensitive to. A water test confirms it, and we can then weigh leaching, blending, treatment, or rootstock options.

Can a little boron really hurt citrus?

Yes. Citrus can show boron toxicity when levels climb much above about 0.5 to 1 ppm, so even modest concentrations in well water deserve a test and a plan.

Do I need a separate system just for frost protection?

If you run overhead sprinklers for freeze protection, you need far higher instantaneous flow than normal irrigation, often 50 or more GPM per acre. Most growers supply that from on-site storage rather than oversizing the well.

What does SGMA mean for my Romoland well?

The San Jacinto Groundwater Basin beneath the Perris Valley is a high-priority basin managed under SGMA with an adopted sustainability plan. Practically, that means efficient irrigation, metering, and good maintenance let you use a shared resource responsibly while keeping the grove productive.

Is it possible to revive a low-yield well without redrilling?

Frequently, yes. Hydrofracturing and rehabilitation can restore capacity in many wells for far less than a new one. We begin with a diagnostic to determine what is realistic for your well.
