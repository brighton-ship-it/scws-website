What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Yermo and the Mojave High Desert

What Hydrofracturing Is and How It Works

Hydrofracturing (sometimes called hydrofracking, though it has nothing to do with oil and gas drilling) is a well-rehabilitation method designed for wells drilled into solid, fractured bedrock. Many wells in California's foothills and mountains draw their water from networks of natural cracks and fissures running through granite and other hard crystalline rock. Over time, those cracks can clog with fine sediment and mineral scale, or a new well may simply have intersected too few of them to produce well.

To open those pathways, a technician lowers a set of inflatable rubber sleeves called packers into the well and inflates them to isolate a target section of the borehole. Clean water is then pumped into that sealed zone at high pressure, typically 1,500 to 3,000 PSI or more. The pressure flushes debris out of existing fractures and can extend them slightly deeper into the rock, reconnecting the well to water-bearing cracks it had lost contact with. No chemicals are used at any point, only clean water, which is one reason it is safe for drinking-water wells. A single treatment usually takes about four to eight hours, and the well is flow-tested afterward to measure the improvement.

The key thing to remember: hydrofracturing works by opening fractures in rock. If there is no rock to fracture, there is nothing for the high pressure to act on. That single fact is what makes geology the deciding factor for any Yermo property.

Why Most Yermo Wells Are the Wrong Fit

Yermo lies within the Mojave River groundwater basin, and that geology is fundamentally different from the fractured-granite country where hydrofracturing shines. The water beneath the valley floor is held in deep alluvial deposits, layer upon layer of sand, gravel, and silt washed down and laid out over hundreds of thousands of years by the Mojave River and the surrounding alluvial fans. According to U.S. Geological Survey studies of the basin near Barstow and Yermo, the productive aquifers here are contained in this unconsolidated sand and gravel, not in fractured stone.

Local wells are often several hundred feet deep, drilled down through that loose material and screened across the water-bearing sand and gravel. There is essentially no solid bedrock to fracture in the upper, water-producing zone. Pumping water at 2,000 PSI into loose sand does not open durable new pathways; the formation simply absorbs the pressure or shifts, and the well sees little or no lasting benefit. For this reason, hydrofracturing is generally the wrong tool for a typical Yermo valley-floor well. Anyone who promises dramatic results from fracking a sand-and-gravel well in this area is, at best, misinformed.

How to Tell Whether Your Well Is in Rock or Alluvium

You usually do not have to guess. Two clues tell the story:

  • Location. Wells out on the valley floor near the Mojave River, Yermo proper, and the Daggett area are almost always in deep alluvium. Wells higher up on the flanks of the surrounding desert mountains are more likely to reach bedrock.
  • The driller's log. Every legally drilled well in California has a well completion report filed with the state. It lists the materials the drill passed through at each depth. If your log reads "sand," "gravel," "clay," and "silt" all the way down, you have an alluvial well. If it shows "granite," "decomposed granite," "bedrock," or "fractured rock," hydrofracturing may be worth considering. We can help you read your log during a free assessment.

Better-Fit Solutions for Alluvial Desert Wells

If your Yermo well is in sand and gravel and the yield has dropped, the good news is that there are several proven fixes that actually match the geology, often for less than the cost of a major fracking job. The right one depends on what the diagnosis turns up.

  • Well redevelopment and cleaning. Over years of use, sand-and-gravel wells accumulate fine silt, mineral encrustation, and biological buildup on the screen and in the gravel pack around it. Surging, brushing, jetting, and airlifting can clear that material and restore much of the original flow. This is frequently the single most effective step for a tired alluvial well, and it is the desert equivalent of what fracking does for a rock well.
  • Screen and casing repair. If the well screen is corroded, collapsed, or filling with sand, or if the casing has split, water and sediment can move in the wrong ways. Repairing or relining the screen and casing can return a well to healthy production.
  • Deepening the well. As regional water levels decline across the High Desert, a once-adequate well can end up too shallow. Drilling deeper into the aquifer, where feasible, restores access to water that has dropped below the old screen.
  • Pump upgrade or repositioning. Sometimes the aquifer is fine and the real problem is an undersized, worn, or poorly set pump. Lowering the pump or installing the right-sized submersible can resolve what looks like a "low well."
  • A new well. When an old well has reached the end of its life, a properly sited and constructed new well is sometimes the soundest long-term investment, especially compared with repeatedly patching a failing one.

The honest point is this: for the typical Yermo property, one of these alluvial-well solutions will almost always serve you better than hydrofracturing. Knowing which one requires a real diagnosis, not a sales pitch.

When Hydrofracturing Does Make Sense Near Yermo

Hydrofracturing is not useless in this region; it is simply specialized. The candidates around Yermo are the bedrock wells drilled higher up near the surrounding desert mountains, away from the deep alluvium of the valley floor. If your property sits on the rocky margins of the basin and your driller's log shows granite or fractured rock, and your well yield has declined or never met expectations, then hydrofracturing may genuinely help. In hard fractured rock, clearing and extending the natural fractures can meaningfully increase flow.

The way to know is a straightforward assessment: we review your well log, confirm the geology, run a baseline flow test, and only recommend hydrofracturing when the rock is actually there to work with. If it is not, we will tell you and point you toward the solution that fits.

Safety First: What to Check Before You Call Anyone

Before assuming the well itself is the problem, rule out the simpler, cheaper causes that often masquerade as a "dying well." Many low-water calls in the desert turn out to be equipment issues:

  • Pump: Listen for whether the pump is running, short-cycling, or silent.
  • Pressure tank: A waterlogged or failed tank causes rapid cycling and weak pressure.
  • Pressure switch: A worn or dirty switch can shut the system down or stop it from kicking on.
  • Leaks: Check for wet spots, running toilets, or breaks in the line between the well and the house.
  • Flow test: A simple flow test tells you how much water the well is actually delivering, which separates a true yield problem from a hardware problem.

Stay safe and never open an energized well head, lift a submersible pump, or work inside a pressure tank yourself; well systems combine electricity, water, and heavy equipment. When in doubt, have a licensed professional take the readings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing right for a Mojave Desert well?

Usually not for a typical Yermo valley-floor well. Most wells here draw from deep alluvial sand and gravel, and hydrofracturing only works on fractured bedrock. For alluvial wells, redevelopment, screen or casing repair, deepening, or a pump upgrade are almost always the better fit. Bedrock wells up near the surrounding desert mountains can be candidates, which is why we always confirm your geology first.

How do I know if my well is in rock or sand and gravel?

Check your well completion report (driller's log), which lists the materials the drill passed through. Sand, gravel, clay, and silt mean an alluvial well; granite or fractured rock means a bedrock well. Location helps too, valley-floor wells near the Mojave River are almost always alluvial. We can read your log with you during a free assessment.

Will hydrofracturing damage my well?

When performed correctly on an appropriate bedrock well, it is safe and uses only clean water, no chemicals. The bigger risk in Yermo is paying for a treatment on an alluvial well where it cannot work, which is exactly why we confirm candidacy before recommending it.

My water pressure dropped suddenly. Does that mean my well is failing?

Not necessarily. Sudden pressure changes are often caused by a failing pressure tank, a worn pressure switch, a pump problem, or a leak rather than the aquifer itself. A quick flow test and equipment check usually pinpoint the cause before any major work is considered.

Hydrofracturing, where appropriate, runs about $3,000–$8,000, and a turnkey new well runs roughly $18,000–$42,000.
