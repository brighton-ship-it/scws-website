Shallow Well Drilling Guide: When Less Depth Means More Value

Published February 1, 2026 | 13 min read

Shallow wells—typically under 100-150 feet—offer an affordable alternative to deep drilling when geological conditions allow. While most of San Diego County requires deep wells due to granite bedrock, certain areas with alluvial soils and high water tables can support shallow wells at significantly lower cost. This guide covers everything you need to know about shallow well drilling, including where it works, costs, pump options, and the trade-offs to consider.

Shallow Well Quick Facts

  • Definition: Wells under 100-150 feet deep
  • Typical cost: $8,000 - $18,000 complete
  • Drilling time: Often 1 day
  • Best locations: Valley floors, alluvial areas, high water tables
  • San Diego viability: Limited to specific areas

What Is a Shallow Well?

A shallow well is one that taps into the upper water table or surficial aquifer rather than deeper confined aquifers. Industry definitions vary, but generally:

Well Depth Classifications

  • Very shallow (under 25 ft): Can use surface jet pumps; rare in San Diego County
  • Shallow (25-100 ft): Requires submersible or deep-well jet pump
  • Moderately shallow (100-150 ft): Transition zone; still economical
  • Standard depth (150-400 ft): Typical for many San Diego areas
  • Deep (400+ ft): Common in backcountry and mountain areas

In San Diego County context, where typical wells run 300-600+ feet, anything under 150 feet is considered shallow and represents significant cost savings when viable.

Where Shallow Wells Work in San Diego County

Shallow wells are only viable in specific geological conditions. In our region, these conditions exist in limited areas:

Areas Where Shallow Wells Don't Work

Most of San Diego County requires deep wells due to:

  • Granite bedrock: Hard rock doesn't hold water near the surface—water is found only in deep fractures
  • Mountain elevations: Julian, Alpine, Cuyamaca—water tables are hundreds of feet down
  • Inland foothills: Ramona, Valley Center, Escondido hills—granite underlies thin soil
  • Backcountry: Campo, Boulevard, Potrero—deep fractured rock systems

If your property is in these areas, a deep well is typically required. Attempting shallow drilling would waste money without reaching water.

Shallow Well Drilling Costs

Shallow wells cost significantly less than deep wells, making them attractive where viable:

Shallow Well Cost Breakdown

  • 25-50 feet: $8,000 - $12,000 complete
  • 50-75 feet: $10,000 - $14,000 complete
  • 75-100 feet: $12,000 - $16,000 complete
  • 100-150 feet: $14,000 - $20,000 complete

*Includes drilling, casing, pump, pressure system, and permits

Why Shallow Wells Cost Less

  • Faster drilling: Soft soil drills quickly—often completed in one day
  • Less casing: Fewer feet of casing material needed
  • Smaller pumps: Shallow depths require less powerful (less expensive) pumps
  • Lower power costs: Pumps use less electricity lifting water shorter distances
  • Easier installation: Pump replacement is faster and cheaper

Shallow Well Pump Options

The pump system for a shallow well depends on depth and water level:

Jet Pumps (Surface Mounted)

Jet pumps sit above ground and use suction to draw water up. They work for very shallow applications:

  • Shallow well jet pumps: Water level within 25 feet of surface
  • Deep well jet pumps: Water level 25-100 feet (uses two pipes)
  • Advantages: Easy to access for maintenance, lower initial cost
  • Disadvantages: Less efficient, limited depth, more noise

Submersible Pumps (In-Well)

Submersible pumps are installed inside the well, submerged in the water:

  • Depth range: Any depth from 25 feet to 1,000+ feet
  • Advantages: More efficient, quieter, longer lasting, self-priming
  • Disadvantages: Must pull pump for service (though easier with shallow wells)
  • Recommendation: Preferred for most shallow wells in San Diego County

For detailed pump information, see our jet pump vs. submersible pump comparison.

Advantages of Shallow Wells

When conditions allow, shallow wells offer several benefits:

1. Lower Initial Cost

The most obvious advantage—shallow wells cost 40-60% less than deep wells. For budget-conscious property owners in suitable areas, this makes water independence accessible at $10,000-$15,000 rather than $30,000-$50,000.

2. Faster Installation

Shallow wells in soft soil often complete in a single day. Deep wells in granite may take 4-7 days. Faster completion means:

  • Less disruption to your property
  • Quicker access to water
  • Lower labor costs

3. Lower Operating Costs

Pumping water from 100 feet uses significantly less electricity than pumping from 400+ feet. Annual electricity savings of $200-$500 add up over the life of the well.

4. Easier Pump Replacement

When pumps eventually need replacement (typically 15-20 years), shallow well pump jobs are:

  • Faster to complete (less pipe/wire to pull)
  • Less expensive (lighter equipment needed)
  • Sometimes DIY-able for handy homeowners

5. Good for Specific Uses

Shallow wells can be ideal for:

  • Irrigation-only systems
  • Livestock watering
  • Secondary/backup water source
  • Construction water supply

Disadvantages and Risks of Shallow Wells

Shallow wells come with trade-offs that must be considered:

1. Seasonal Water Level Fluctuations

Shallow aquifers are directly connected to surface water and rainfall. Water levels may:

  • Drop significantly in summer and fall
  • Decline during drought years
  • Rise during wet winters

Deep wells tapping confined aquifers maintain more consistent levels year-round.

2. Drought Vulnerability

California's periodic droughts hit shallow wells hard. Extended dry periods can cause shallow wells to:

  • Produce reduced flow rates
  • Temporarily go dry
  • Require pump lowering or well deepening

Deep wells accessing regional aquifers are more drought-resilient. Learn about protecting your well during drought.

3. Contamination Risk

Shallow wells are more susceptible to contamination from:

  • Septic systems: Must maintain proper setback distances
  • Agricultural chemicals: Fertilizers and pesticides can infiltrate
  • Surface runoff: Storm water can carry contaminants
  • Bacteria: E. coli and coliform from surface sources

Proper sanitary sealing and regular water testing are essential for shallow wells.

4. Lower Yields

Shallow aquifers often produce lower flow rates than deep fractured rock aquifers. A shallow well might produce 2-5 GPM where a deep well in the same area might yield 10-20+ GPM.

5. May Need Future Deepening

If water tables decline over time (common in growing areas), shallow wells may need:

  • Pump lowering
  • Well deepening (if construction allows)
  • Drilling a new, deeper well

Shallow Well Construction Best Practices

If you're in an area where shallow wells work, proper construction is critical for reliability and safety:

Sanitary Seal

Even for shallow wells, a proper sanitary seal (grouted annular space around the casing) is essential. This prevents surface water from contaminating your well. San Diego County typically requires a minimum 50-foot seal.

Casing Material

Quality PVC or steel casing should extend from the surface to below the water table. Proper casing prevents collapse and contamination.

Well Cap

A secure, vermin-proof well cap prevents insects, rodents, and debris from entering the well. This is especially important for shallow wells close to the surface.

Setback Distances

Maintain required distances from potential contamination sources:

  • 100 feet from septic tanks and leach fields
  • 50 feet from property lines
  • 50 feet from streams and drainage
  • Distance varies by local regulations

Is a Shallow Well Right for Your Property?

Consider a shallow well if:

  • You're in a documented high water table area
  • Neighbors have successful shallow wells
  • You're on alluvial valley floor (not granite hillside)
  • Budget is a primary concern
  • Use is for irrigation, not sole household supply
  • You can accept some seasonal variation

Consider a deep well if:

  • You need reliable year-round water
  • Your area has granite bedrock
  • Neighbors have deep wells (300+ feet)
  • Drought resilience is important
  • Water is your only source (no city backup)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered a shallow well?

A shallow well is typically defined as a well less than 50-100 feet deep, though definitions vary. These wells tap into the upper water table or surficial aquifers rather than deeper confined aquifers. In San Diego County, any well under 150 feet is generally considered shallow compared to typical depths of 300-600+ feet.

How much does a shallow well cost?

Shallow wells typically cost $8,000-$18,000 complete with pump and pressure system, significantly less than deep wells. A 50-foot well might cost $8,000-$12,000, while a 100-foot well runs $12,000-$18,000. Costs are lower because drilling is faster, less casing is needed, and pumps are less expensive for shallow depths.

Can I drill a shallow well in San Diego County?

Only in limited areas. Most of San Diego County has granite bedrock and deep water tables, requiring wells of 300-600+ feet. Shallow wells may work in some portions of Fallbrook, Bonsall, and valley floors with alluvial soil and high water tables. A site assessment is essential to determine if shallow drilling is viable.

What type of pump does a shallow well need?

Shallow wells under 25 feet can use jet pumps installed at the surface. Wells 25-100+ feet typically use submersible pumps installed down in the well. Jet pumps are less expensive but limited to shallow depths. Submersible pumps work at any depth and are more efficient and reliable.

Are shallow wells reliable?

Shallow wells can be reliable in appropriate locations but are more susceptible to seasonal water table fluctuations, drought impacts, and potential contamination from surface sources. They may produce less water during dry summer months and can go dry during extended droughts. Deep wells generally offer more consistent, reliable water supply.

Do shallow wells require more maintenance?

Generally yes. Shallow wells are more prone to sediment issues, bacterial contamination from surface water, and seasonal water level changes. They may require more frequent water testing, occasional chlorination, and careful monitoring during drought conditions. Proper construction and sanitary sealing minimize but don't eliminate these concerns.

Can I deepen a shallow well that's not producing enough water?

Sometimes, but it depends on the well construction and geology. If the well hits bedrock or the casing is too narrow, deepening may not be possible. Often, drilling a new deeper well is more practical and cost-effective than trying to deepen an existing shallow well. Consult a well professional for evaluation.

What are the advantages of shallow wells?

Shallow wells offer lower initial cost ($8,000-$18,000 vs. $25,000-$50,000+), faster drilling time (often completed in one day), less expensive pumps and equipment, and easier pump replacement when needed. For properties in suitable locations with high water tables, shallow wells can be an economical water source.
