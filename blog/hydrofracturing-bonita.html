What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

  • Well runs dry during peak usage

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Bonita's Sweetwater River Valley

Bonita sits in San Diego County's South Bay, tucked into the Sweetwater River valley between Chula Vista and National City and just downstream of the Sweetwater Reservoir. It is one of the few corners of the South Bay that still feels semi-rural: larger lots, horse properties, citrus and avocado trees, and a country character that has mostly disappeared from the surrounding suburbs. Most homes here are connected to municipal water, but a minority of properties — especially older horse ranches and larger parcels along the valley and up on the surrounding mesas — still rely on private wells for irrigation, livestock, and household use. If you own one of those wells and the flow has fallen off, hydrofracturing is one of the tools worth understanding before you spend money on a brand-new well.

Bonita is also a place where honest geology matters more than a sales pitch. Hydrofracturing is genuinely effective for the right kind of well, but it is not a universal fix — whether it helps depends on what your well is drilled into, and in the Sweetwater valley that answer varies block by block. This guide covers how the process works, which Bonita wells are good candidates and which are not, what to check first, and the realistic costs and outcomes.

What Hydrofracturing Is and How It Works

Hydrofracturing (sometimes called hydrofracking) is a well rehabilitation method that uses clean, high-pressure water to open and clean the natural fractures in the bedrock that surrounds a well. It has nothing to do with oil-and-gas fracking: there are no chemicals, no sand or proppants, and no deep shale formations involved. The only thing going down the well is water.

The procedure is straightforward. After the pump is pulled, a technician lowers inflatable rubber packers into the casing or open borehole and inflates them to seal off a target section. Clean water is then injected into that isolated zone at roughly 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure widens the existing hairline fractures, flushes out the silt, clay, and mineral scale choking them, and connects the well to water-bearing cracks it could not previously reach. The packers are repositioned to treat additional zones, and the well is surged and developed to clear loosened debris.

A typical hydrofracturing job runs about four to eight hours start to finish, plus setup and a flow test on either side. Because it relies on opening fractures in hard rock, the technique only works where there is hard rock to fracture — which is exactly where Bonita's geology becomes the deciding factor.

The Bonita Nuance: Valley-Floor Wells vs. Bedrock Wells

This is the single most important thing for a Bonita well owner to understand, and it is where a lot of generic well-service advice goes wrong. The Sweetwater River valley floor is made up of alluvial deposits — layers of sand, gravel, and finer sediment washed down and laid in by the river over thousands of years. A well drilled into that valley-floor alluvium draws water from the spaces between loose grains of sediment, not from cracks in solid rock. Hydrofracturing needs fractures to open; loose sand and gravel have none to open, so injecting high-pressure water into an alluvial well usually accomplishes little and can even disturb the formation. In short, classic valley-floor alluvial wells are generally not good hydrofracturing candidates.

The surrounding slopes and mesas around Bonita are a different story. Climb out of the valley and the formation transitions to bedrock — and bedrock wells draw their water from fractures in that rock. Those are the wells that can respond well to hydrofracturing, because there are real fractures to clean and widen.

So how do you tell which kind of well you have? Two clues:

  • Location and elevation. A well on the flat valley floor near the Sweetwater River is far more likely to be in alluvium. A well drilled on a hillside, mesa, or the higher ground around the valley is more likely to be in bedrock.
  • The driller's log (well completion report). This is the definitive answer. Your well log describes exactly what the driller passed through — "sand and gravel" or "clay" points to alluvium, while "granite," "decomposed granite," "fractured rock," or "bedrock" points to a fractured-rock well. If you do not have your log, California keeps these records and a licensed contractor can often help you locate it.

Private wells in Bonita typically run between about 150 and 400 feet deep, and the deeper end is more likely to have reached bedrock. But depth alone is not proof — the log settles it. We would rather tell you honestly that your well is not a candidate than take your money for a treatment that cannot work.

Signs a Bedrock Well May Need Hydrofracturing

If your driller's log confirms you have a fractured-rock well, the following symptoms suggest hydrofracturing may be worth considering:

  • Declining yield over time. The well used to keep up with the household and irrigation, and now it does not. Gradual decline often means fractures have silted up or scaled over.
  • Slow recovery after pumping. You draw the well down quickly and it takes a long time to refill — a sign water is reaching the borehole through too few open fractures.
  • Dry-season shortfalls. The well runs low in late summer and fall when the local water table drops, then partially recovers in the wet months.
  • Can't keep up with irrigation. Watering pasture, an orchard, or a large Bonita lot pulls the well down faster than it can recover.
  • An under-producing new well. A recently drilled bedrock well that came in disappointingly low is often an excellent candidate, because hydrofracturing can connect it to fractures the drill bit just missed.

Better-Fit Solutions for Alluvial Wells

If your well turns out to be a valley-floor alluvial well, do not lose heart — hydrofracturing simply is not the right tool, but several other approaches often are:

  • Well redevelopment and cleaning. Surging, jetting, and mechanical or chemical cleaning can remove the sediment and biofilm that clog the screen and the gravel pack, restoring flow that has been slowly choked off.
  • Screen and casing work. A damaged, corroded, or undersized screen can starve an otherwise productive aquifer. Repairing or replacing it can make a real difference.
  • Deepening the well. In some cases the well can be drilled deeper to reach a more productive zone.
  • Pump upgrade or repositioning. An undersized or poorly set pump can make a healthy well perform like a weak one. Right-sizing the pump frequently solves what looks like a "low water" problem.
  • A booster system for irrigation. Where the aquifer is fine but pressure or delivery is the issue, a booster pump and storage tank can comfortably handle pasture and landscape demand.

Safety First: What to Check Before You Spend a Dime

Before assuming the well itself is failing, rule out the simple and far cheaper culprits. Many "the well is going dry" calls turn out to be equipment problems:

  • The pump. A worn or failing submersible pump produces less water and cycles oddly. Check whether it is even running properly.
  • The pressure tank. A waterlogged tank with a bad bladder or lost air charge causes pressure swings and rapid pump cycling that mimic a yield problem.
  • The pressure switch and controls. A mis-set or failing switch can choke delivery or short-cycle the system.
  • Leaks. A broken line, stuck irrigation valve, or leaking trough can quietly drain your system and make the well look exhausted.
  • A flow test. The honest way to know what your well is actually producing is a measured flow test. It establishes a real baseline so any later treatment can be judged against numbers, not guesses.

Realistic Success in Fractured Rock

For genuine fractured-rock wells, hydrofracturing succeeds in improving yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, with typical flow improvements on the order of two to five times the pre-treatment rate. Results are verified, not assumed: we run a flow test before treatment to set a baseline and another within about 24 to 48 hours afterward to document the change. Outcomes depend on the rock — a well sitting in well-fractured bedrock tends to respond strongly, while one in tight, poorly fractured rock may gain less. And to repeat the central point: a well in loose sand or gravel is not a candidate, and no honest contractor should promise it results.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing work on my Bonita well?

It depends on what your well is drilled into. Bedrock wells on the slopes and mesas around Bonita can respond well, because the technique opens fractures in rock. Valley-floor wells in the Sweetwater River alluvium — loose sand and gravel — generally are not candidates. The fastest way to know is to check your driller's log; we can also help you find it.

How can I tell if my well is in alluvium or bedrock?

Two clues. First, location: a flat valley-floor well near the river is more likely to be in alluvium, while a hillside or mesa well is more likely to be in bedrock. Second, and definitively, the driller's log (well completion report), which records exactly what the well was drilled through — sand and gravel versus granite or fractured rock.

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure — no chemicals, no sand, no proppants. It simply cleans and widens existing fractures in the bedrock around a residential or agricultural well. It is unrelated to the deep shale fracking used in oil and gas extraction.

How much does hydrofracturing cost, and how does it compare to a new well?

Where it applies, hydrofracturing typically runs about $3,000 to $8,000. A new well, turnkey, runs roughly $18,000 to $42,000. For a qualifying bedrock well, hydrofracturing is well worth investigating before committing to a new well.

What should I check before assuming my well needs work?

Rule out the simple things first: the pump, the pressure tank, the pressure switch, and any leaks. Many "low water" complaints are really equipment problems. A measured flow test (about $125, usually credited toward the work) tells you what your well is actually producing.

How long does the process take and how soon will I see results?

The hydrofracturing itself usually takes about four to eight hours. We run a flow test beforehand for a baseline and another within roughly 24 to 48 hours afterward to document the improvement.
