Bacteria in Well Water: Complete Guide to Testing, Treatment & Prevention

Published February 1, 2026 | 14 min read

Discovering bacteria in your well water is alarming, but it's also one of the most common issues private well owners face. The good news? Most bacterial contamination can be eliminated through proper treatment, and ongoing contamination can be prevented with the right precautions. This comprehensive guide covers everything San Diego County well owners need to know about bacteria in well water—from understanding the types of bacteria to treatment options and long-term prevention strategies.

⚠️ Important Safety Notice

If you've tested positive for E. coli or are experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms, stop drinking your well water immediately. Use bottled water or boil tap water for at least one minute until your well has been properly treated and retested. E. coli indicates fecal contamination and the potential presence of dangerous pathogens.

Understanding Bacteria in Well Water

Unlike municipal water systems that are continuously monitored and treated, private wells can become contaminated without any obvious warning signs. Bacteria can enter your well through various pathways, and because most bacterial contamination is invisible, tasteless, and odorless, regular testing is the only way to ensure your water is safe.

The EPA estimates that approximately 15% of Americans rely on private wells, and studies suggest that up to 40% of private wells contain some form of bacteria at any given time. In rural areas of San Diego County—places like Ramona, Julian, Valley Center, and Alpine—private wells are common, making bacterial contamination a significant concern for many homeowners.

Types of Bacteria Found in Well Water

Not all bacteria are created equal when it comes to health risks. Understanding the difference between types of bacteria helps you know how serious a positive test result is and what action to take.

Total Coliform Bacteria

Total coliform is a broad category of bacteria found naturally throughout the environment—in soil, on plants, and in surface water. While most coliform bacteria are harmless themselves, their presence in well water is a crucial indicator of potential problems.

Why it matters: Coliform bacteria in your well indicates a pathway exists for surface contamination to reach your groundwater. This pathway could also allow dangerous pathogens to enter your water supply.

Action required: Retest to confirm, then treat with shock chlorination if positive. Investigate potential contamination sources.

E. coli (Escherichia coli)

E. coli is a specific type of coliform bacteria that lives exclusively in the intestines of humans and warm-blooded animals. Unlike general coliform, E. coli's presence definitively indicates fecal contamination—there's no other explanation for its presence in your well.

Why it's serious: Where E. coli is present, other dangerous pathogens may also exist, including Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Salmonella, Shigella, and various viruses that cause severe gastrointestinal illness.

Action required: Stop drinking water immediately. Treat with shock chlorination and identify the contamination source. Retest before resuming use.

Other Waterborne Pathogens

While bacteria tests typically focus on coliform and E. coli as indicators, contaminated wells may contain other harmful organisms:

  • Giardia: A parasite causing severe diarrhea, cramps, and nausea that can last weeks
  • Cryptosporidium: A chlorine-resistant parasite causing prolonged gastrointestinal illness
  • Legionella: Bacteria causing Legionnaires' disease, a serious form of pneumonia
  • Hepatitis A: A virus causing liver infection, spread through fecal contamination
  • Norovirus: The most common cause of acute gastroenteritis outbreaks

This is why E. coli contamination is taken so seriously—it's not just about E. coli itself, but what else may have entered your water supply.

How Bacteria Enters Your Well

Understanding contamination pathways helps you both treat existing problems and prevent future ones. Bacteria can enter wells through several routes:

Surface Water Infiltration

The most common contamination source. Surface water carrying bacteria can seep into your well through:

  • Damaged well cap or seal: Cracked, loose, or missing well caps allow insects, animals, and contaminated water direct entry
  • Corroded or cracked casing: Older wells may have deteriorated steel casings with holes or cracks
  • Improper well construction: Inadequate grout seal around the casing or insufficient casing depth
  • Flooding: Surface flooding can overwhelm even properly constructed wells

Septic System Contamination

Septic systems are a primary source of bacteria in well water, especially in rural areas where both are common. Contamination occurs when:

  • Septic systems are located too close to wells (minimum 50-100 feet required)
  • Septic systems are failing, overloaded, or improperly maintained
  • Soil conditions allow rapid percolation of septic effluent toward the well
  • Both well and septic draw from or discharge to the same shallow groundwater zone

Animal and Agricultural Sources

In San Diego County's rural areas, animal-related contamination is common:

  • Livestock operations near wells
  • Pet waste near wellhead areas
  • Wildlife accessing poorly sealed wells
  • Manure application on nearby agricultural land
  • Runoff from animal enclosures or pastures

Well Maintenance Issues

Sometimes bacteria enters during well work or due to equipment problems:

  • Pump or equipment work: Bacteria can be introduced during pump installation or repair if proper sanitation isn't followed
  • Drawdown conditions: When water levels drop significantly, bacteria from shallow contaminated zones may be pulled into the well
  • Biofilm buildup: Bacteria colonies can establish in the well casing, pressure tank, or pipes

Health Risks of Bacterial Contamination

The health effects of drinking bacteria-contaminated water range from mild to severe, depending on the type and concentration of organisms present:

Symptom Category Common Symptoms Typical Onset
Gastrointestinal Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, stomach pain 1-10 days after exposure
Systemic Fever, chills, fatigue, headache, body aches 2-7 days after exposure
Severe (rare) Bloody diarrhea, kidney problems, dehydration 3-10 days after exposure

High-risk groups are more susceptible to severe illness: infants and young children, elderly individuals, pregnant women, and anyone with a compromised immune system. For these groups, what might be a mild illness in healthy adults can become a serious medical emergency.

If multiple household members experience gastrointestinal symptoms simultaneously, contaminated well water should be the first suspect. Contact your healthcare provider and test your water immediately.

Well Water Testing Requirements

Regular testing is the cornerstone of safe well water. California health authorities and the EPA recommend the following testing schedule for private wells:

Recommended Bacteria Testing Schedule

  • Annual testing: All wells should be tested for total coliform and E. coli at least once per year
  • After well work: Test following any pump, casing, or wellhead repairs
  • After flooding: Test whenever surface water may have reached your wellhead
  • After earthquakes: Seismic activity can shift underground formations and open new contamination pathways
  • When symptoms appear: Test immediately if household members experience unexplained GI illness
  • Property transactions: Test when buying or selling property with a well

How to Test for Bacteria

Bacteria testing requires proper sample collection technique to avoid false positives or false negatives. For detailed instructions, see our comprehensive well water testing guide. Key points:

  • Use only sterile containers provided by a certified laboratory
  • Sterilize the sample faucet with flame or bleach solution
  • Run water for 2-3 minutes before collecting
  • Don't touch the inside of the container or cap
  • Keep sample cold (not frozen) and deliver to lab within 24 hours
  • Basic bacteria testing costs $30-$75 at certified labs

Shock Chlorination: The Primary Treatment

Shock chlorination (also called well disinfection) is the first-line treatment for bacteria in well water. This process uses a high concentration of chlorine to kill bacteria throughout the entire well system.

How Shock Chlorination Works

Unlike ongoing chlorination, shock chlorination is a one-time treatment using concentrated chlorine (typically 100-200 ppm) left in the well for 12-24 hours. The chlorine kills bacteria in the well, casing, pump, pressure tank, and household plumbing. The system is then flushed until chlorine levels return to normal.

The Shock Chlorination Process

  1. Calculate chlorine amount: Based on well depth, diameter, and water volume. Typically 1-3 gallons of household bleach for average residential wells.
  2. Prepare the well: Remove well cap. If safe and accessible, scrub the inside of the casing with chlorine solution.
  3. Add chlorine: Pour chlorine solution directly into the well. Mix by running a hose back into the well for 15-30 minutes.
  4. Circulate through plumbing: Run each faucet until you smell chlorine, then turn off. Include all fixtures: sinks, showers, washing machine, outdoor faucets.
  5. Let it work: Leave chlorinated water in the system for 12-24 hours. Don't use any water during this time.
  6. Flush the system: Drain chlorinated water away from septic system and vegetation. Use an outdoor hose to a safe discharge area. Run until chlorine odor is gone.
  7. Wait and retest: Wait 7-14 days, then retest for bacteria. Only resume drinking after a negative test.

DIY vs. Professional Shock Chlorination

DIY Shock Chlorination

Cost: $50-$150 (materials and testing)

Pros:

  • Lower cost
  • Can be done quickly
  • Appropriate for simple systems

Cons:

  • Risk of improper dosing
  • May miss contamination source
  • Safety risks with chemicals

Professional Shock Chlorination

Cost: $200-$400

Pros:

  • Proper dosing for your well
  • Inspection for contamination sources
  • Equipment and safety handled
  • May identify needed repairs

Cons:

  • Higher cost
  • Scheduling required

UV Treatment: Continuous Protection

If shock chlorination resolves the problem, you may not need ongoing treatment. However, if bacteria returns repeatedly, or if you want guaranteed continuous protection, ultraviolet (UV) disinfection is an excellent solution.

How UV Disinfection Works

UV water treatment uses ultraviolet light to destroy the DNA of microorganisms, rendering them unable to reproduce and cause infection. Water flows through a chamber where it's exposed to UV light from a specialized lamp. The process kills 99.99% of bacteria, viruses, and parasites—including chlorine-resistant organisms like Cryptosporidium and Giardia.

Advantages of UV Treatment

  • Chemical-free: No chlorine taste or smell, no disinfection byproducts
  • Effective against resistant organisms: Kills Giardia and Cryptosporidium that resist chlorine
  • Low maintenance: Annual lamp replacement is typically the only required upkeep
  • Instant disinfection: No contact time required—water is treated as it flows through
  • No water waste: Unlike reverse osmosis, UV doesn't produce wastewater

UV System Considerations

  • Pre-filtration required: Water must be clear—sediment or iron can shield bacteria from UV light
  • Power dependent: System only works when electricity is available
  • No residual protection: Unlike chlorine, UV doesn't protect water after treatment (no protection against recontamination in pipes)
  • Lamp replacement: UV lamps lose effectiveness over time and require annual replacement ($50-$150)

UV System Costs

  • Whole-house UV system: $800-$2,000 installed
  • Point-of-use UV (under sink): $200-$500 installed
  • Annual lamp replacement: $50-$150
  • Electricity cost: Minimal ($30-$50/year)

Other Treatment Options

Continuous Chlorination

A chlorine injection system continuously adds small amounts of chlorine to incoming water. This provides residual protection throughout your plumbing system.

  • Cost: $1,500-$3,000 installed
  • Pros: Residual protection, effective against bacteria and viruses
  • Cons: Chlorine taste/odor (removable with carbon filter), ongoing chemical costs, more maintenance

Ozone Treatment

Ozone is a powerful oxidizer that kills bacteria without adding chemicals. It's effective but more expensive than UV or chlorination.

  • Cost: $2,000-$4,000 installed
  • Pros: No chemical taste, very effective, helps with iron and sulfur
  • Cons: Higher cost, more complex system, no residual protection

Preventing Bacteria in Well Water

Prevention is always better than treatment. These measures help keep bacteria out of your well in the first place:

Wellhead Protection

  • Maintain a secure well cap: Install a vermin-proof, watertight cap. Inspect annually for cracks, gaps, or damage.
  • Ensure proper grading: Ground should slope away from the wellhead to direct surface water away
  • Maintain a buffer zone: Keep a 50-foot minimum clearance from potential contamination sources
  • Eliminate standing water: Address drainage issues near the wellhead
  • Fence the wellhead: Keep animals away from the well area

Septic System Maintenance

  • Pump septic tank every 3-5 years
  • Don't overload the system with excessive water use
  • Never drive over or park on the drain field
  • Maintain at least 100 feet between septic and well when possible
  • Address any signs of septic failure immediately

Regular Well Maintenance

  • Schedule annual well inspections to identify problems early
  • Test water quality annually for bacteria and nitrates
  • Address pressure problems or pump issues promptly
  • Follow the maintenance schedule in our well maintenance guide
  • Keep detailed records of all well work and testing

When Bacteria Keeps Coming Back

If bacteria returns after shock chlorination, there's an ongoing contamination source or pathway that needs to be addressed. Common causes of recurring contamination include:

  • Damaged well casing: Corroded, cracked, or improperly sealed casing allowing surface water intrusion. May require well rehabilitation or repair.
  • Inadequate well construction: Shallow casing, poor grout seal, or other construction deficiencies
  • Biofilm in the well system: Bacterial colonies established in the well, pipes, or pressure tank that resist single shock treatments
  • Ongoing contamination source: Failing septic system, animal waste, or other persistent source
  • Shallow well in contaminated aquifer: May need to deepen the well to access clean water

For recurring contamination, a professional well inspection can identify the source and recommend appropriate repairs or continuous treatment systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well water has bacteria?

The only reliable way to know if your well water has bacteria is through laboratory testing. Most bacterial contamination has no visible signs—the water may look, taste, and smell perfectly normal. Annual testing for coliform bacteria is recommended for all private wells. However, if you experience unexplained gastrointestinal illness, notice changes in water taste or odor, or have had flooding near your wellhead, test immediately.

What's the difference between coliform bacteria and E. coli in well water?

Total coliform bacteria are a broad group found throughout the environment—in soil, plants, and water. Their presence indicates a possible pathway for contamination but doesn't necessarily mean harmful pathogens are present. E. coli is a specific type of coliform that lives in the intestines of humans and animals. Its presence definitively indicates fecal contamination and potential exposure to dangerous pathogens like Giardia, Cryptosporidium, or pathogenic bacteria.

Can I drink well water with coliform bacteria?

While most coliform bacteria themselves aren't harmful, their presence indicates your well has a contamination pathway that could allow dangerous pathogens to enter. You should retest to confirm the result, then treat the well with shock chlorination if positive. During treatment, use bottled water or boil well water for at least one minute before drinking. If E. coli is detected, stop drinking the water immediately—this indicates definite fecal contamination.

How much does it cost to treat bacteria in a well?

DIY shock chlorination costs $50-$150 for materials (chlorine, testing supplies). Professional shock chlorination services typically run $200-$400. If bacteria returns repeatedly, you may need well repairs ($500-$2,500) to address the contamination source, or continuous treatment like a UV system ($800-$2,000 installed) or chlorine injection system ($1,500-$3,000 installed). Annual bacteria testing costs $30-$75 per test.

How long after shocking a well is the water safe to drink?

After shock chlorination, let the chlorinated water sit in the well for 12-24 hours. Then flush the system until chlorine is no longer detectable (usually 1-3 days depending on well depth and household plumbing). Wait at least 7-14 days after flushing before retesting for bacteria. Only resume drinking the water after you receive a negative bacteria test from a certified lab. During this entire period, use bottled water or boil tap water.
