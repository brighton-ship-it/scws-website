What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Rimforest Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Rimforest: A Strong Fit for Mountain Wells

Rimforest sits at roughly 5,400 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, perched along Highway 18 between Crestline and Lake Arrowhead. Up here, the ground beneath your feet is not the deep sand-and-gravel basin of the valley floor — it is hard, crystalline granitic bedrock. There are no true alluvial aquifers in this part of the mountains. Instead, the snowmelt and winter rain that fall on these slopes percolate down into a network of cracks and fractures in the granite, and that fractured rock is what private wells tap. For well owners, that geology is both the challenge and the opportunity, because fractured granitic bedrock is exactly the setting where hydrofracturing performs best.

While many Rimforest properties receive treated water piped in by the Crestline-Lake Arrowhead Water Agency, plenty of homes and parcels in and around the community still depend on private wells drilled into the bedrock. When one of those wells starts producing less than it used to, hydrofracturing is often the single most cost-effective way to restore it — and frequently to push yield well above where it started.

Why Fractured Granite Responds So Well

A well in granitic bedrock does not draw from a uniform pool of water. It produces only where the borehole happens to intersect water-bearing fractures. A well that hit just one or two productive seams may yield a modest gallon or two per minute — enough on a wet year, frustrating on a dry one. Over time, those few fractures can also clog with fine silt, iron and manganese precipitate, and mineral scale, slowly strangling the flow.

Hydrofracturing addresses both problems at once. By injecting clean water under high pressure, the process flushes the existing fractures clean and, just as importantly, propagates them outward — extending each fracture deeper into the rock and opening connections to additional water-bearing seams the original drilling never reached. In hard, competent granite like the San Bernardino Mountains, those new openings stay open, which is precisely why the technique fails in loose valley sand but excels here.

It helps to picture the rock as a sponge made of stone — not porous like a kitchen sponge, but riddled with thin cracks where water can travel. The more of those cracks your well connects to, the more water it produces. A freshly drilled hole is like a straw pushed into the sponge at one spot; hydrofracturing widens the contact, linking your straw to far more of the wet network around it. That is why two wells drilled a hundred feet apart on the same Rimforest lot can produce wildly different amounts of water, and why a low producer is so often fixable rather than hopeless.

How the Process Works on a Rimforest Well

The job is methodical. First we pull the pump and run a baseline flow test so we have a documented before-number. Next, a downhole camera survey inspects the casing and identifies the depth of existing fracture zones. We then lower an inflatable packer to isolate a target zone and inject clean water at pressures from roughly 1,500 to over 3,000 PSI. The pressure cleans and extends the fractures; we may treat more than one zone in a single well. The active fracturing typically runs four to eight hours. Afterward we run a post-treatment flow test — usually within 24 to 48 hours once the well settles — and hand you a written before-and-after comparison. No chemicals are involved at any stage; this is clean water only.

Common Scenarios We See Around Rimforest, Crestline, and Lake Arrowhead

  • The well that "went dry" in late summer. Mountain wells often recharge beautifully in spring from snowmelt, then fade by August or September. If your well runs low only in the dry months, fracturing more fracture volume into the rock can carry you through the season.
  • The disappointing new well. Drilling 400, 600, even 800 feet into granite is expensive, and sometimes the borehole simply misses the best seams. Rather than abandoning a deep, costly hole, hydrofracturing it can connect that borehole to nearby productive fractures.
  • The slowly declining old well. Decades of pumping leave mineral scale and silt in the fractures. A well that once gave five gallons per minute and now gives two is a classic candidate for rehabilitation.
  • Iron and manganese buildup. Mountain granite water is often clean and soft but can carry iron and manganese that precipitate in the fractures and on the screen. Fracturing clears the obstruction; treatment then handles any remaining water-quality concern at the tap.

What to Check Before You Call

You can gather useful information before we arrive:

  1. Track your recovery time. After heavy use, how long until pressure returns to normal? A slow recovery suggests limited fracture flow — the situation hydrofracturing targets.
  2. Note the seasonal pattern. Is the shortage year-round, or only in late summer and fall? Seasonal-only shortages point toward a yield ceiling that fracturing can raise.
  3. Find the driller's log. The well-completion report (filed with the California Department of Water Resources) shows depth, casing, and the fracture zones the driller logged — gold for planning a treatment.
  4. Watch for sediment or color. Reddish or black staining can indicate iron and manganese; cloudy water after the pump kicks on can indicate a tired well that needs development.
  5. Check the pressure tank and switch first. Sometimes "low water" is just a waterlogged tank or a failing switch — cheap fixes we always rule out before recommending anything bigger.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Rimforest well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

Very likely. Wells in the San Bernardino Mountains are drilled into hard granitic and crystalline bedrock, which is the ideal setting for hydrofracturing. We confirm with a flow test and, where possible, a camera survey before recommending treatment.

How much can hydrofracturing increase my yield?

In fractured mountain bedrock, gains of two to five times the pre-treatment flow are typical, and larger improvements happen. Results depend on the rock and the number of fractures we can reach, which is why we test before and after.

I just paid to drill a deep well and the flow is poor. Did I waste my money?

Not necessarily. A deep granite borehole that missed the best seams can often be connected to nearby productive fractures by hydrofracturing — far cheaper than starting over with a new well.

Will fracturing damage my well casing or pump?

No, when done correctly. We isolate the treatment zone below the casing with packers and remove or protect the pump beforehand. The pressure acts on the open rock, not on your equipment.

How long does the improvement last?

Because the granite holds the fractures open, results are typically long-lasting — often many years. Wells that carry heavy iron and manganese may eventually need re-treatment or ongoing filtration.

Do you handle the snow and steep mountain access?

Yes. We regularly work the mountain corridor and plan equipment access around grade and weather. Let us know your road conditions when you call and we will schedule accordingly.
