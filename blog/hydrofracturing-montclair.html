What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Montclair Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Montclair: An Honest Local Assessment

Montclair sits at the western edge of San Bernardino County, tucked against the Los Angeles County line between Pomona, Upland, and Ontario. It is a compact, fully developed city on the floor of the Pomona Valley, served by the Monte Vista Water District and the Chino Basin groundwater system. If you have landed on this page hoping hydrofracturing will boost a private well in Montclair, we want to give you a straight answer rather than a sales pitch: Montclair is a poor candidate for hydrofracturing, and here is exactly why.

Hydrofracturing is a technique for wells drilled into bedrock. It works by opening and cleaning the natural cracks in hard rock so groundwater can move into the borehole. Montclair does not sit on bedrock near the surface. It sits on hundreds of feet of alluvial valley fill — sand, gravel, and clay washed down from the San Gabriel Mountains over geologic time. That is wonderful for a regional aquifer, but it is the wrong formation for fracturing. There are no rock fractures to widen in loose alluvium, so injecting high-pressure water simply will not produce the lasting yield gains it does in a granite well.

What Montclair Sits On — and Why It Matters

The Chino Groundwater Basin beneath Montclair is one of the largest and most heavily managed aquifers in Southern California. Production wells here are typically gravel-packed and screened against thick sand-and-gravel layers, not bored into rock. When one of these wells loses capacity, the cause is almost never a sealed bedrock fracture. It is usually one of the following:

  • Screen and gravel-pack fouling — fine sediment, mineral scale, and iron bacteria plug the well screen and the gravel envelope around it.
  • Encrustation — calcium and manganese build up on the screen slots, choking flow.
  • Falling water table — during drought, basin levels drop and a shallow pump loses its column of water.
  • Worn pump or motor — the equipment, not the aquifer, is the bottleneck.

None of these is fixed by hydrofracturing. They are fixed by well rehabilitation methods built for sand-and-gravel wells: mechanical brushing, surging, jetting, chemical descaling, and screen redevelopment — or by replacing tired equipment.

How Hydrofracturing Works (and Where It Belongs)

So you understand the contrast, here is the actual process. A crew lowers an inflatable packer into a bedrock borehole and seals off a chosen section of the hole. Clean water — no chemicals — is then injected into that isolated zone at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes silt and scale out of the rock's natural seams and widens them, so groundwater flows in more freely afterward. On a granite or fractured-rock well, that can multiply yield several times over.

That technology shines in the foothills and mountains — places like Mt. Baldy, Lytle Creek, Wrightwood, or the granite parcels above the valley floor. On the alluvial floor of Montclair, there is simply nothing to fracture. We would rather tell you that now than take your money for a treatment that cannot work on your formation.

Better Solutions for a Low-Producing Montclair Well

If you do own a private well in or near Montclair and it is underperforming, the right fixes usually look like this:

  • Well rehabilitation — brushing, surging, and chemical treatment to clear a clogged screen and gravel pack and recover lost capacity.
  • Pump and motor service — replacing a worn submersible pump that can no longer move the water the aquifer still holds.
  • Lowering the pump setting — if the basin water level has dropped, resetting the pump deeper restores supply.
  • Drilling a new, properly screened well — the durable answer when an old well has reached the end of its service life.

For most Montclair-area homes and small properties on municipal supply, of course, none of this applies — you are on Monte Vista Water District service. But for ag, nursery, and legacy parcels around the valley fringe with their own wells, these are the tools that actually move the needle.

Water Quality and the Chino Basin Context

Even when a Montclair-area well produces plenty of water, the bigger day-to-day issue is usually quality, not quantity. Groundwater drawn from the Chino Basin tends to be hard, with elevated calcium and magnesium, and parts of the basin carry historic nitrate and total-dissolved-solids concerns from a century of agricultural and dairy use upstream. None of that is addressed by hydrofracturing either — it is a treatment question, not a yield question. A properly sized softener, a sediment pre-filter, or a nitrate-selective treatment train will do far more for a Montclair household than any downhole pressure work.

This is the heart of our honesty pledge on this page: the right tool depends on the problem. Low yield in bedrock means hydrofracturing; low yield in valley fill means rehabilitation or a new screen; poor water quality means treatment. We diagnose first so you spend money on the fix that matches your geology and your symptoms, rather than the one that happens to have the most marketable name.

Drought, Demand, and Seasonal Drawdown

Southern California's recurring dry cycles pull regional water tables down, and Montclair is no exception. When basin levels fall during a drought year, a private well that sat comfortably above the water column can start drawing air at peak summer demand. Owners often read that as “my well is failing” and start researching dramatic fixes. In a valley-fill setting, the practical responses are lowering the pump, adjusting the schedule to recover overnight, or in some cases deepening the well — not fracturing rock that is not there. Tracking your static and pumping water levels across a season tells us whether you are facing a true capacity loss or a temporary, weather-driven dip.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Confirm whether you are actually on a private well or on district water — many properties here are connected to municipal supply.
  • Pull the well log if you have one: a screened, gravel-packed completion in sand and gravel confirms you are not a hydrofrac candidate.
  • Check the pressure switch and the bladder tank's air charge first — a waterlogged tank or a failing switch mimics a “weak well.”
  • Note whether yield faded gradually (rehabilitation) or dropped suddenly (likely pump or controls).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you hydrofracture a well in Montclair?

In nearly all cases, no. Montclair sits on deep alluvial valley fill, not bedrock. Hydrofracturing needs rock fractures to open, and valley-fill wells do not have them. We will recommend rehabilitation or pump work instead.

My Montclair-area well lost capacity. What is wrong?

Usually a clogged screen or gravel pack, mineral encrustation, a dropped water table, or a worn pump. All of these have proven fixes that are better suited to valley wells than hydrofracturing.

What does well rehabilitation cost compared to hydrofracturing?

Rehabilitation varies with the method and well depth; we quote it after a $125 diagnostic. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000 but only applies to bedrock wells, which Montclair generally does not have.

Where does hydrofracturing actually work near here?

In the foothills and mountains on granite and fractured rock — areas like Mt. Baldy, Lytle Creek, and Wrightwood. Those wells respond well; Montclair's valley-floor wells do not.

How do I know if I am even on a private well?

Many Montclair properties are served by the Monte Vista Water District. If you have no well head, pressure tank, or pump on your property, you are on municipal water and well services do not apply.

When should I just drill a new well?

When an old gravel-packed well has reached the end of its life and rehabilitation no longer restores capacity. A new, properly screened well runs $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey and we will tell you honestly when that is the smarter spend.
