Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your North Clairemont well has elevated chloride:

From Aquifer to Tree: The Anatomy of a Grove Well System

An avocado grove is unforgiving of interruptions. The trees want a steady, even supply — a mature tree can drink 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and a planted acre often needs 3 to 5 acre-feet across a year. Delivering that reliably takes a system, not just a hole in the ground. Here is how the pieces fit together.

The Well Itself

In San Diego County's backcountry, water moves through fractures in decomposed granite and crystalline bedrock rather than through a deep sand-and-gravel aquifer. Wells commonly reach 200 to 600 feet, and two neighboring parcels can yield very differently depending on how the fractures line up. That is why we start with a yield test: there is no point sizing a pump for water the well cannot supply.

The Pump and Controls

A submersible pump is matched to the well's depth, its recovery rate, and the grove's peak demand. Get it wrong and you either starve the trees in summer or overdraw the well and pull in sand. Modern constant-pressure controls keep delivery steady as demand changes through an irrigation cycle.

Storage and Re-Pressurization

Because many fractured-rock wells produce a modest, steady flow rather than a large burst, growers store that flow in a tank and then boost it back up to irrigation pressure on demand. A well that yields only a few gallons a minute can still water a sizable block when it fills a tank around the clock.

The Irrigation Network

Drip lines and micro-sprinklers put water at the root zone slowly and evenly. Avocados have shallow roots and hate both drought and waterlogging, so uniform pressure and clean emitters across the whole block are what separate a healthy grove from a patchy one.

Water Quality and the Avocado's Famous Sensitivity to Salt

If there is one crop that punishes poor water quality, it is the avocado. Among commercial tree crops it has one of the very lowest salt thresholds — research places it around 0.57 dS/m of salinity, with yield dropping sharply above that point. Chloride is the specific culprit. Irrigation water carrying more than roughly 100 ppm of chloride starts to harm the trees, and studies have recorded yield losses of about 10 to 12 percent for every additional 35 ppm. The tell-tale damage is scorched leaf tips and margins that spread as a dry season wears on.

For that reason, an avocado grower needs a water analysis that goes well beyond a basic potability test. The numbers that matter most include:

  • Chloride. The single most important figure for avocado. High chloride accumulates in the root zone and burns foliage; managing it means lower-chloride sources, blending, and deliberate leaching.
  • Overall salinity (EC) and sodium. Total dissolved salts and a high sodium-adsorption ratio both stress the tree and degrade soil structure over time.
  • Hardness. Calcium and magnesium scale up pumps and plug the tiny orifices in drip and micro-sprinkler heads.
  • Iron and manganese. Beyond staining, they encourage slime that clogs emitters and creates uneven watering.

We collect and interpret these samples against avocado-specific limits, then recommend a practical plan — blending, leaching, filtration, or treatment — rather than a generic softener that misses the point.

Why Groves Lose Water: The Usual Suspects

Most grove-water calls we answer in the backcountry come down to a handful of recurring causes.

  1. Salt accumulation in dry years. When the rains fail and growers irrigate more heavily, chloride and salts build up in the soil. Leaf burn follows, and so does a hit to fruit size and yield.
  2. Falling well yield. Fractures silt up and static water levels drop during drought, so a well that once kept ahead now empties the storage tank by afternoon.
  3. Worn pumps and weak pressure. Scale and sand shorten pump life. The symptoms are low pressure at the end of a line, rapid on-off cycling, and a tank that never fully recovers.
  4. Clogged emitters. Hardness scale, iron, manganese, and bacterial slime block drip and micro-sprinkler outlets, leaving some trees parched and others soaked.
  5. Outgrown systems. A grove that has expanded beyond what the original well, pump, and tank were sized for simply cannot keep up.

A Quick Self-Check Before the Truck Arrives

Spending a few minutes gathering observations helps us solve the problem on the first visit.

  • Read the pressure. Note the cut-in and cut-out pressures and whether they hold through a full cycle.
  • Inspect the foliage. Progressive tip and margin burn points to chloride or salinity, not just summer heat.
  • Walk the block. Dry trees at the far end of a line usually mean lost pressure, a tired pump, or plugged emitters.
  • Watch the tank. Short-cycling or a tank that will not refill overnight points to a tank, switch, or yield problem.
  • Note any changes. New staining, odor, or sediment in the water is worth flagging.

When It Is Time to Bring in a Licensed Contractor

A grower can keep an eye on gauges and leaves, but the moment the storage tank stops keeping up, pressure falls off across the block, leaf burn persists, or you are planning to plant more trees on an existing well, it is time to call a professional. Pulling a pump, rehabilitating a well, and any work inside the casing require the right rig and a C-57 license. Avocados are a long-term investment; a season lost to a failed system during fruit sizing is an expensive way to learn that lesson.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really avocado groves near North Clairemont?

The named neighborhood is urban and on city water, but it sits inside San Diego County, one of the country's top avocado regions. The commercial groves are inland — Escondido, Valley Center, Pauma Valley, Bonsall, Ramona, and Fallbrook — and that is where we do most of our grove-well work. If you have avocado trees on a private well anywhere in the county, we can help.

How do I know if my well water is hurting my avocado trees?

Scorched leaf tips and margins are the classic warning sign of chloride or salinity stress, which begins above roughly 100 ppm chloride. A proper water test measures chloride, salinity, sodium, hardness, iron, and manganese against avocado-specific thresholds so you know exactly where you stand.

My well only produces a few gallons a minute. Can it water a grove?

Often yes. By storing the well's steady output in a tank and boosting it back to pressure on demand, a low-flow well can irrigate a much larger block than its instantaneous rate suggests. Where output is genuinely marginal, hydrofracturing can sometimes open up additional fractures and improve yield.

How deep will a well need to be out in the county?

In the granite backcountry, domestic and agricultural wells typically run 200 to 600 feet. Because the water travels through fractures, depth and yield vary widely from one parcel to the next, so we test each well rather than assume.

What is the cheapest fix versus the most expensive?

A pressure switch is among the least costly repairs at $150 to $350, while a complete new well runs $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey. Most service calls fall in between — a pump replacement at $2,500 to $5,500 or water treatment at $1,500 to $3,500 are common. We quote before any work and credit the $125 diagnostic.

Do you offer emergency service if my grove suddenly loses water?

Yes. We provide same-day emergency response across San Diego County. Losing water during a heat wave can stress an avocado grove quickly, so call as soon as you notice a problem and we will prioritize getting you back online.
