Well Pump Control Box Guide

The Brain of Your Well System

SC

By SCWS Team

Published February 17, 2026 · 9 min read

That gray metal box near your pressure tank isn't just decoration. Your well pump control box contains the critical components that start your submersible pump motor properly—and when it fails, you're without water until it's fixed.

The good news: control box problems are often cheaper and faster to fix than pump problems. Understanding what's inside and how it works can help you diagnose issues and potentially save hundreds on unnecessary pump pulls.

Control Box Basics

  • Purpose: Houses starting components for 3-wire submersible motors
  • Location: Usually mounted near pressure tank or in pump house
  • Key components: Start capacitor, run capacitor (some), relay, overload protection
  • Not needed for: 2-wire motors, jet pumps
  • Cost to replace: $50-$200 for box; $125-$350 installed

What's Inside the Control Box

The control box contains components that the pump motor needs to start and run properly but that would be inaccessible if located in the well with the pump. By placing them above ground, troubleshooting and replacement are much easier.

Start Capacitor

Single-phase motors (what most residential submersible pumps use) need a "boost" to begin rotating. The start capacitor provides this boost by creating a phase shift in the electrical supply that generates starting torque. Without it, the motor receives power but just sits and hums—it can't start turning.

Start capacitors are electrolytic (aluminum or electrolytic style) with typical ratings of 150-400 microfarads. They're designed for brief duty—just seconds during startup. Running a start capacitor continuously would cause it to overheat and fail rapidly.

Run Capacitor

Some control boxes include a run capacitor in addition to the start capacitor. Run capacitors are oil-filled and designed for continuous operation, improving motor efficiency and power factor during normal running. Not all systems have them—their presence depends on motor design and manufacturer.

Relay

The relay is an electromechanical switch that disconnects the start capacitor once the motor reaches running speed. When you first energize the pump, both start and run circuits are active. As the motor accelerates and current drops, the relay senses this change and opens the start circuit, leaving only the run circuit active.

Two main relay types exist: potential (voltage-sensing) relays are most common in well pumps, while current (amperage-sensing) relays are used in some applications. The relay must match your motor's characteristics—using the wrong relay causes starting problems or relay burnout.

Overload Protection

Thermal overload protection prevents motor damage from overheating. If the motor draws excessive current (from a stuck pump, low voltage, or other problem), the overload heats up and opens the circuit. After cooling, it resets automatically. This protection saves motors from burnout but also trips during legitimate problems—repeated tripping indicates an underlying issue.

⚠️ Warning: Never bypass overload protection. If the overload keeps tripping, there's a reason. Find and fix the cause rather than removing the protection—you'll end up with a burned-out motor in the well, costing $2,000+ to replace.

2-Wire vs 3-Wire: Do You Need a Control Box?

Whether your system has a control box depends on your motor type:

3-Wire Motors (Need Control Box)

Three-wire motors have three conductors plus ground running down the well. The starting components live in the above-ground control box. Advantages: easier troubleshooting and component replacement without pulling the pump. Disadvantages: more connections that can fail, additional box to maintain.

2-Wire Motors (No Control Box Needed)

Two-wire motors have two conductors plus ground. All starting components are sealed inside the motor housing at the bottom of the well. Advantages: fewer above-ground components, fewer connections. Disadvantages: when starting components fail, you must pull the pump to access them—a major service call.

Neither type is inherently better—it's a trade-off between accessibility and simplicity. Many installers prefer 3-wire for deeper wells where pulling the pump is expensive, and 2-wire for shallow installations where access is easier.

Troubleshooting Control Box Problems

Pump Hums But Won't Start

This is the classic symptom of a failed start capacitor. The motor is getting power but can't generate starting torque. Turn off power immediately—a motor that hums but doesn't turn draws locked-rotor current and can burn out in minutes.

To test the capacitor: turn off power, open the control box, locate the capacitor (cylindrical component, usually black or brown). Look for visible damage—bulging, leaking, burn marks. If it looks okay, test with a multimeter capable of measuring capacitance. The reading should be within 10% of the microfarad rating printed on the capacitor.

Pump Trips Repeatedly

If the overload trips during normal operation, possible causes include:

  • Low voltage: Check voltage at the control box while pump runs—should be within 10% of rated voltage
  • Failing capacitor: Weak capacitors cause high starting current
  • Pump problem: Stuck pump, worn bearings, or seized impeller draw excess current
  • Incorrect overload: Overload rating must match motor amp draw
  • Ambient heat: Hot location can cause nuisance tripping

Intermittent Starting

If the pump sometimes starts and sometimes doesn't, suspect relay problems. Relays have contacts that can become pitted or corroded, causing inconsistent connections. Listen for clicking when the pump tries to start—no click may indicate relay failure. Testing relays requires a multimeter and knowledge of the specific relay type.

Burn Marks or Smell

Any visible burning, melting, or burnt smell in the control box indicates a serious problem. Possible causes: loose wire connections generating heat, component failure, or water intrusion. Don't operate the pump until the damage is assessed and repaired.

Replacing Control Box Components

Capacitor Replacement

Replacing a capacitor is often a DIY job for those comfortable with electrical work:

  1. Turn off power at the circuit breaker
  2. Verify power is off with a voltage tester
  3. Discharge the capacitor by shorting terminals with an insulated screwdriver
  4. Note or photograph wire connections
  5. Disconnect wires from old capacitor
  6. Install new capacitor with matching ratings (microfarads and voltage)
  7. Reconnect wires exactly as they were
  8. Close box and restore power

Capacitors cost $15-$50 at electrical supply houses. Match the microfarad rating exactly—using the wrong value causes starting problems or capacitor failure.

Whole Control Box Replacement

Sometimes replacing the entire control box is more practical than replacing individual components—especially if multiple parts have failed or the box is old and corroded. Replacement boxes cost $50-$200 depending on HP rating. Make sure to match:

  • Motor horsepower
  • Voltage (typically 230V for residential)
  • Motor type (PSC, capacitor-start, etc.)

Franklin Electric is the most common brand, with standardized QD (Quick Disconnect) and CRC control box styles. When ordering, specify your motor HP and the existing box model if visible.

Advanced Controllers and Protection

Beyond basic control boxes, several advanced options protect your pump investment:

Pump Protection Devices

Cycle sensors count pump starts and shut down the system if cycling becomes excessive—protecting against running with a failed pressure tank or major leak.

Undervoltage protection prevents the pump from starting if voltage is too low, which would cause high current and overheating.

Low-water cutoffs (like Pumptec or Symcom) detect when the pump is drawing air instead of water and shut it down before damage occurs—essential for wells with limited yield.

Constant Pressure Controllers

Variable frequency drives (VFDs) like the Franklin SubDrive replace standard control boxes with sophisticated electronics that adjust motor speed for constant pressure operation. These provide all the basic control functions plus soft-start, variable speed, and pressure regulation. Learn more in our constant pressure guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a well pump control box do?

The control box contains starting components for 3-wire submersible pump motors. Inside are start capacitors (provide the burst of power to start motor rotation), run capacitors (on some models, for running efficiency), a relay (switches from start to run mode), and overload protection (thermal switch to prevent burnout). When the pressure switch calls for water, the control box energizes the motor properly.

Do all well pumps need a control box?

No. Only 3-wire submersible motors require an external control box. 2-wire motors have starting components built into the motor housing down in the well. Jet pumps have their starting components integrated. If you have a 3-wire submersible (three conductors plus ground going down the well), you need a control box.

How do I know if my control box capacitor is bad?

Signs of a failed capacitor include: pump hums but won't start, pump starts slowly or weakly, visible bulging or leaking on the capacitor, burn marks inside the control box, or motor trips overload repeatedly. Test with a multimeter set to capacitance—compare reading to the microfarad rating printed on the capacitor. More than 10% deviation means replacement is needed.

Can I replace a control box capacitor myself?

Yes, if you're comfortable with electrical work. Turn off power at the breaker and verify with a tester. Capacitors store charge—discharge by shorting terminals with an insulated screwdriver. Note wire positions before disconnecting. Match the new capacitor's microfarad and voltage ratings exactly. Capacitors cost $15-50 and take about 15 minutes to replace.

How much does a new control box cost?

Control boxes range from $50-$200 depending on motor horsepower and features. A standard Franklin QD-style box for a 1 HP motor costs about $80-$120. Labor for professional replacement adds $75-$150. Total installed: $125-$350. Often more economical than replacing individual components if multiple parts have failed.
