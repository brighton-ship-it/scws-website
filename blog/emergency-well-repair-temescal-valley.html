Most Common Well Emergencies in Temescal Valley

Wells along the valley floor and the foothill margins each face their own pressures, from hot, dry summers that push pumps to run long hours to groundwater levels that fluctuate within the Upper Santa Ana Valley basin system. These are the failures our crews handle most frequently in the area:

  • Sudden total loss of water, usually the first and most alarming symptom of a system fault.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout, often after extended summer run times.
  • Pressure tank failure, which can cause rapid short-cycling or leave you with no steady pressure.
  • A well that has gone dry or dropped below the pump as the water table shifts.
  • Electrical or control box failure, sometimes following a heat-driven power event.
  • Burst pipes or leaks at the wellhead, tank, or supply line.
  • No water after a power outage, when the system does not come back online on its own.

Temescal Valley has grown rapidly, but its character remains a mix of new development and older rural acreage, and that mix shapes the well work we do. A property on the valley margins may have a well that predates the surrounding subdivisions by decades, with aging equipment that finally gives out on the hottest day of the year. We take the time to assess the actual condition of your well and equipment rather than simply swapping parts, so the repair fits your system and your situation.

What to Check First

A few safe checks can occasionally restore water and will always help us diagnose faster. Run through them in order, and never open the well cap or touch wet electrical parts:

  1. Check the breaker for your well pump. If tripped, switch it completely off, then back on, one time.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge. A zero reading suggests a pump or supply problem; normal pressure with no flow indicates something else.
  3. Tap your pressure tank. A hollow top and solid bottom is normal; a tank that feels heavy throughout is probably waterlogged.
  4. Listen at the pressure switch for a click or hum when you call for water. Silence may point to a failed switch.
  5. Confirm the grid is up in your area, since an outage can stop your pump even when other circuits appear fine.

What to Do While You Wait

Once you have called us, conserve the water still in your pressure tank and any storage. Skip the laundry, the dishwasher, and unnecessary flushing. If you suspect a leak, shut the supply valve at the tank to preserve your reserve and prevent flooding near the wellhead. Filling a few containers for drinking and basic needs is a smart move while our truck is en route. In a valley that bakes through the summer, having a little water set aside keeps everyone comfortable until we arrive.

Because the valley draws so much of its rural water from the Temescal subbasin within the larger Upper Santa Ana Valley groundwater system, water levels are not static. A dry year can lower the table enough to affect older or shallower wells, and we factor that local hydrology into every diagnosis we make.
