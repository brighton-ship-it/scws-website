Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your La Jolla well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How Well Water and Irrigation Work on the Soledad Slopes

La Jolla's mixed geology, sandstone bluffs, rocky shoreline, and the steep escarpment of Mount Soledad along the Rose Canyon Fault, creates dramatic elevation changes across many properties. That terrain is the central irrigation challenge: delivering even, efficient water from the lower garden to the upper terraces of a hillside estate. The realistic water questions in La Jolla are about irrigation performance and any existing wells, not drilling new agricultural wells on coastal lots. On sloping ground, consistent coverage depends on careful pressure regulation, properly zoned drip and micro-spray, and filtration to keep emitters clear.

Where an estate or older property has a dedicated irrigation pump or a legacy well, a typical setup pairs a pump with a pressure tank and, on larger landscapes, a storage tank. Reliability comes down to a healthy pressure switch, a correctly charged tank, and clean filtration, and on tall hillside lots a booster or constant-pressure system is often essential so the highest zones receive the same water as the lowest. The marine climate is gentle on trees, but salt-laden coastal air and the chloride sensitivity of avocados make water quality worth attention wherever a private source is used.

Common Water and Irrigation Scenarios in La Jolla

Across three decades serving San Diego County, the issues we see most often on coastal, hillside properties include:

  • Uneven hillside coverage. Steep Soledad-slope lots leave upper terraces dry without proper pressure regulation or a booster.
  • Pressure-tank and switch wear. Failing tanks and switches cause short-cycling that destroys pumps.
  • Clogged drip systems. Sediment and scale block emitters, stressing estate trees and gardens.
  • Chloride-sensitive trees. Backyard avocados can show salt stress when irrigation water carries elevated chloride.
  • Legacy wells. Older estate parcels sometimes hold forgotten wells needing rehabilitation or proper sealing.

What to Check Before You Call

If your irrigation is weak or your plants look stressed, a few quick checks help:

  1. Confirm power and the timer. Check the breaker, any pump disconnect, and your irrigation controller's program.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Note whether pressure builds normally, never builds, or builds and crashes.
  3. Listen to the pump. Constant running without pressure, or rapid clicking, points to a tank or switch issue.
  4. Inspect filters and emitters. Clogged filters or blocked drip emitters are a common, quick fix.
  5. Walk the slopes for leaks. Look for wet spots, broken heads, or blown emitters on the terraces.
  6. Watch for salt stress. Leaf-tip burn on avocados may signal a water-quality issue worth testing.

Coastal Water Quality and Sensitive Estate Plantings

La Jolla's marine setting is a gift to gardeners, but it comes with one wrinkle worth understanding: salt. Ocean-influenced air, and in some cases the water sources used for irrigation, can push chloride and total dissolved solids high enough to stress the very plants estate owners prize most, including avocados, camellias, Japanese maples, and other salt-sensitive ornamentals. Because these landscapes represent a significant investment, it pays to test irrigation water and manage salts proactively rather than waiting for leaf burn and dieback to appear. The remedy is the same toolkit professional growers use: periodic deep leaching irrigations to flush salts below the root zone, blending sources when one is saltier, and treatment only where a water test shows it is warranted. We evaluate water quality for the parameters that matter to sensitive plantings and recommend the least-cost approach that actually protects your trees, so you are not over-engineering a solution your water does not require.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I grow avocados in La Jolla?

La Jolla is a built-out coastal neighborhood, not commercial avocado country, but its mild marine microclimate is excellent for backyard avocado and citrus. With consistent, low-chloride irrigation, those trees do very well here. We help keep your watering reliable.

How do I get even water on a steep Mount Soledad lot?

Tall hillside lots need strong pressure regulation and usually a booster or constant-pressure system so the upper terraces get the same water as the lower ones. Properly zoned drip and micro-spray with filtration completes the picture. We can design or repair it.

Why are my avocado leaves browning at the tips?

That is often chloride or salt stress from irrigation water. Testing, periodic leaching, and sometimes treatment or blending resolves it. We can test your water and advise.

I found an old well on my estate. What should I do?

It should be assessed and either rehabilitated for irrigation or properly decommissioned to San Diego County standards for safety. We handle both and pull the required permits.
