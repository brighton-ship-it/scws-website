Well Pump Won't Start in Big Bear Lake?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Big Bear Lake homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

No Water in Big Bear Lake? Start Here

Big Bear Lake is a resort city ringed by the San Bernardino National Forest in San Bernardino County, where homes from the Village to Boulder Bay and Moonridge rely on private wells fed by the lake basin's fractured bedrock and decomposed-granite soils. A pump that refuses to start is one of the most disruptive things that can happen to a mountain household — and because so many properties here are second homes or short-term rentals, the failure is often discovered after the system has sat cold and idle. The good news is that the cause is usually a single, identifiable component.

With more than three decades serving the mountain communities, Southern California Well Service has seen how altitude, snow load, and the area's hard water shape these breakdowns. Below is a practical, plain-English look at what makes a well pump go quiet up here.

The Anatomy of Your Pressure System

It helps to picture water delivery as a chain. If any link breaks, the tap runs dry:

  • The 240-volt power feed energizes the pump. Big Bear Lake's grid takes a beating in winter, and post-outage surges are a leading cause of dead pumps.
  • The pressure switch reads tank pressure and switches the pump on and off. Corroded contacts and a chilled diaphragm are common in unheated pump enclosures.
  • The control box and capacitor (on three-wire submersibles) provide the jolt the motor needs to start. A failed capacitor produces the classic hum-without-spin.
  • The bladder pressure tank buffers demand. When its bladder fails and it waterlogs, the pump short-cycles and can trip its overload.
  • The motor and downhole wiring sit deep in the casing and can fail from age, scale, or insulation breakdown.
  • The well's water level rises and falls with snowmelt; a dry winter can drop it toward the pump intake and trigger thermal cutouts.

Why Big Bear Lake Pumps Quit

Three local realities drive most of our service calls. First, freeze events: a vacation home with an unheated well house can lose its pressure switch or split a pipe overnight while no one is watching. Second, power instability: when SCE restores service after a storm, the inrush can pop a capacitor or weld the switch contacts shut. Third, water hardness: the calcium and iron typical of this basin slowly coat impellers and pit switch points, so equipment that performed for years finally gives out — often right when a full house of guests arrives.

Properties on the higher slopes near Moonridge and along the south shore can also see seasonal water-level swings that stress a pump set too shallow for a dry year.

Safe DIY Checks

  1. Look at the breaker. Find the double-pole well breaker and reset it a single time if tripped. An immediate re-trip means a fault — leave it off.
  2. Listen for the switch. With a faucet open, a soft click at the pressure switch shows it is calling the pump. Silence suggests the switch or its power has failed.
  3. Check the gauge. Record the PSI. Zero with no response, or a needle that jumps around, is useful diagnostic information.
  4. Hunt for freeze clues. Frost on piping or a cracked fitting in the pump house is a strong tell during cold months.
  5. Test other 240V appliances. If they misbehave too, the issue may be your main panel rather than the well.

Safety first: Do not open the control box or touch pressure-switch and well terminals with power on. The 240-volt circuit and submersible motor can be fatal.

Tips That Save You a Service Call

Because vacancy and cold cause so many Big Bear Lake pump failures, a little prevention goes a long way. Insulate or heat your pump house, drain or freeze-protect the system before leaving a cabin for the winter, and have the pressure tank's air charge checked annually so it never runs waterlogged. If your water is noticeably hard, adding filtration when you next service the pump dramatically slows the scale that kills switches and impellers. These small steps are far cheaper than an emergency pull during a holiday weekend.

How We Diagnose a No-Start in the Field

When our technician arrives at a Big Bear Lake property, the work follows a logical sequence rather than guesswork. We first confirm voltage is reaching the system at the breaker and the switch, which immediately separates an electrical-supply problem from a mechanical one. Next we test the pressure switch and, on three-wire systems, the control box and capacitor, since these inexpensive parts cause a large share of failures. If power and controls check out, attention turns downhole to the motor, wiring splices, and check valve. Finally we verify the pressure tank's air charge and the well's water level. This step-by-step method means we rarely replace parts that don't need replacing — important to homeowners who have been burned by shotgun repairs.

Telling us what you observed speeds this up. A pump that is dead silent, one that runs without building pressure, and one that cycles on and off rapidly each point to different culprits, and that single detail often determines what we bring up the mountain.

The Cost of Waiting

It is tempting to ignore early warning signs — a tap that sputters, water that takes longer to return, or a pump that runs more than it used to. In Big Bear Lake's climate, those small symptoms tend to escalate into a complete no-start at the worst possible moment, often during freezing weather or a fully booked rental weekend. Addressing a tired pressure switch or a weak capacitor early is inexpensive; replacing a burned-out motor that failed because it was fighting a waterlogged tank is not. Catching problems while they are still minor is the single best way to protect both your water supply and your wallet.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Big Bear Lake cabin sat empty all winter — why won't the pump start now?

Idle, unheated systems frequently suffer frozen pressure switches or split pipes. The pump may be fine, but the freeze prevents it from getting the signal or holding pressure. We can thaw, repair, and freeze-protect the system.

What does it mean when the pump just hums?

The motor has power but can't turn — typically a bad start capacitor, a seized motor, or a jammed check valve. It calls for diagnosis rather than repeated reset attempts.

How deep are Big Bear Lake wells?

Depths vary with the fractured granite, with many in the roughly 200–500 foot range. Depth influences pump selection and sensitivity to dry-year water-level drops.

Should I keep flipping the breaker?

No. One reset is fine; repeated tripping signals a genuine electrical fault and risks fire or motor damage. Let a professional find the cause.
