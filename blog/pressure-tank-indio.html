How Your Pressure Tank Works

Your pressure tank is essentially a shock absorber for your well system. It stores pressurized water so your pump doesn't have to kick on every time someone turns a faucet, flushes a toilet, or runs the sprinklers. Inside the tank, a rubber bladder separates an air charge from the water supply. When the pump fills the tank, the air compresses. When you use water, that compressed air pushes it through your pipes.

The pressure switch controls the whole cycle. Most Indio homes run a 40/60 PSI setting (the pump turns on at 40 PSI and off at 60 PSI), though 30/50 PSI systems are also common. The gap between those numbers determines your drawdown — how much water the tank delivers between pump cycles. Get the sizing right, and your pump cycles just a few times per hour. Get it wrong, and you're burning through pump motors.

When the bladder ruptures or the air charge slowly leaks through the rubber, the tank fills with water and loses its air cushion — it's "waterlogged." Without that cushion, every water draw triggers the pump. A pump designed to cycle maybe 300 times a day might suddenly cycle 3,000 times. In Indio's summer heat, that pump motor overheats fast. We've seen short-cycling destroy $2,500 submersible pumps in as little as two weeks during July and August.

Warning Signs of Pressure Tank Failure

Most pressure tank failures develop gradually. Knowing what to look for can save you thousands in pump replacement costs:

🔴 Rapid Pump Cycling (Short Cycling)

The pump clicks on and off every few seconds when water is running. You can often hear it from inside the house — a rhythmic clicking or humming from the pump house or wellhead. This is the most urgent sign. In Indio's heat, a short-cycling submersible pump overheats rapidly in the warm groundwater, potentially failing within days.

🟡 Pulsing or Surging Pressure

Water pressure surges when the pump activates then quickly drops. Showers feel like they pulse between strong and weak. Sprinklers alternate between reaching full distance and barely covering half. This usually means the air charge is depleted but the bladder may still be intact — catching it now can save the tank.

🟡 Waterlogged Tank

Knock on the tank from top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow at the top (where the air sits) and solid at the bottom (where the water is). If the entire tank sounds solid and feels heavy when you push on it, there's no air cushion left. The bladder has failed or the air has leaked out completely.

🟠 Rust, Corrosion, or Leaks

Visible rust on the tank shell, corroded fittings, or water weeping from connections. In Indio, UV radiation and extreme heat cycling degrade paint and protective coatings much faster than in coastal areas. Once the coating fails, corrosion follows quickly on steel tanks.

🟠 Air Blasts from Faucets

Sputtering air mixed with water from your faucets means the bladder has ruptured, allowing air and water to mix freely inside the tank. You may also see rust-colored water — without the bladder separating them, water contacts the steel tank interior and causes internal corrosion.

🔵 Unusually High Electric Bills

A short-cycling pump draws far more electricity than one cycling normally. If your power bill jumps without an obvious explanation, your well pump may be running constantly due to a waterlogged pressure tank. We've seen monthly increases of $50-100+ from short cycling alone.

Indio Well Conditions & Challenges

Indio's desert environment creates specific challenges for pressure tanks that homeowners should understand:

  • Extreme heat degradation: With summer highs regularly exceeding 115°F and ground surface temperatures above 150°F, rubber bladders inside pressure tanks degrade faster than anywhere in coastal Southern California. A tank that would last 15 years in San Diego might last 8-10 in Indio if it's in an unshaded outdoor installation. We always recommend shaded or enclosed installations and check bladder condition at every service visit.
  • High TDS and hard water: Indio's groundwater from the Coachella Valley aquifer typically has elevated total dissolved solids, hardness, and sometimes arsenic or fluoride. Mineral deposits accumulate on pressure switch sensing ports, brass fittings, and check valve seats. These deposits can cause the pressure switch to read incorrectly — your system may not reach full pressure, or the pump may not shut off. We clean and descale these components during every service call.
  • Sand production: Some Indio wells — particularly older ones or those where the water table has dropped — produce fine sand with the water. Sand abrades bladders from the inside, clogs pressure switch ports, and settles in the tank bottom reducing effective capacity. We recommend spin-down sediment filters upstream of the pressure tank for any well producing sand.
  • Seasonal demand swings: Water use in Indio can triple or quadruple between winter and summer due to landscape irrigation, pool top-offs, and evaporative cooling. A pressure tank sized for winter demand may be dramatically undersized for summer peaks, causing excessive pump cycling exactly when heat makes cycling most dangerous.
  • Subsidence concerns: Parts of the eastern Coachella Valley have experienced land subsidence from groundwater extraction. While this primarily affects wells and casings, it can also stress surface plumbing connections to pressure tanks, causing leaks at fittings that weren't present at installation.
  • Ant and pest infiltration: Desert ants are notorious for nesting inside pressure switch housings, attracted to the electrical contacts. A single ant colony can short out a pressure switch, causing the pump to run continuously or not at all. We install sealed switch covers and apply preventive treatments during installation.

Pressure Tank Sizing for Indio Properties

In the desert, we almost always recommend going one size up from the calculated minimum. The small additional cost provides significantly more pump protection during peak summer demand:

Property Type Recommended Size Notes
Small home (1-2 bed), minimal landscape 40-50 gallons Sized up from typical 30-gal minimum for desert cycling protection
Standard home (3-4 bed) 50-85 gallons Handles multiple bathrooms, moderate outdoor use, pool top-off
Large home with pool & landscape 85-120 gallons Summer irrigation and pool evaporation create high sustained demand
Agricultural / date farm / nursery 120+ gallons or dual-tank system Sustained irrigation requires maximum drawdown; dual tanks allow maintenance without downtime

Tank sizing also depends on your pump's flow rate and your well's production capacity. A 5 GPM pump paired with a 30-gallon tank provides only about 8 gallons of drawdown before the pump must cycle on — that's barely one toilet flush. We calculate proper drawdown based on your specific system to ensure adequate pump protection.

Pressure Tank Maintenance Tips for Indio Homeowners

Desert conditions require more frequent maintenance than moderate climates. Here's what we recommend for Indio well owners:

  1. Check air precharge every 6 months. Desert heat causes air to permeate through bladder walls 2-3x faster than coastal climates. Turn off the pump, drain the tank, and use a tire gauge on the Schrader valve. Precharge should be 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (38 PSI for a 40/60 switch). Add air with a bicycle pump — never use the well pump to pressurize the tank.
  2. Shade your equipment. This is the single most impactful thing you can do. A shaded or enclosed pressure tank in Indio can last 3-5 years longer than one baking in direct sun. Even a simple shade sail or corrugated metal roof over your pump house makes a significant difference.
  3. Listen for short cycling monthly. Run a faucet and count how often the pump kicks on. More than 6-8 cycles per hour during normal use means something is wrong. In summer, catching short cycling early could be the difference between a $150 air charge adjustment and a $2,500 pump replacement.
  4. Check for ants in the pressure switch. Desert ants love electrical contacts. Pop the pressure switch cover once a quarter and look for ant activity. If you see ants, clean them out and apply a perimeter ant treatment around the pump house base.
  5. Inspect fittings for mineral buildup. High TDS water deposits white or yellowish scale on brass fittings and threads. Check connections for drips or crusty deposits. Vinegar or a commercial descaler can remove minor buildup, but call us for heavily scaled connections.
  6. Flush the tank drain annually. Open the drain valve at the bottom of the tank and let it run for 30-60 seconds. This flushes out settled sediment and sand that reduces effective capacity and can damage the bladder.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size pressure tank do I need?

For most residential wells, we recommend a minimum 30-gallon pressure tank. Homes with higher water demand or multiple bathrooms benefit from 50-85 gallon tanks. Proper sizing reduces pump cycling and extends pump life.

How do I know if my pressure tank is failing?

Signs of a failing pressure tank include: pump short cycling (turning on and off frequently), waterlogged tank (heavy when you tap it), fluctuating water pressure, and the tank feeling uniformly heavy rather than having an air-filled top section.

How long do pressure tanks last?

Quality pressure tanks typically last 10-15 years. Bladder-type tanks (like Well-X-Trol) tend to last longer than diaphragm tanks. Annual pressure checks can extend tank life significantly.
