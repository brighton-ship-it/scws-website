What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Bernardino Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Well Service in San Bernardino

The City of San Bernardino sits on top of one of Southern California's most productive aquifers — the Bunker Hill Groundwater Basin, a deep wedge of sand and gravel washed down from the San Bernardino Mountains and trapped against bedrock barriers that keep the water from draining away toward the coast. For most of the valley floor, that means groundwater is held in loose, water-laid sediment rather than in cracked rock. It is an important distinction, because hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) only works on wells that produce from fractured bedrock. For a typical alluvial-valley well in the heart of San Bernardino, hydrofracturing is usually a weak fit, and an honest contractor will steer you toward the remedies that actually move the needle here.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation method for an existing drilled well. When a well draws water from bedrock, it does so through a network of natural fractures and joints. Over years of pumping these pathways gradually clog with fine sediment, mineral scale, and bacterial growth, so the well refills more slowly than it once did. To restore flow, a crew lowers an inflatable packer to seal off a section of the borehole, then injects clean water at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI to flush the fractures and, where the rock allows, extend them to tap nearby water-bearing seams. The packer is repositioned to treat additional intervals. Only water is used — no chemicals — and a residential treatment usually takes four to eight hours plus a flow test.

The mechanism is the whole story for San Bernardino. Fracturing needs fractures. A well screened in the sand and gravel of the Bunker Hill basin has no fracture network to open; injected water simply dissipates into the porous fill without any durable yield improvement. That is why we are upfront that most basin-floor wells are not good candidates.

Common San Bernardino Well Scenarios

  • Valley-floor wells in alluvium. Across most of the city, private and shared wells draw from the deep sand-and-gravel basin. Declining supply here is usually about regional water levels, pump condition, or screen fouling — not cloggable rock fractures — so hydrofracturing rarely applies.
  • Foothill and canyon-margin wells. Up against the base of the San Bernardino Mountains and along the harder bedrock margins, wells are more likely completed in or near fractured granite. These are the properties where hydrofracturing can be worth evaluating.
  • Water-quality complaints. The basin generally yields good water, but localized iron, manganese, hardness, or sediment problems are common and call for treatment, not fracturing.
  • Equipment-driven problems. Many "my well is failing" calls turn out to be a tired pump, a waterlogged tank, or a failed pressure switch — quick, affordable fixes unrelated to the formation.

What Usually Helps a San Bernardino Well

Because we will not sell a treatment that cannot perform in your geology, here is how we typically steer city-area owners:

  • Deepening or a new well. When a basin well has lost its column to a falling table or aged out structurally, deepening the borehole or installing a turnkey new well ($18,000-$42,000) is the durable answer.
  • Pump and pressure work. A replacement submersible pump ($2,500-$5,500), a new pressure tank ($600-$1,500), or a pressure switch ($150-$350) resolves a large share of supply complaints. A constant-pressure or booster system ($2,000-$4,500) steadies pressure under peak demand.
  • Water treatment. Sediment filtration runs $300-$900; iron, manganese, or softening systems run $1,500-$3,500. These solve quality issues that the valley's mineral-rich groundwater can produce.
  • Decommissioning. Old, unused, or collapsed wells should be properly destroyed ($1,500-$5,000) to protect the basin and meet county requirements.

Where Hydrofracturing Fits in San Bernardino County

It also helps to understand why the basin behaves the way it does. The same bedrock barriers that make Bunker Hill such a reliable aquifer for the valley floor are themselves fractured at the basin's edges, and groundwater migrates from those rock fractures into the porous fill. A well sited right at that contact can behave like a bedrock well even though the city a few miles away is pure alluvium. This is exactly why a generic assumption about San Bernardino wells is unreliable, and why we insist on reading each individual well log and running a downhole camera before recommending hydrofracturing rather than a basin-appropriate fix.

The technique earns its keep where the rock is right. Along the foothill belt and up into the mountain communities — places where wells are drilled into the solid granite of the San Bernardino Mountains and fed by fracture systems that collect snowmelt and rain — hydrofracturing can restore a declining hillside well or coax a better flow from a disappointing new one. If your property sits against the mountain front or in the higher country rather than on the open basin floor, the conversation is genuinely different, and fracturing may be a smart, money-saving alternative to drilling anew.

What to Check Before Spending

  1. Pull your well completion report. It records the drilled depth and materials. Sand and gravel to total depth means alluvium; hard rock at the production zone means a possible fracturing candidate.
  2. Read the symptom. Short-cycling and fast tank drain-down usually point to equipment; gradual multi-year decline points to the table or screen.
  3. Inspect the water. Staining and sediment indicate treatment needs, not formation problems.
  4. Map the timeline. Sudden loss is typically mechanical; slow decline is typically hydrogeologic.
  5. Ask around. If neighboring wells dropped together, the regional level is the likely cause.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing likely to help my San Bernardino well?

If your well produces from the sand-and-gravel Bunker Hill basin, probably not — there are no rock fractures to clean. If it is completed in fractured bedrock along the foothill or mountain margins, it may well help. A video inspection and your well log tell us which you have.

My supply is dropping. What is the most likely cause here?

On the valley floor, it is usually a falling water table, a fouled screen, or worn pump equipment. We diagnose the specific cause before recommending a fix.

Can hydrofracturing improve my water quality?

No. Hardness, iron, manganese, and sediment are treatment issues. Fracturing only addresses how much water the formation yields.

What does a typical fix cost if it is not fracturing?

It varies: a pressure switch is $150-$350, a tank $600-$1,500, a pump $2,500-$5,500, treatment $300-$3,500, and a new well $18,000-$42,000. We quote precisely after diagnosis.

How long does hydrofracturing take when it is appropriate?

The on-site work is four to eight hours, followed by a flow test usually within 24 to 48 hours to confirm the improvement.

Does it use chemicals?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure, with none of the additives associated with oil-and-gas fracking.
