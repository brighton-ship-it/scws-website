What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Bird Rock well.

What Turns Water Rusty

The usual suspects are four. Dissolved iron rises clear from the aquifer and oxidizes to orange in air. Iron bacteria consume iron and leave a reddish, slimy film. Corroding steel casing and galvanized pipes shed rust directly. And a failing water heater rusts only the hot water. Near the coast, higher mineral content and the potential for salt-driven corrosion can speed up casing and plumbing breakdown, making testing all the more useful.

Coastal Considerations

Because seawater influence and elevated chlorides can corrode steel faster, coastal-area wells sometimes benefit from corrosion-resistant casing materials and treatment tuned to both iron and hardness. We size systems to the full water profile, not just a single number.

Common Causes Near the Coast

  • Dissolved iron in local groundwater.
  • Iron bacteria recurring in the well.
  • Accelerated corrosion of steel casing and galvanized pipe from coastal minerals.
  • Hard-water mineral content compounding staining.
  • Water-heater rust on the hot side.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Is the rust on hot, cold, or both lines?
  2. Does it clear after running the tap, or persist?
  3. Is there slimy reddish buildup in tanks or on screens?
  4. Did the discoloration appear suddenly or build gradually?

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes well water rusty in the Bird Rock area?

Rusty or orange water in the Bird Rock and La Jolla area of San Diego comes from iron — dissolved iron from groundwater, iron bacteria, or rust from corroding steel casing, galvanized plumbing or a water heater. Coastal proximity can also bring elevated minerals and salts, so we test the water to identify exactly what is causing the discoloration.

Does living near the coast affect my well water quality?

It can. Coastal San Diego groundwater may carry more dissolved minerals and, in some areas, salt intrusion, which can accelerate corrosion of steel casing and plumbing and contribute to rust. A water test measures iron, hardness and chlorides so we can recommend the right combination of filtration and, if needed, corrosion-resistant components.

How much will it cost to treat rusty water?

Sediment filtration runs $300 to $900 and a dedicated iron or manganese filter or softener $1,500 to $3,500. Iron bacteria are handled with shock chlorination at a few hundred dollars. Corroded casing or plumbing is quoted after inspection. Our $125 diagnostic, credited toward the work, scopes the right approach.

Is rusty water dangerous?

Iron is an aesthetic contaminant, not a health hazard at typical well levels, but it tastes metallic and stains everything it touches. Because rusty water can sometimes accompany corrosion or bacterial issues, we recommend a test rather than assuming it is harmless.

Why does my water leave orange stains in sinks and tubs?

Those stains are oxidized iron settling out of the water. The lasting fix is to remove the iron before it reaches your fixtures with an oxidizing filter or softener sized to your iron level, rather than constantly cleaning the stains. We match the system to your test results.

Protecting Coastal-Area Water Quality Over Time

Coastal wells face a double challenge: iron from the groundwater plus the corrosion-accelerating effect of higher minerals and possible salt influence. That makes ongoing maintenance even more valuable here. We design treatment to handle iron and hardness together, and where corrosion is a factor we recommend corrosion-resistant materials so the cure does not become the next source of rust.

Maintenance keeps the whole system honest. Staying ahead of those changes is far cheaper than chasing recurring stains.

The Chemistry Behind Coastal Rust

Near the coast, rusty water is rarely a single-ingredient problem. Iron drives most of the orange staining, but manganese often accompanies it with darker marks and a bitter taste, and elevated hardness and chlorides can ride along thanks to the marine setting. Treating only the iron leaves the rest behind, so a complete water test is the foundation of any coastal fix.

Form matters as well as quantity. Dissolved ferrous iron is invisible until it oxidizes and turns the water orange, calling for aeration or an oxidizing filter; already-oxidized ferric iron is a particle that sediment filtration removes; bacterial iron is bound in slime and requires disinfection. Coastal corrosion can add freshly shed rust from steel casing and galvanized pipe on top of all that, so several sources frequently overlap in a single home.

Because of that complexity, coastal treatment is best designed against the full water profile. We measure iron, manganese, hardness and chlorides together and specify a system — sometimes multi-stage — that clears the water and resists the corrosion that coastal chemistry encourages.

Cleaning Up Stains and Protecting Fixtures

Stopping the rust is the priority, but the staining it has already left behind needs separate attention, especially on the higher-end fixtures common in coastal homes. Oxidized iron does not yield to ordinary abrasive cleaners; iron-specific, mildly acidic products dissolve it without scratching surfaces. On laundry, a rust-removing additive lifts the stain, while chlorine bleach can set it permanently, so the order of treatment matters.

Behind the walls, iron, manganese and the corrosion byproducts that coastal chemistry encourages may have accumulated in the pressure tank, water heater and supply lines. When we correct a rust problem we also flush and, where appropriate, sanitize these components, so freshly treated water is not being re-colored by legacy deposits. And because coastal corrosion tends to recur, we recommend periodic inspection of casing and plumbing to catch new rust sources before they reach your fixtures again.

The result is not just clear water at the tap today, but a system kept clean and protected against the accelerated corrosion that proximity to the ocean can bring.
