What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Fontana Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

How Hydrofracturing Increases Yield

Hydrofracturing forces clean water into your well under high pressure to clean and open the natural fractures in the surrounding formation. A technician isolates a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer and pumps water against it, often between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes out fine sediment and mineral scale and reconnects the well to fractures that had grown too tight to deliver water. In the fractured bedrock and consolidated material along the San Gabriel mountain front, opening those pathways is what raises the flow rate.

The process uses only water. There are no chemicals involved, which keeps it entirely distinct from oil-field fracking.

Common Reasons Fontana Wells Decline

On the rural fringes of Fontana, low yield typically comes from:

  • Sediment infiltration. Fine material from the alluvial fans works its way into the screen and the fractures feeding the well, gradually restricting flow.
  • Dropping water tables in dry years. The Inland Empire endures long droughts, and as the regional aquifer falls a marginal well begins running short during peak summer demand.
  • A new well with weak production. Wells drilled toward the foothills can miss the best fracture zones; hydrofracturing such a well is a standard remedy.
  • Mineral scaling. Hard local groundwater leaves deposits that slowly narrow the flow paths over the years.

Wells in the higher, harder ground around North Fontana commonly run 300 to 800 feet deep, deeper as the terrain rises toward the mountains.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Track when you run short. Shortages only during simultaneous heavy use indicate a yield and recovery problem — hydrofracturing's specialty.
  • Watch the pressure tank. Quick on-off cycling commonly means a waterlogged tank or a failing pressure switch, both cheap repairs.
  • Notice continuous pump running. If the pump runs and never builds pressure, it may be drawing the well down faster than it recovers.
  • Check for air at the taps. Sputtering faucets suggest the water level is reaching the pump intake during use.
  • Locate your well log. The original depth, static level, and tested yield let you measure how far production has slipped.

When to Call a Professional

When recovery slows, the well runs dry under normal use, or output drops well below the original log, schedule a professional assessment. Hydrofracturing relies on specialized high-pressure equipment and an experienced operator, so it is not a homeowner project. A licensed contractor runs a pre-treatment flow test, inspects the casing, and confirms your Fontana-area well is a good candidate before any pressurized work starts.

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Fontana

A hydrofracturing treatment generally costs $3,000 to $8,000, depending on depth and the number of zones. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward the job if you proceed. A new turnkey well in San Bernardino County typically runs $18,000 to $42,000 with drilling, casing, pump, and surface equipment included.

If the trouble turns out to be equipment, repairs are far less: a pump runs $2,500 to $5,500, a pressure tank $600 to $1,500, and a pressure switch $150 to $350. Because hydrofracturing costs a fraction of a new well and often restores 2 to 5 times the prior flow, most Fontana owners try it first.

Protecting Your Well After Treatment

After a hydrofrac restores yield on a Fontana-area well, a bit of upkeep makes it last. Schedule a flow test every couple of years so any gradual decline shows up early rather than during peak Inland Empire summer demand. Because foothill and alluvial wells can take on fine sediment over time, ask whether a sediment screen or periodic servicing is worthwhile for your setup. Keep your before-and-after flow records together; they give the next technician an immediate read on the well's history and keep future visits efficient. Catching a problem in spring is always cheaper and less stressful than scrambling for water in the heat of a Fontana July.
