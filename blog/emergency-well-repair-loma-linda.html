Don't Wait Until Complete Failure

Many well emergencies could be prevented with attention to warning signs. If you notice any of these symptoms, call for service before you lose water completely:

  • Sputtering or air in the water lines
  • Decreased water pressure throughout the house
  • Pump running constantly or cycling on and off rapidly
  • Unusual noises from the well or pressure tank
  • Sudden increases in electric bills
  • Sandy or sediment-filled water
  • Water that looks, smells, or tastes different than normal

Addressing these warning signs promptly can prevent inconvenient emergencies and often costs less than emergency repairs.

What Counts as a Well Emergency in Loma Linda

Not every well problem is urgent, but these situations usually are, and they are the calls we prioritize for Loma Linda and the surrounding San Bernardino County communities:

  • Total loss of water — you turn on a tap and nothing comes out. This is the most common emergency and usually points to a burned-out submersible pump, a tripped breaker or control box, or a failed pressure switch.
  • Sudden pressure collapse — water that trickles instead of flows, often from a waterlogged pressure tank or a pump that has lost prime.
  • A pump that will not shut off — constant running signals a leak, a stuck pressure switch, or a tank that has lost its air charge, and it can burn the motor out within hours.
  • Electrical failure after a heat wave or monsoon storm — summer surges and lightning off the mountains routinely take out control boxes and capacitors.
  • Air spitting from the faucets with sandy water — a possible sign the water level has dropped to the pump intake during heavy summer draw.

What You Can Safely Check Before We Arrive

While you wait, a few safe checks can speed things up or occasionally solve the problem outright:

  1. Look at the breaker or fuse dedicated to the well pump. If it has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop — that points to a shorted pump or wiring, and repeated resets can be dangerous.
  2. Find the pressure switch (usually a small gray box near the pressure tank) and listen for it clicking. Silence often means a switch or electrical fault.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. A hollow ring near the top and a dull thud near the bottom is normal; a tank that sounds full of water top to bottom is waterlogged.
  4. Note anything unusual — recent power outages, a burning smell at the control box, or new noises — and tell the technician. It shortens the diagnosis.

Please do not pull wiring, open the control box, or attempt to lift the pump yourself. Well electrical circuits carry enough voltage to be lethal, and a dropped pump can turn a repair into a full recovery job.

Why Summer Is Peak Season for Loma Linda Well Emergencies

The stretch from June through September is when our phones ring most for Loma Linda. Inland Empire heat regularly pushes past 100 degrees, and a well that coasted through spring is suddenly asked to deliver far more water — irrigation for landscaping and gardens, extra showers, cooling misters, and topping off pools. A submersible pump that runs a few hours a day in April may run most of the day in August, and that sustained duty cycle is exactly what pushes an aging motor over the edge. Add the summer's power surges and brownouts, and control boxes and start capacitors fail right alongside the pumps.

Foothill properties toward San Timoteo Canyon face a second summer risk: drawdown. As neighboring wells all pull hard at once, the static water level in the aquifer can dip, and a pump set only slightly above that level starts drawing air. The first symptom is usually sputtering taps and cloudy or sandy water, followed by the pump losing prime entirely. If you notice these signs during a heat wave, call before the pump runs itself dry — a pump that cavitates on air overheats quickly and can be destroyed in a single afternoon.

What a Loma Linda Emergency Service Visit Actually Includes

Homeowners are often unsure what they're paying for on an emergency call, so here's exactly what our technician does once on site. First comes a safety check of the electrical supply — verifying voltage at the disconnect, inspecting the control box for burnt contacts or a swollen capacitor, and confirming the breaker isn't feeding a dead short. Next we test the pump itself: we read the amperage it draws under load, which tells us instantly whether the motor is healthy, dragging, or seized. We inspect the pressure tank and switch, checking the tank's air charge and watching how the switch cuts in and out.

From those readings we can usually name the failure with confidence rather than replacing parts by trial and error. If it's a switch, capacitor, or control box, we typically fix it on the spot from the parts on the truck. If the pump has to come out of the ground, we set up to pull it safely, replace it with a properly sized unit, and re-establish the electrical and plumbing connections. Before we leave, we cycle the system through several pressure cycles, confirm flow at the fixtures, and make sure the tank holds pressure. That final testing is what separates a lasting repair from a callback.

When to Call a Pro and When It Can Wait

Some situations are genuine emergencies and some are not, and being honest about the difference saves you money. Call immediately if you have no water at all, if your breaker trips every time you reset it, if you smell burning at the control box, or if water is spraying from a broken line or fitting. These are urgent and, left overnight, tend to get worse and more expensive. On the other hand, a slow decline in pressure over weeks, an occasional flicker of sediment, or a pump that cycles a touch more than it used to are real issues worth addressing — but they can usually be handled on a scheduled appointment at the lower non-emergency rate. When you call, describe the symptoms plainly and we'll tell you honestly which category you're in.
