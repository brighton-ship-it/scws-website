How a Mountain Well Pressure System Works

A Crestline well builds pressure with four components. The pump lifts water from the well, typically a submersible unit in fractured granite. The pressure tank stores water under a cushion of compressed air so the pump cycles less often. The pressure switch turns the pump on at the cut-in setting (often 40 psi) and off at cut-out (often 60 psi). The plumbing, screens, and any sediment filtration deliver water to your fixtures. On Crestline's steep grades, elevation between the well, the tank, and the house matters, since roughly every 2.3 feet of lift costs 1 psi. A healthy home targets a steady 40 to 60 psi, and that is the first thing we measure when we arrive.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in Crestline Wells

Fractured-rock geology, freezing winters, and hillside lots produce a recognizable set of culprits:

  • Low-yield bedrock wells: Water sits in narrow granite fractures, so yield can be limited and pressure drops when multiple fixtures run together.
  • Elevation losses: Homes above their wells, plus long or undersized supply lines along the slopes, naturally reduce delivered pressure.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank: A failed bladder floods the tank, causing short-cycling and unstable pressure.
  • Freeze damage: Crestline's cold winters crack exposed pipe, tanks, and well-house plumbing, bleeding pressure once thawed.
  • Sediment and grit: Rock flour from fractured granite clogs screens, intakes, and filters.
  • Aging pump or switch: Older pumps lose lift and worn switch contacts leave the system stuck at the low end of its pressure band.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe checks help narrow things down:

  1. One fixture or the whole house? A single weak faucet usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge. Clean it before suspecting the well.
  2. Read the gauge. Watch the pressure gauge near the tank during use. Note it if it never reaches 40 psi or bounces rapidly.
  3. Tap the tank. Hollow on top and solid below is normal; solid all the way up suggests waterlogging.
  4. Listen to the pump. Rapid cycling points to the tank or switch; constant running without pressure points to the pump, a leak, or a low-yield well drawing down.
  5. Inspect for freeze damage. After cold snaps, check exposed pipe and the well house for cracks and drips.

Leave the 240-volt wiring, well cap, and pressurized parts to a professional, particularly on icy, sloped mountain sites.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Crestline well pressure drop when the family is all home?

Limited fractured-rock yield means running several fixtures at once can outpace how fast the well recovers. A constant-pressure system, a larger tank, or hydrofracturing to improve yield typically fixes it.

Does my hillside lot affect water pressure?

Yes. Lifting water uphill to a home above its well costs roughly 1 psi per 2.3 feet of rise. A properly sized booster or constant-pressure pump restores strong delivery.

Can Crestline winters cause low pressure?

They can. Freezing temperatures crack pipe, tanks, and well-house plumbing, which bleeds off pressure. We repair freeze damage and insulate vulnerable lines to prevent recurrence.

Do I need a permit for well work in San Bernardino County?

Pump and tank swaps usually do not, but new wells, deepening, and hydrofracturing do. As a licensed C-57 contractor we handle the permits.

Could hydrofracturing improve my low-yield well?

Frequently yes. Hydrofracturing applies controlled water pressure to open existing fractures and connect new water-bearing seams, often boosting yield and pressure in Crestline's fractured-granite wells.

Maintenance Tips for Crestline Well Owners

Crestline's cold winters and fractured-rock wells make routine care worthwhile. A few seasonal habits keep your pressure strong between professional visits:

  • Insulate against freezing by wrapping exposed pipe, the tank, and well-house plumbing before winter sets in.
  • Get an annual checkup so a technician can verify pump output, tank air charge, and switch operation.
  • Clean or replace filters on schedule to handle grit from the granite aquifer.
  • Stagger heavy use to give a modest-yield well time to recharge.
  • Track pressure trends and report falling numbers or short-cycling early, before they become bigger repairs.
