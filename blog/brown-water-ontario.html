Common Causes of Brown Well Water

  • Sediment Disturbance: Recent pump work, earthquakes, or construction
  • Iron/Manganese: Dissolved minerals oxidizing when exposed to air
  • Tannins: Organic matter from decaying vegetation
  • Surface Water Intrusion: Contamination from runoff entering well
  • Failing Well Screen: Sand and sediment entering well
  • Clay/Silt: Fine particles from the aquifer

When Brown Water is an Emergency

  • Sudden change after no recent work on well
  • Foul odor (possible contamination)
  • Recent flooding or heavy rains
  • Sand or grit in the water
  • Neighbors reporting similar issues

Brown Well Water in Ontario? A Local Guide to Causes, Testing, and Lasting Fixes

Ontario sits near the heart of the Chino Groundwater Basin, the broad alluvial reservoir that stretches across southwestern San Bernardino County beneath Ontario, Chino, Montclair, Upland, and Rancho Cucamonga. That basin is fed by hundreds of feet of Pleistocene alluvium washing down out of the San Gabriel Mountains, and it is exactly that mix of sand, gravel, silt, and iron-bearing sediment that makes brown or rusty water such a common complaint on private wells in this part of the Inland Empire. If you have turned on a tap in your Ontario home and watched it run the color of weak tea, cola, or muddy orange, you are not imagining a problem, and you are not alone. The good news is that discolored well water almost always has a specific, identifiable cause, and most causes are very fixable once you know which one you are dealing with.

This guide walks through why Ontario wells turn brown, how to narrow down the cause yourself before anyone comes out, when discoloration is a true emergency, and what professional repairs and treatment realistically cost. If at any point you would rather just have it handled, our licensed C-57 crew has been servicing wells across San Bernardino County for more than 30 years and can usually get someone out the same day.

Why Ontario Well Water Turns Brown

Brown water is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Five underlying causes account for the overwhelming majority of what we see on Ontario-area wells, and each one leaves its own signature.

Dissolved iron and manganese

This is the single most common cause across the Chino Basin. The alluvial sediments that supply local wells are naturally rich in iron and, to a lesser degree, manganese. Underground, in the oxygen-starved aquifer, these metals stay invisibly dissolved in the water. The moment that water hits the air in your pressure tank, your toilet bowl, or your glass, the iron oxidizes into rust-colored particles and the manganese into a blackish-brown haze. The tell-tale sign: water that pours out clear but turns brown or yellow after it sits for a few minutes, plus orange staining on fixtures, laundry, and concrete.

A corroded steel casing or galvanized plumbing

Many older Ontario-area wells and homes were built with steel casing and galvanized supply lines. After decades in service, that steel rusts from the inside out. When it does, you get rusty flakes and a metallic taste, often worst on the first draw of the morning after water has sat overnight in the pipes. If the brown clears up after you run the tap for a minute or two, corroded plumbing or casing is a prime suspect.

Silt and sediment from heavy pumping or storms

The center of the Chino Basin carries fine clay and silt within the alluvium. A pump that is set too low, a worn well screen, or a sudden heavy demand can pull those fines up into your water, especially after the winter storms that recharge the basin. This kind of brown water is usually cloudy and gritty, and it settles into a fine layer at the bottom of a glass rather than staying suspended.

Tannins from organic matter

Less common in the well-flushed central basin but still possible near recharge zones and the Prado Basin to the south, tannins are dissolved organics from decaying vegetation. Tannin water looks like tea, stays evenly tinted no matter how long it sits, and does not leave gritty sediment.

Iron bacteria

These harmless-but-nuisance bacteria feed on iron and leave behind a slimy, rust-colored buildup. If you find reddish, gelatinous slime inside your toilet tank or a slick coating on fixtures, and the water sometimes smells swampy or like rotten eggs, iron bacteria are likely at work.

How to Narrow Down the Cause Yourself

Before you call anyone, spend five minutes running a few simple tests. What you observe tells a professional a great deal and often shortens the visit.

  1. Clear-then-brown vs. brown-from-the-tap: Fill a clear glass. If it starts clear and browns as it sits, you are looking at dissolved iron or manganese. If it is brown or cloudy the instant it comes out, suspect stirred-up silt or a failing screen.
  2. Hot side vs. cold side: If only the hot water is brown, the problem is very likely inside your water heater, not your well. Flushing or replacing the heater's anode rod may be all you need.
  3. First-draw vs. continuous: If the first draw of the day is rusty and then clears, the culprit is corroding pipes or casing. If the water stays discolored no matter how long you run it, the source is the well or aquifer itself.
  4. Does it settle? Let a glass sit for an hour. Grit at the bottom points to sediment. An even color with no settling points to tannins. A cloud that clears from the bottom up points to dissolved gases and minerals.
  5. Check for slime: Lift the lid on your toilet tank. Rust-colored slime means iron bacteria.

The one step no home test replaces is a proper water test. A lab or field analysis for iron, manganese, pH, hardness, and bacteria removes the guesswork and tells us exactly what treatment your Ontario water needs. We fold this into our diagnostic visit.

When Brown Water Is an Emergency

Most discoloration is a nuisance you can address on a normal schedule. Treat it as urgent, though, and call us right away if any of the following is true:

  • The water suddenly turned brown with no recent work on the well and it is getting worse by the hour.
  • There is a foul or sewage odor, which can signal surface-water intrusion or contamination.
  • You have had recent flooding or heavy storm runoff near the wellhead.
  • You are seeing sand or grit that could damage your pump and fixtures.
  • Neighbors on nearby wells are reporting the same sudden change, which can point to a basin-wide event.

Until you know the water is safe, use bottled water for drinking and cooking.

Solutions and Realistic Costs for Ontario Wells

The right fix depends entirely on the cause, which is why diagnosis comes first. Here is how the common repairs map to the common causes, with realistic San Bernardino County price ranges.

  • Oxidizing / greensand or air-injection iron filter - the workhorse for the dissolved iron and manganese that dominate Chino Basin water. These systems oxidize the metals and filter them out, delivering clear water throughout the house.
  • Sediment / spin-down filtration ($300-$900) - for silt, clay, and sand, a whole-house sediment filter or spin-down separator protects your plumbing and appliances.
  • Water softener ($1,500-$3,500) - where iron is low and hardness is the bigger issue, a softener can remove modest iron along with the hardness the Chino Basin is known for.
  • Chlorination / shock treatment - the standard fix for iron bacteria, sometimes paired with ongoing treatment to keep them from returning.
  • Casing and pump inspection or replacement - if a corroded casing or worn screen is letting sediment in, a downhole video inspection ($150-$400) shows us the condition, and repairs range from targeted work to a submersible pump replacement ($2,500-$5,500) when the pump is set too low or failing.
  • Reverse osmosis ($300-$1,200) or UV ($800-$1,800) - for polishing drinking water at the tap or disinfecting where bacteria are a concern.

Other common line items on Ontario wells include a pressure switch ($150-$350), a pressure tank ($600-$1,500), and a control box or capacitor ($400-$900). Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any work you approve, so you are never paying twice to find out what is wrong.

When to Call a Professional

Swapping a whole-house sediment cartridge is a reasonable DIY job. Diagnosing why brown water started, cracking open a well casing, or sizing an iron filter for your specific water chemistry is not. Call a licensed contractor when the discoloration is persistent, when you see sand or slime, when the first-draw test points to casing or pump problems, or when a store-bought filter clogs within days. Guessing at treatment is the single most expensive mistake we see. Homeowners buy a softener when they needed an iron filter, or a sediment filter when the real problem was a pump pulling silt from the bottom of the well, and end up paying for the correct fix anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is brown well water in Ontario safe to drink?

Iron and manganese discoloration is usually an aesthetic problem, not a health one, but brown water accompanied by odor, recent flooding, or a sudden change can indicate contamination. Until a water test confirms it is safe, use bottled water for drinking and cooking and have the well checked.

Why is only my hot water brown?

When just the hot side is discolored, the problem is almost always inside your water heater rather than your well. Sediment settles and the anode rod corrodes over time. Flushing the tank or replacing the anode often resolves it.

My water is clear at first, then turns brown in the glass. Why?

That is the classic signature of dissolved iron or manganese. It stays invisible underground with no oxygen, then oxidizes into rust-colored particles once it meets the air. An oxidizing or air-injection iron filter is the standard fix.

Will a water softener fix brown Ontario water?

A softener can handle low levels of iron along with the hardness common in Chino Basin water, but it is not designed to remove moderate or heavy iron. If iron is the main issue, a dedicated iron filter will outperform a softener and last longer.

How much does it cost to fix brown well water?

It depends on the cause. Sediment filtration runs $300-$900, a softener $1,500-$3,500, UV $800-$1,800, and a reverse osmosis drinking system $300-$1,200. If the well itself needs work, a video inspection is $150-$400 and pump replacement is $2,500-$5,500. We start with a $125 diagnostic, credited toward the repair.

The brown water started after a storm. Is that normal?

Winter storms recharge the Chino Basin and can stir fine silt and clay into the water, especially if your pump sits low or your screen is worn. It often clears on its own, but if it persists or comes with odor, have the well inspected to rule out surface-water intrusion.
