Does Well Depth Affect Water Quality?

Updated February 2026 | By Southern California Well Service

Quick Answer: Depth affects quality differently: deeper wells are better protected from surface contamination (bacteria, nitrates) but may have more minerals or naturally occurring contaminants. There's no universal "better depth"—it depends on local geology. Always test regardless of depth.

General Patterns

What Typically Improves with Depth

  • Protection from surface contamination
  • Lower bacteria risk
  • Less nitrate contamination
  • More stable water level
  • Less influence from weather/seasons

What May Get Worse with Depth

  • Higher mineral content (hardness)
  • More dissolved solids (TDS)
  • Possible arsenic, fluoride, radon
  • Sulfur (hydrogen sulfide)
  • Higher water temperature

The Key Point

Depth alone doesn't guarantee quality—local geology determines what's in the water at any depth. Testing is essential.

Shallow Wells (Under 50 feet)

Characteristics

  • Draw from water table aquifer
  • More connected to surface
  • Water level fluctuates with weather
  • Faster recharge but faster contamination

Potential Issues

  • More vulnerable to bacteria
  • Nitrates from fertilizer/septic
  • Pesticides and surface chemicals
  • Turbidity after heavy rain
  • May go dry in drought

Advantages

  • Often softer water
  • Lower dissolved minerals
  • Cheaper to drill and maintain
  • Easier pump access

Protection Measures

  • Proper well sealing critical
  • Setback from contamination sources
  • Test more frequently
  • Consider deeper well if problems persist

Deep Wells (150+ feet)

Characteristics

  • Draw from confined aquifers
  • Protected by overlying rock/clay layers
  • More stable water level
  • Water may be older (decades to centuries)

Advantages

  • Better protected from surface contamination
  • More consistent quality
  • Less seasonal variation
  • Often more reliable supply

Potential Issues

  • Higher mineral content
  • Very hard water common
  • Naturally occurring arsenic or fluoride
  • Sulfur odor (hydrogen sulfide)
  • Radon in some areas
  • Expensive to drill

Not Automatic Quality

Deep wells aren't immune to problems—they just have different ones. Always test.

Local Factors Matter Most

Geology Determines Quality

  • Granite = possibly arsenic, radon
  • Limestone = hard water
  • Sandstone = variable quality
  • Alluvial = often good quality
  • Shale = may have sulfur

Regional Patterns

San Diego County examples:

  • Mountain areas: granite, fractured rock, variable quality
  • Valleys: alluvial, often good quality at moderate depth
  • Desert: deep for water, may be mineralized

Neighboring Wells

  • Ask neighbors about their water quality
  • Similar depths may have similar water
  • But not guaranteed—aquifers vary

Test Before Drilling

  • Review existing well logs in area
  • Consult hydrogeologist for important projects
  • Know what to expect at different depths

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a deeper well have better water quality?

Not always. Better protection from surface contamination, but may have more minerals or naturally occurring contaminants. Depends on local geology.

What's considered a shallow vs deep well?

Generally: under 50 feet is shallow, 50-150 is medium, 150+ is deep. Standards vary by region.

Should I drill deeper to improve water quality?

Depends on the problem. Bacteria/nitrates might improve. Hardness might get worse. Consult a professional about your specific situation.

Why does my deep well have sulfur smell?

Hydrogen sulfide can occur naturally in deep aquifers with certain geology. Treatable but not unusual for deep wells.

Is groundwater cleaner than surface water?

Generally yes—filtered through soil and rock. But groundwater can have natural contaminants that surface water doesn't.
