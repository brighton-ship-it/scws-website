What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Crest Park Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Crest Park

Crest Park is a small mountain community along Highway 18 in the San Bernardino Mountains, sitting at roughly 5,400 feet just south of Lake Arrowhead between Rim Forest and the Crestline area. Surrounded by the San Bernardino National Forest, it is a community of cabins and full-time homes set among the pines, and like its neighbors along the Rim of the World, its wells reach water through fractured granite rather than soft soil. When a Crest Park well starts producing less than it used to, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is frequently the most cost-effective way to restore flow without drilling a new well on a steep, wooded lot.

Granite Wells Along the Rim

There is almost no soft sediment up here. Wells are drilled into the hard crystalline granite that forms the crest of the range, and water collects in the cracks, joints, and fracture zones within that rock. A Crest Park well does not pull from a wide underground reservoir; it depends on the particular set of water-bearing seams it happens to intersect. That is exactly the situation hydrofracturing is built for. The treatment reopens and extends those seams, and because the surrounding rock is solid granite, the new openings stay propped apart long after the work is done. A well in loose, sandy ground would not hold a fracture the same way.

Why Crest Park Wells Run Short

The seams that feed a granite well gradually narrow over the years as fine mineral scale, silt, and iron and manganese deposits build up along the fracture faces. Most homeowners notice it first in the dry stretch from late summer into fall, when a well that once ran the house comfortably now sputters by evening, recovers slowly overnight, and leaves the pump short-cycling. The water is usually still in the mountain; the pathways into the borehole have simply tightened. Snowmelt and winter rain recharge the rock slowly through fractures, so in dry years the seams feeding a marginal well are the first to draw down, and a well that was adequate when drilled in a wetter decade can fall behind.

How the Treatment Works

Hydrofracturing uses only clean water. We lower inflatable packers into the cased and open-hole sections of your well to seal off a target zone, then pump water into that isolated interval at pressures that commonly run from about 1,000 up to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes accumulated sediment out of existing fractures and gently widens and extends the natural cracks, reconnecting your borehole to seams that had silted shut or were never fully open. Because Crest Park bedrock is genuine fractured granite, it responds to this treatment far better than soft ground would. The entire process is closed-loop and chemical-free, so nothing is added to your aquifer.

Signs Your Crest Park Well Could Benefit

  • Steady decline: production has dropped noticeably over a few years, even after a normal-snow winter.
  • Afternoon slowdowns: pressure and volume fade under heavy use and need hours to bounce back.
  • A weak new borehole: a recently drilled hole hit granite but came in with disappointing flow.
  • Frequent pump cycling: the pump kicks on and off because the well cannot keep up.
  • Seasonal rationing: you stretch water through September and October every year.

What to Rule Out First

Before assuming the well is failing, check the simpler causes. A low or waterlogged pressure tank, or a worn pressure switch, can imitate a yield loss and is far cheaper to fix. A single weak fixture usually means a clogged aerator or a plumbing restriction. At Crest Park's elevation, frozen lines and tanks in winter can mimic a dry well until they thaw. And a pump drawing air or pushing sandy water may signal a dropping water level reaching the pump, or a worn pump, rather than the aquifer. When the well genuinely recovers more slowly than it used to, runs low only under sustained demand, and has declined gradually over seasons, that pattern points squarely at a tightening fracture network, which is what hydrofracturing addresses.

When to Call a Professional

If your well has clearly lost ground year over year, if a newer hole came in weak, or if you are deciding between a new well and saving the existing one, that is the moment to bring in a licensed contractor for a flow test and camera inspection. A new well in the San Bernardino high country is a five-figure gamble, so a proper diagnosis is well worth its modest cost. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward the work if you move forward.

The Step-by-Step Process

  1. Baseline flow test: we measure current yield and check static and pumping water levels for a real before-number.
  2. Downhole camera survey: a video inspection shows casing condition and the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Packer isolation: we seal the chosen interval so the pressure concentrates on that zone.
  4. High-pressure injection: clean water clears and extends the fractures, usually four to eight hours on site.
  5. Post-treatment flow test: we re-measure yield after the well settles 24 to 48 hours and provide a documented before-and-after comparison.

What Hydrofracturing Will and Will Not Do

Clear expectations make for satisfied customers. Hydrofracturing does not create new groundwater and it cannot undo a dry winter; it reconnects your borehole to the water already moving through Crest Park's granite by clearing and widening the seams that feed it. On a true fractured-rock well that has slowly lost production, that is usually enough to restore a comfortable household supply. It will not rescue a well whose water level has genuinely dropped below the pump in a deep drought, where lowering the pump may be the better answer, and it is not the right tool for a well drawing from a pocket of loose decomposed-granite sand. Because nearly all Crest Park wells sit in solid fractured granite, most are good candidates, but we confirm that before recommending anything.

After Treatment: Water Quality and Winter Care

For the first hours to a day after treatment, expect cloudy water as the opened fractures flush silt and fine mineral material; it clears as the well develops, and we run the well to move that sediment through. Mountain granite can carry naturally elevated iron and manganese, and opening new seams may briefly shift the mineral mix until the well settles, so service any iron filter or softener afterward. Because Crest Park sits above 5,000 feet, protecting your wellhead, pressure tank, and exposed lines from freezing keeps your restored flow reliable through winter. A periodic flow check lets you catch any renewed decline early, when a simple re-treatment is far easier than an emergency in a January storm.

Cost Compared to a New Well in Crest Park

Drilling a new well in the San Bernardino high country is expensive and uncertain, given the depth needed to reach reliable granite seams and the challenge of getting a rig onto a steep forest lot. A turnkey new well generally runs from about $18,000 to $42,000. Hydrofracturing an existing well usually costs between $3,000 and $8,000, and it improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of the fractured-rock wells we treat, with typical gains of two to five times the starting flow. For most Crest Park homeowners, it is simply the smart first move before committing to a new borehole.

Why Rehabilitation Often Wins Here

Crest Park's location along the spine of the Rim makes new drilling especially awkward. Lots tend to be narrow and sloping, the pines crowd the building pads, and Highway 18 access means any rig has to climb the grade before it ever reaches your driveway. All of that adds cost and risk to drilling a new well. Hydrofracturing sidesteps most of it: the equipment is compact, the work happens at the existing wellhead, and a single day on site can restore a well that has served the property for decades. For homeowners deciding between a fresh borehole and saving the well they have, that practical reality usually tips the scale toward treatment first.
