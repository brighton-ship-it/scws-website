What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mount Helix Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Mount Helix

Mount Helix rises about 1,375 feet above the surrounding mesas of San Diego County's East County, a landmark granite dome crowned by its famous open-air amphitheater and cross, ringed by the custom homes and large lots of the Mount Helix, Fuerte, and Casa de Oro neighborhoods near La Mesa and El Cajon. Unlike the city neighborhoods down the hill, plenty of properties on and around the mountain sit on private wells — and the rock under your feet here is exactly the kind that makes hydrofracturing genuinely effective. If you own a well in Mount Helix, this is good news.

Mount Helix is prime hydrofracturing country. The mountain is a mass of granitic and metamorphic bedrock, part of the Peninsular Ranges batholith, shot through with natural fractures and joints. Wells here are typically drilled straight into that hard rock, and they draw their water from the network of cracks within it. When those fractures get clogged with fine mineral debris or were never well-connected to begin with, hydrofracturing can reopen and extend them — often delivering a dramatic jump in yield without the cost of a new well.

How Hydrofracturing Works on the Mountain

We start by lowering an inflatable packer into your well to seal off a specific section of the borehole. Clean water is then injected against that isolated zone at high pressure — typically 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. In Mount Helix's fractured granite, that pressure flushes out accumulated silt and mineral scale and widens the existing cracks, creating more and larger pathways for groundwater to flow into the well. The whole job usually takes four to eight hours and uses nothing but water — no chemicals. In hard-rock wells like these, a two- to fivefold improvement in flow is a realistic outcome, and we see success in roughly 70–80% of qualifying wells.

Why Mount Helix Wells Lose Yield

Even good granite wells slow down over the years. The usual culprits we find here are:

  • Mineral scaling in the fractures — iron, manganese, and calcium carbonate slowly plate out and choke the cracks that feed the well.
  • Poorly connected fracture networks — a well that was marginal from day one may only intersect a few productive seams; hydrofracturing can link it to more.
  • Drought-driven water-table decline — multi-year dry spells lower the saturated fracture zone, and a well that once produced plenty starts to struggle in late summer.
  • Aging pump and pressure equipment — sometimes the rock is fine and the real problem is a tired pump or a waterlogged tank.

Why Hydrofracturing Makes Sense Here

For a Mount Helix homeowner, the economics are compelling. A new well on the mountain can mean drilling deep into hard granite — a turnkey new well runs $18,000 to $42,000. Hydrofracturing an existing, properly constructed bedrock well typically costs $3,000 to $8,000 and can restore the yield you have lost. Because the geology here is so well-suited to the technique, it is almost always worth trying before committing to a new well. We measure your flow before and after so the improvement is documented, not guessed at.

What to Check Before You Call

A little information speeds up the diagnosis:

  • How deep is your well, and do you have the original driller's log showing the fractured zones it hit?
  • Has the yield dropped gradually over years, or suddenly? A sudden drop often points to the pump; a slow decline points to the formation.
  • Are you seeing rusty staining or scale, which suggests iron and manganese that may be clogging fractures (and may warrant treatment, $1,500–$3,500)?
  • Is the pressure tank holding charge and the pressure switch ($150–$350) cycling correctly?

When to Call a Professional

If your Mount Helix well has lost noticeable yield, runs low in late summer, or has you worried about going dry, a professional evaluation is the smart first move. Our $125 diagnostic (credited toward any work we perform) includes a flow test and, where useful, a downhole camera survey to map the casing and fracture zones. For a granite well like yours, that assessment usually points straight toward hydrofracturing as the cost-effective fix — and we will confirm it is a good candidate before recommending it.

Typical Mount Helix-area figures: hydrofracturing a qualifying bedrock well $3,000–$8,000; pressure switch $150–$350; pressure tank $600–$1,500; pump replacement $2,500–$5,500; sediment filtration $300–$900; iron or manganese treatment or a softener $1,500–$3,500; constant-pressure or booster system $2,000–$4,500; a turnkey new well $18,000–$42,000; and decommissioning an old well $1,500–$5,000.
