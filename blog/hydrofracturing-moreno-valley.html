What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Moreno Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Moreno Valley: When It Helps and When It Doesn't

Plenty of Moreno Valley well owners ask us about hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) when their water production slips. It is a fair question, and sometimes the answer is yes. But Moreno Valley's geology means the honest answer is often no — and a good contractor should tell you that before taking a deposit. This page lays out where hydrofracturing makes sense in this part of Riverside County, where it does not, and what the better fix usually is.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation method for bedrock wells. We seal off a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer, then inject clean water at high pressure — commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure pushes into the natural hairline fractures in solid rock, flushes out the silt and mineral scale clogging them, and widens the network so groundwater flows to your well more freely. It uses only water, no chemicals, and a typical job takes four to eight hours. The catch is in that one word: bedrock. You need solid, fractured rock for the technique to do anything.

Why Most Moreno Valley Wells Are Weak Candidates

Moreno Valley sits on a broad alluvial valley floor on the Perris Block. The aquifer that most local wells tap is made of layered sand, silt, and gravel — loose sediment, not rock. Many residential wells here are relatively shallow, often under 200 feet, with static water levels around 90 feet, because that productive sediment is close to the surface. High-pressure water cannot create lasting fractures in loose sand and gravel — there is nothing solid to crack. Pump water hard against unconsolidated material and it simply slumps back. So for a classic valley-floor Moreno Valley well, hydrofracturing is the wrong tool, and we will say so plainly.

Where Hydrofracturing Can Work Around Moreno Valley

The exception is the margins. Toward the Box Springs Mountains, the Reche Canyon corridor, and the rising ground at the valley's edges, wells are more likely to reach fractured granitic and crystalline bedrock. Those wells are good candidates, and they are where we see the two-to-five-times yield gains the technique is known for. If your property sits against the hills rather than out on the flats, it is worth checking your well log — you may be one of the wells that responds.

Better Options for a Valley-Floor Well

If your well is bottomed in alluvial sediment and the yield is falling, hydrofracturing is not your answer — but you still have good ones:

  • Deepen the existing well. Extending the borehole to a deeper, more productive layer can restore yield without the full cost of a new well.
  • Drill a new, properly screened well. A correctly sited and screened well in good alluvium can be a strong long-term producer. A new turnkey well runs $18,000–$42,000.
  • Fix the equipment, not the aquifer. A worn pump ($2,500–$5,500), a waterlogged pressure tank ($600–$1,500), or a failing pressure switch ($150–$350) can mimic a dying well at a fraction of the cost.
  • Address screen clogging. Sediment filtration ($300–$900) or iron and manganese treatment ($1,500–$3,500) can solve flow and quality problems caused by encrustation.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Pull your well completion report. It tells us depth and whether you are in sediment or rock — the single most important fact for candidacy.
  • Watch the symptoms. Air spitting at the faucet and slow recovery after heavy use point to the well or aquifer; steady weak pressure everywhere points to the pump or pressure system.
  • Inspect the pressure tank. If the pump short-cycles on and off, the tank or switch is a likely culprit and a cheap fix.
  • Note water quality. Sand, cloudiness, or rust staining hints at screen or scale issues rather than a fracturing problem.

Moreno Valley's Shallow Aquifer in Plain Terms

One reason hydrofracturing rarely fits Moreno Valley is that the water here is close to the surface and held in sediment. With many residential wells finished under 200 feet and static levels often near 90 feet, the typical Moreno Valley well is tapping a shallow alluvial layer rather than reaching down into rock. That is good news in one sense — drilling is cheaper and faster than in hard granite country — but it also means the technique that boosts bedrock wells has nothing to grip. Understanding which layer your well draws from is the single most useful thing you can learn before spending money, and your completion report spells it out.

Deepening vs. a New Well: How We Decide

When a valley-floor Moreno Valley well falls short, the choice usually comes down to deepening the existing borehole or drilling fresh. Deepening makes sense when the casing is sound, the site is good, and a more productive layer sits within reach below the current bottom — it reuses what you already have. A new well makes sense when the casing is compromised, the original siting was poor, or you want a larger-diameter, properly screened well built for the long haul. We weigh casing condition, the driller's log, your water demand, and the local aquifer behavior, then give you a recommendation with real numbers. A new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000; deepening is typically less, depending on depth and conditions.

Don't Overlook the Cheap Fixes

Before assuming the aquifer is the problem, it is worth ruling out the equipment, because those repairs are a fraction of any well work. In Moreno Valley we regularly find that a short-cycling pump traces to a waterlogged pressure tank or a worn pressure switch, that low pressure throughout a house is a tired pump rather than a dying well, and that gritty or rust-stained water points to screen encrustation that filtration or iron and manganese treatment can resolve. A $125 diagnostic that gets credited toward the work often saves a homeowner thousands by catching one of these before anyone talks about drilling.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing help a typical Moreno Valley well?

Often not, and we will be honest about it. Most Moreno Valley wells are finished in shallow alluvial sand and gravel on the valley floor, where there is no solid rock to fracture. Hydrofracturing works on wells that reach fractured bedrock — more common along the Box Springs, Reche Canyon, and foothill margins than in the heart of the valley.

If my well is in loose sediment, what should I do instead?

The usual options are deepening the existing borehole to a more productive zone, or drilling a properly sited and screened new well. We can also check whether the real problem is the pump, pressure tank, or a clogged screen, which are far cheaper to fix than the aquifer.

How deep are wells in Moreno Valley?

Many residential wells here are relatively shallow — commonly under 200 feet — because the alluvial aquifer is near the surface. Static water levels are often around 90 feet. Shallow alluvial wells are exactly the kind that do not respond well to fracturing.

How can I tell if my well reaches bedrock?

Your well completion report (well log) shows the materials drilled through and the total depth. If it notes granite, decomposed granite, or fractured rock near the bottom, you may be a candidate. Our camera survey confirms it.
