  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Spring Valley North well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

The Parts of Your Well System

A private well is a chain of components, and a problem in one link shows up as a symptom you notice at the tap. Knowing the chain helps you describe issues clearly.

  • Borehole and casing: the drilled hole and its liner. In the decomposed granite and hard bedrock common across eastern San Diego County, wells here commonly reach 150 to 500 feet.
  • Submersible pump and motor: the workhorse that lifts water from depth to the surface.
  • Pressure tank: holds a cushion of pressurized water so your pump is not slamming on and off all day.
  • Pressure switch and wiring: the small control that starts and stops the pump at set pressures.
  • Treatment and storage: filters, softeners, and tanks added when water quality or peak demand requires them.

Typical Problems We See in This Part of the County

Hard-Water Scaling

Eastern San Diego County groundwater tends to be mineral-rich. Over time, scale builds inside pumps, pipes, and fixtures, shortening equipment life and choking flow. Softening or scale treatment is a common fix.

Pressure That Comes and Goes

A pump that cycles rapidly, or pressure that drops the moment a second faucet opens, usually means a pressure tank that has lost its air charge. It is one of the most frequent and least expensive repairs we make.

Air Spitting From Faucets

Sputtering taps can mean the water level has dropped near the pump intake during a dry stretch, a cracked drop pipe, or a failing check valve. Each has a different fix, so diagnosis matters.

Iron Staining and Odor

Rusty stains on fixtures point to iron and manganese; a sulfur smell points to hydrogen sulfide. Both are treatable with the right media.

What to Check Before Calling Us

  1. Confirm power. Reset the well breaker once. A breaker that trips again immediately signals an electrical fault, not a nuisance trip.
  2. Read the gauge. Note the pressure at the tank. Zero or wildly swinging readings are useful clues.
  3. Check the tank charge. With the pump off and a faucet open to drain pressure, the air valve on top of a properly charged tank should read about 2 psi below your pump's cut-in pressure.
  4. Walk the line. Look for wet spots or pooling between the wellhead and the house that could indicate a buried leak.
  5. Note recent changes. New noise, a recent storm, or a gradual decline all point the diagnosis in different directions.

Leave anything involving the pump, the control box, or well wiring to a licensed crew. Those jobs combine high voltage with heavy, deep equipment.

How Often Should a Well Be Maintained?

Wells are easy to ignore until they fail, but a little routine attention prevents most emergency calls. We suggest an annual look at pressure-tank charge, pump cycling, and water quality, plus a water test every year or two for bacteria, nitrates, and the iron and hardness that are common in eastern San Diego County groundwater. Sediment filters should be changed on a schedule rather than waiting for pressure to drop. If your well is more than fifteen or twenty years old, it is worth having the pump and wiring inspected proactively, since a planned replacement is far cheaper and less stressful than a failure during a heat wave.

Owners with irrigation also benefit from checking emitters and lines each spring. Clogged drip emitters waste water and stress trees, and a quick walk-through before the hot months catches problems while they are small. We are glad to fold an irrigation check into a maintenance visit.

Low Well Yield and What Can Be Done

Some properties in this part of the county sit over fractured rock that simply produces water slowly. If your well struggles to keep up during summer, you are not necessarily facing a new well. A larger storage tank lets a slow well fill a buffer overnight for use during the day, and a properly sized constant-pressure system smooths delivery. Where a well has lost production over time, hydrofracturing can sometimes reopen fractures and restore flow at a fraction of the cost of drilling new. We assess each situation honestly and recommend the least expensive option that actually solves the problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Spring Valley North have private wells?

Yes, on many of its larger and more rural parcels. Closer-in suburban lots are usually on a water district, but properties toward Jamul, Dehesa, and the outskirts frequently rely on private wells.

Why is my water leaving rust stains?

That is almost always iron, sometimes with manganese, in your groundwater. It is cosmetic rather than dangerous in most cases and is readily handled with an iron-removal or filtration system.

How deep are wells around Spring Valley North?

It varies with the fractured granite, but many local wells fall in the 150 to 500 foot range depending on where a productive fracture is found.

My pump runs constantly. Is that bad?

Yes. Continuous running usually means a waterlogged tank, a leak, or a failing pump, and it dramatically shortens motor life. Have it checked promptly to avoid a larger bill.

Do you charge for a diagnosis?

Our diagnostic visit is $125, and we apply that amount to any repair we carry out, so the assessment effectively pays for itself.

Can you help with irrigation for citrus or backyard avocado trees?

Definitely. We size pumps, storage, and drip or micro-sprinkler systems for everything from a few backyard trees to several acres of landscape and small orchard.
