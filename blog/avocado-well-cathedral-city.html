How a Desert Well and Irrigation System Works

A well on the Coachella Valley floor is a complete system. A submersible pump set deep in the casing lifts water from the aquifer up to a pressure tank, and a pressure switch tells the pump when to cycle so your plumbing and irrigation see steady pressure. From the tank, water feeds the house and the irrigation manifold supplying drip lines, bubblers, and the deep-watering emitters that desert palms and citrus need. Because the desert demands a lot of water in summer, many properties pair the well with a storage tank and a booster system so the well can recharge and still deliver strong flow during early-morning and evening irrigation windows when evaporation is lowest.

Coachella Valley groundwater sits in a deep alluvial basin, and public-supply wells across the valley are commonly completed to depths between roughly 490 and 900 feet, with some wells drilled past 1,000 feet. Private wells vary, but desert wells generally run deeper than the fractured-rock wells of the coastal mountains, which is one more reason local experience matters when sizing a pump or planning a new well here.

Common Local Scenarios We Get Called For

Across Cathedral City and the valley floor, the well work we do tends to cluster around a few recognizable desert situations:

  • Pumps overworked by summer demand, running long hours in extreme heat and wearing out faster than they would in a milder climate.
  • Hard, mineral-rich water leaving heavy scale on fixtures, plugging drip emitters, and crusting on hardscape.
  • Pressure tank and switch failures that leave irrigation sputtering during the hottest part of the season.
  • Sediment or sand in the water from older or poorly screened wells in the alluvial basin.
  • Decommissioning of an abandoned well on a parcel being redeveloped, which Riverside County requires be done to code.

What You Can Check Yourself Before Calling

A few quick observations help us help you faster, and sometimes save you a service call entirely:

  1. Listen to the pump. Short, rapid on-off cycling almost always points to a pressure tank that has lost its air charge or a failing pressure switch.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Healthy residential systems usually hold 40 to 60 psi; a needle that never builds or swings wildly is a red flag.
  3. Check the breaker. Desert heat is hard on electrical components, and a tripped breaker or failed connection is a common, inexpensive culprit.
  4. Look at the water. Note any new color, smell, grit, or heavy scaling and when it started; that timeline narrows the diagnosis quickly.
  5. Walk your irrigation zones. If only some emitters are weak, the problem may be mineral scale or clogging rather than the well itself.

When to Call a Professional

Anything that involves pulling the pump from a deep desert well, opening the wellhead, handling 240-volt wiring, or interpreting a water-quality lab report is work for a licensed contractor. If you have lost water entirely in peak summer, see a sudden change in clarity, or the pump runs constantly without building pressure, call us right away rather than guessing. In desert heat, a well outage is not just an inconvenience; it can kill landscaping fast. A wrong move on a deep submersible can also drop expensive equipment down the casing. Why Desert Wells Need Local Expertise

Desert wells are a different animal from mountain or coastal wells, and treating them the same way is a costly mistake. Deeper aquifer wells need correctly sized pumps and motors to lift water efficiently, and an undersized or overworked pump in 110-degree heat fails early. Mineral content in valley groundwater is often high, so treatment and emitter selection matter for protecting both equipment and plantings. Because we work across Riverside County desert terrain regularly, we can recommend the right pump, the right storage and pressure strategy, and the right treatment the first time, which saves you money and downtime when the temperature is at its worst.

Desert Water Quality and Treatment

Coachella Valley groundwater is generally hard and mineral-rich, and depending on the well it can carry elevated total dissolved solids, sand, or trace contaminants worth testing for. Hardness leaves heavy scale on water heaters, fixtures, and irrigation emitters, and in the desert that scaling happens fast. A properly sized softener or filtration system protects your plumbing and keeps emitters flowing. We always start with a water test so the treatment matches what is actually in your water rather than guessing, which keeps you from paying for equipment you do not need and ensures your landscape gets clean, reliable water through the long irrigation season.

Seasonal Maintenance Beats Summer Breakdowns

In the desert, the cheapest repair is the one you prevent, because a no-water emergency in July can cost you an entire landscape in a matter of days. We recommend a simple pre-summer check of the pressure tank air charge and the pressure switch, an inspection of the wellhead and the heat-stressed electrical connections, and regular filter and softener service so scale does not choke your system at the worst possible time. On irrigation, walking the zones before the hot season catches plugged emitters and broken bubblers before they leave a palm or citrus tree dry. For properties that depend entirely on a single well, a periodic flow test confirms the well and pump can still meet peak summer demand before the heat arrives. A modest spring maintenance habit almost always costs far less than an emergency deep-well pump replacement in the middle of a heat wave, and it protects the desert plantings and shade trees that make a Coachella Valley property livable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you grow avocados in Cathedral City?

Not really. Cathedral City is a hot desert climate in the Coachella Valley, which is not suited to commercial avocado growing. Out here we focus on desert-appropriate landscaping, palms, and citrus, and on the reliable groundwater systems that support them.

How deep are wells in the Coachella Valley?

Valley wells are relatively deep. Public-supply wells are commonly completed between about 490 and 900 feet, and some go past 1,000 feet. Private wells vary, but desert wells generally run deeper than coastal or mountain wells.

Why does my desert well water leave so much scale?

Coachella Valley groundwater is typically hard and mineral-rich, so scaling happens quickly, especially in the heat. A softener or filtration system sized to your water test solves it and protects your equipment.

Why does my pump keep failing in summer?

Extreme desert heat and long summer run times are hard on pumps and motors. An undersized or aging pump fails faster here, so correct sizing and timely replacement are important.
