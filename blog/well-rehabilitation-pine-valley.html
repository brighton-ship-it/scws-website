Pine Valley sits at roughly 3,700 feet in the Cuyamaca-Laguna Mountains of eastern San Diego County, a forested pocket along Interstate 8 surrounded by the Cleveland National Forest. Out here, away from any municipal water main, a private well is not a convenience but a lifeline, and the geology underfoot makes that well harder to keep healthy than most homeowners expect. Wells in Pine Valley and the neighboring communities of Guatay, Descanso, Mount Laguna, and Alpine are typically drilled into fractured granite and decomposed granite, the crystalline bedrock that forms these mountains. Because water in this rock moves through narrow fracture networks rather than through a deep, sandy aquifer, a well's output depends heavily on how open and unobstructed those fractures stay over time. When mineral scale, iron bacteria, or fine granitic sediment begins to plug them, yield falls off, and a well that once kept up with the household starts to struggle. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years rehabilitating wells in exactly these conditions, and the good news is that a slowing mountain well can very often be restored without the expense of drilling a new one.

When Your Pine Valley Well Needs Rehabilitation

Wells decline gradually, so the warning signs are easy to dismiss until the day a shower runs dry. The fractured-granite formations around Pine Valley are particularly prone to slow fouling because the same hard, mineral-rich water that feeds the well also deposits scale on its screen and in the surrounding rock. If you notice any of the following, your well is telling you it needs attention:

  • Declining yield: The most common complaint. Your well simply produces less water than it used to, and the pump now runs far longer to refill the pressure tank or fill a stock trough.
  • Sand or sediment in the water: Grit at the faucet aerators, sediment in the toilet tank, or fine granitic sand collecting in fixtures often means decomposed-granite material is migrating into the well and the screen is no longer holding it back.
  • Cloudy or discolored water: Turbidity, a sudden metallic taste, or reddish-brown staining on fixtures and laundry frequently points to iron, manganese, or iron-bacteria activity inside the well.
  • A fouled or encrusted screen: When the intake screen becomes coated with mineral scale or bacterial slime, water cannot enter the well freely even though the aquifer still holds plenty of it.
  • A short-cycling pump: A pump that rapidly switches on and off is often fighting a well that can no longer supply water as fast as the pump wants to draw it, a classic symptom of reduced well capacity.
  • Rising power bills: As resistance builds inside a clogged well, the pump works harder and longer for the same amount of water, and that extra effort shows up on your electric bill before it shows up anywhere else.

Any one of these can have several causes, which is why diagnosis matters more than guesswork. Catching the problem early almost always means a cheaper, simpler fix.

What Causes the Problem and How We Fix It

Three culprits dominate in the Pine Valley area. Mineral scale forms when the hard water common to granitic terrain leaves calcium and carbonate deposits on the screen and in the rock fractures feeding the well. Iron and manganese, abundant in crystalline bedrock, both stain water and feed iron bacteria, which build a slimy biofilm that physically blocks the screen and clogs the formation around it. And because so much of the local soil is decomposed granite, fine sediment steadily works its way into the well over years of pumping. Sorting out which of these is at work, and in what combination, determines the right rehabilitation approach. Here is how we proceed.

Video Inspection

Every rehabilitation begins with a downhole camera survey. Before we treat anything, we lower a video camera into the well to document casing condition, screen integrity, scale buildup, sediment depth, and any cracks or corrosion. On a fractured-granite well, the camera also reveals where the producing fractures actually are, which guides the rest of the work. This step prevents the costly mistake of paying for a treatment that does not match the real problem, and it gives you a clear before-and-after record of the results.

Mechanical Cleaning

Once we know what we are dealing with, mechanical methods physically dislodge buildup from the screen and casing. Wire brushing scrubs encrustation off the casing walls, surging drives water back and forth through the screen to break loose deposits, and high-pressure jetting blasts scale and biofilm out of the screen slots and the immediate formation. Mechanical work is especially effective against the bacterial slime and softer scale that accumulate in mountain wells, and it loosens hardened material so that chemical treatment can finish the job.

Chemical Treatment

Acidizing is often the most cost-effective single step for a Pine Valley well. A measured acid solution is introduced and allowed to dwell, dissolving the calcium, iron, and manganese scale that mechanical cleaning alone cannot fully remove from the screen and the surrounding fractures. For iron-bacteria fouling, we may pair acid treatment with a disinfecting agent to kill the biofilm at its source. Because crystalline-rock wells depend on open fractures, dissolving the scale that has sealed those fractures frequently restores a substantial share of the original yield.

Well Redevelopment

After cleaning and treatment, redevelopment removes everything we have loosened along with the fine sediment that has accumulated over years of use. Air-lift pumping and additional surging pull the broken-down scale, dead bacteria, and decomposed-granite fines up and out of the well so they cannot resettle. Redevelopment also re-establishes good hydraulic connection between the well and the producing fractures, which is the entire point of the exercise on a bedrock well.

Screen Cleaning

The screen is where most of the trouble concentrates, because it is the gateway between the formation and the pump. Targeted screen cleaning combines focused jetting, brushing, and chemical contact directly across the screened interval to reopen plugged slots. A clean screen lets the aquifer deliver its full output to the pump again, and it protects the new investment by removing the fouling that would otherwise return quickly.

Rehabilitation vs. a New Well: Making the Right Call

Not every well is worth saving, and we will tell you honestly when it is not. Rehabilitation is the clear choice when the casing is structurally sound, the well is still drawing from productive fractures, and the loss of yield traces to scale, bacteria, or sediment rather than to a depleted aquifer or a collapsed casing. In those cases, rehab restores performance at a fraction of the cost of starting over. A new well becomes the better investment when the casing has failed, when corrosion has compromised the well's structure, or when the camera and pump testing show the surrounding rock simply no longer holds enough water. The video inspection and our experience with the region's geology let us give you a straight recommendation rather than a sales pitch, so you can put your money where it will actually do the most good.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes wells to lose production?

Wells lose production due to mineral scale buildup, biofouling from iron bacteria, sediment accumulation, or casing deterioration. In the fractured-granite formations around Pine Valley, scale and bacteria sealing off the producing fractures are especially common. Rehabilitation can often restore flow without drilling a new well.

How much does well rehabilitation cost in Pine Valley?

Most well rehabilitation projects run $1,500 to $6,000 depending on depth and how badly the well is fouled, while a standalone video inspection is $300 to $600. That is far less than a turnkey new well, which typically costs $18,000 to $42,000. Our $125 diagnostic visit is credited toward any rehab work you approve.

Why do wells in the Pine Valley area foul so often?

Pine Valley wells are drilled into hard, mineral-rich crystalline bedrock. The same water chemistry that makes the water hard also deposits calcium, iron, and manganese scale on the screen, while iron bacteria thrive on that iron and form a clogging biofilm. Decomposed granite adds fine sediment to the mix, so regular maintenance pays off in this terrain.

How often should a well be rehabilitated?

Most wells benefit from rehabilitation every 10 to 20 years, or sooner if you notice declining pressure, reduced flow, or changes in water quality. In the mineral-heavy mountains around Pine Valley, wells often need attention toward the shorter end of that range. Periodic video inspections help catch problems while they are still inexpensive to fix.

Will rehabilitation really restore my well's original flow?

It depends on the cause, but when the loss comes from scale, bacteria, or sediment rather than a falling water table, rehabilitation commonly recovers a large share of the original yield. We confirm the cause with a downhole camera first, so you know what to expect before any treatment begins.

Do I need to be home, and how long does the work take?

A diagnostic visit and video inspection usually take a few hours, and most residential rehabilitations are completed in one to two days depending on the methods required. We coordinate access with you and walk you through the camera footage and results before and after the work so you understand exactly what was done.
