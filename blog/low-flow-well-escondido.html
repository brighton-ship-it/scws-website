Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low Flow and Low Yield Wells in Escondido: A Guide for North County Well Owners

Escondido sits in a broad inland valley of northern San Diego County, ringed by hills and surrounded by the avocado groves, horse properties, and rural estates of areas like Hidden Meadows, Valley Center, San Pasqual, and the eastern reaches toward Lake Wohlford. On these larger parcels, a private well is often the backbone of the property — and when that well starts delivering weak flow, sputtering faucets, or pressure that collapses the moment a second fixture opens, daily life gets complicated fast. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years working the granite country of North County, and in our experience a low-flow or low-yield well almost always has a specific, identifiable cause that testing can reveal and the right repair can resolve.

It helps to separate two ideas. A low-yield well means the aquifer or the fractured rock simply cannot feed water into the well as fast as your household demands it — the pump may be in perfect shape. A low-flow symptom, by contrast, can also come from a pump set wrong, a plugged screen, or a failing component. On Escondido's distinctive geology, knowing which one you have makes all the difference.

Why Escondido Wells Run Low

Escondido rests on classic Southern California crystalline geology: a layer of decomposed granite (DG) overburden sitting on top of solid granitic bedrock. Wells here typically range from about 250 to 500 feet deep, and yield depends heavily on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole happens to intersect in that hard rock. Fractured granite in the Escondido area commonly produces on the order of 5 to 15 GPM — but that varies enormously from one property to the next, because well depth, water table, and fracture patterns can differ sharply even between neighboring lots. A well that hits a good fracture network is a strong producer; one that misses may have been marginal from the day it was drilled.

The usual reasons an Escondido well loses flow include:

  • A dropping or seasonal water table. In dry years, recharge into the fractured-rock system slows and the static level falls. When it drops toward the pump intake, the pump draws air during peak use and flow breaks up.
  • A pump set too high or undersized. Many older North County wells have pumps hung above today's water level, or pumps sized for a smaller household than the property now supports.
  • Scaled or biofouled screen and perforations. Even though Escondido's water is among the better-quality supply in the region — moderate hardness, low TDS, and minimal iron — scale and biofilm can still gradually restrict the openings that let water into the well.
  • Depleted fractures. A granite well's productive fractures can slowly yield less as regional levels decline, especially where nearby pumping draws on the same fracture network.
  • Sediment or DG infill. Decomposed granite and fine sand can wash into and settle in the bottom of the well, burying the productive interval over time.

Common Local Scenarios in North County

A grove owner in the San Pasqual Valley finds the well can't run the drip irrigation and the house together during the summer draw-down — a seasonal water-table issue. A Hidden Meadows family that added a pool and a guest house discovers their steady 8 GPM granite well now falls short at peak demand. An older Valley Center well starts pulling grit and losing pressure as DG sediment fills the casing bottom. Same-sounding symptom, three very different causes — which is exactly why we test the well before recommending anything.

What to Check When Flow Drops

Good diagnosis measures the well itself. When our technicians evaluate a low-flow Escondido well, we look at:

  • Static water level — where water stands with the pump off, versus the original well log, to see how the fractured-rock table has moved.
  • Pumping level and drawdown — how far the level drops while pumping. In granite wells, deep drawdown that keeps falling signals a limited fracture supply.
  • A yield and recovery test — sustained GPM and how fast the level recovers. Slow recovery is typical of a tight fracture network; quick recovery with weak flow points to a mechanical restriction.
  • Pump depth and condition — whether the pump sits correctly for today's level and whether the pump, wiring, and check valve are healthy.
  • Water clarity — grit or turbidity flags screen or casing problems rather than a simple pump fault.

Before we arrive, it helps to note whether flow weakens only during simultaneous use, only in the dry months, or all the time, and whether the water runs clear. Those clues let us come prepared with the right equipment.

Fixes That Work in Escondido's Granite Country

Because so many Escondido wells are drilled into fractured granite, the effective solutions here often revolve around fractures, pump placement, and storage:

  • Lower or replace the pump. When a falling table has left the pump too high, lowering it deeper restores flow directly. A worn or undersized submersible pump is swapped for a properly sized unit, typically $2,500 to $5,500 installed depending on the well's depth and horsepower.
  • Hydrofracturing. This is one of the most powerful tools for granite wells. High-pressure water is injected to open and connect additional fractures around the borehole, and it frequently doubles or triples the yield of a marginal Escondido well — often at a fraction of the cost of drilling new.
  • Well rehabilitation. Mechanical brushing plus chemical or acid treatment clears scale and biofilm from the screen and perforations, reopening the paths water uses to enter the well.
  • Deepening the well. Extending the borehole deeper into the granite can intersect additional water-bearing fractures where they exist.
  • Storage plus constant-pressure systems. When the sustainable yield is genuinely low, a large storage or cistern tank fills slowly around the clock and a booster or constant-pressure system ($2,000 to $4,500) delivers strong household pressure on demand — letting a low-GPM granite well serve a full property.
  • A new well. Where an old well is collapsed or poorly located, a new turnkey well runs roughly $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth and how hard the granite drills.

On the pressure side we also handle pressure switches ($150–$350), pressure tanks ($600–$1,500), and control box or capacitor replacement ($400–$900). Although Escondido's water quality is generally good, sediment filtration ($300–$900) can help where DG grit is an issue.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should an Escondido granite well produce?

Fractured granite in the Escondido area commonly yields around 5 to 15 GPM, but it varies widely because fracture patterns differ from lot to lot. A single-family home needs only 3–5 GPM, so even a modest granite well can serve a household — and a storage-plus-booster system lets a lower-yield well do the same.

Does hydrofracturing really work on Escondido wells?

Yes, it is one of the most effective options here. Because Escondido wells rely on water-bearing fractures in granite, injecting high-pressure water to open and connect more fractures frequently doubles or triples yield. We run a yield test first to confirm your well is a good candidate before recommending it.

Why does my Escondido well go weak in late summer?

That is usually a seasonal water-table drop. In dry months, recharge to the fractured-rock aquifer slows and the static level falls toward the pump intake, causing air and lost flow. Lowering the pump, hydrofracturing, or adding storage typically resolves it.

Is my low flow the pump or the well?

Only testing tells you for certain. We measure static level, drawdown, and recovery. If the level holds and flow is still weak, the pump or screen is likely restricting things; if the level crashes and recovers slowly, the granite's fracture supply is the limiting factor. Each points to a different fix.

What will it cost to fix a low-flow well in Escondido?

It depends on the cause. A new submersible pump runs $2,500–$5,500, hydrofracturing a marginal granite well is often far cheaper than drilling new, and a storage-plus-booster system runs $2,000–$4,500. A full new well is $18,000–$42,000. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward the repair.
