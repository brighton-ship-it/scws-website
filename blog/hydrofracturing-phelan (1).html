What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Phelan Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Wells on the High Desert Margins of Phelan

Phelan sits on the northern apron of the San Gabriel Mountains, where the Transverse Ranges give way to the western Mojave Desert. That transition zone is exactly what makes well water here so unpredictable. One property near the foothills off Sheep Creek Road may tap reliable water in fractured granite at 350 feet, while a neighbor a half-mile out onto the open desert floor near Phelan Road struggles in deep alluvial fill at 600 feet or more. When a Phelan well that once produced well starts to falter, hydrofracturing is one of the most cost-effective ways to find out whether the water is still there and simply blocked from reaching the borehole.

Hydrofracturing (commonly called hydrofracking) is not the chemical-and-sand process used in oil and gas extraction. For domestic water wells it uses nothing but clean water injected under controlled pressure to reopen and clean the natural cracks in bedrock that feed the well. In the right Phelan geology, the results can be dramatic.

Why Phelan's Geology Cuts Both Ways

Phelan straddles two very different aquifer settings, and honesty about which one your well sits in matters more than any sales pitch. Wells drilled into the fractured crystalline bedrock along the foothill margins — toward Wrightwood and the mountain front — are excellent hydrofracturing candidates. Water in that rock moves through joints and fractures rather than through pore space, and high-pressure injection can clean out mineral scale and silt that have slowly choked those pathways over the years.

By contrast, wells out on the deep alluvial basin floor, where water is stored in loose sand and gravel rather than rock, are weaker candidates. Hydrofracturing works by propagating and cleaning fractures; in unconsolidated sediment there are no fractures to open, so the technique has far less to offer. If your Phelan well is screened in sand and gravel, a thorough flow test, a pump evaluation, or in some cases a deepening or replacement well will serve you better. We will tell you which category your well falls into before you spend a dollar on fracturing.

How the Hydrofracturing Process Works on a Phelan Well

The crew arrives with a high-pressure pump rig and a set of inflatable packers. After pulling the existing pump, we run a downhole camera to map the casing, the screened interval, and any visible fracture zones. We then lower a packer (or a pair of packers to isolate a single zone) into the borehole and inflate it to seal off the section we want to treat. Clean water is injected at pressures that typically range from 1,000 to 3,000 PSI for the Phelan area's hard rock. The pressure pushes water into existing fractures, flushing out the fine sediment, calcium, and iron scale that accumulate over decades, and extending those fractures slightly farther into the surrounding rock.

A single Phelan well usually takes four to eight hours. After treatment, the well is surged and developed to remove loosened debris, the pump is reinstalled, and a post-treatment flow test confirms the gain. Because no chemicals are introduced, there is no special disinfection or waiting period beyond ordinary well sanitizing, and the water is safe to use as soon as it clears.

Common Phelan Scenarios We See

  • The slow decline. A well off Phelan Road that gave 12 gallons per minute a decade ago now barely keeps up with the household during summer. Mineral buildup in the fracture network is the usual culprit, and it responds well to fracturing.
  • The disappointing new well. A freshly drilled foothill well hit rock but came in at only 3 to 4 GPM. Fracturing often opens additional fracture pathways the drill bit passed near but did not connect.
  • The dry-season scare. A well that runs fine in spring sputters by August. Hydrofracturing can widen the well's effective reach into the aquifer so it draws from a larger volume of rock.
  • The deep alluvial well. Honestly, this one usually is not a fracturing job. We steer these owners toward pump and flow diagnostics or a new well, and we say so up front.

What to Check Before You Call

A little observation at home helps us help you faster. Note whether your pressure drops only during heavy use or all the time. Watch for air spitting from faucets, which can mean the water level is dropping to the pump intake. Listen for the pump short-cycling on and off, and check whether your pressure tank holds pressure between draws. Pull up your original well log if you have it; the driller's record of depth, casing, and the formation logged is the single most useful document for deciding whether fracturing makes sense. If you do not have the log, San Bernardino County and the California Department of Water Resources keep well completion records on file.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires equipment that can safely generate and contain thousands of PSI downhole, and a misjudged treatment can damage casing or collapse a poorly constructed well. This is not a do-it-yourself project. Call a licensed contractor when production has dropped noticeably, when a new well underperforms, or before you commit to the far larger expense of a replacement well. A professional flow test and camera inspection will tell you whether the water is gone or simply blocked — and only one of those problems is worth fracturing for.

Hydrofracturing vs. Drilling Deeper in the High Desert

Phelan owners weighing fracturing against other fixes should understand the trade-offs. Drilling deeper can help when the water table has fallen below an alluvial well's intake, but it offers little in fractured rock where the issue is clogged fractures rather than depth. Mechanical redevelopment clears fines from a screen but cannot reopen rock fractures. Hydrofracturing is the targeted tool when a foothill granite well has simply lost connection to its fracture network — the most common cause of decline along the mountain margin. Because the high desert mixes these geologies block by block, the only way to choose correctly is a flow test, a camera inspection, and a look at your well log. We bring all three to the assessment so the recommendation fits your specific well rather than a generic formula.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing work on my Phelan well?

It depends on your geology. Wells in fractured foothill granite respond very well; wells in deep desert alluvium usually do not. A camera inspection and your well log let us give you a straight answer before any work begins.

How much can hydrofracturing increase my well's output?

In suitable fractured-rock wells we commonly see flow improve by two to five times, and occasionally more. Results vary, and we always run a before-and-after flow test so the gain is documented rather than guessed at.

Is it safe for my drinking water?

Yes. Domestic well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure — no chemicals, sand, or additives. After we develop the well and reinstall the pump, normal well sanitizing is all that is needed.

How long do the results last?

Many Phelan wells hold their improved yield for years. Because mineral scale and silt build up again over time, some wells benefit from a repeat treatment after a decade or so, but the gain is typically long-lasting.

Could hydrofracturing damage my well?

When performed by a licensed professional on a sound well, the risk is low. We inspect casing condition first, and if a well is too deteriorated to treat safely we will tell you and recommend a better option rather than risk it.

Should I fracture my existing well or just drill a new one?

Almost always, fracturing is worth trying first when the geology supports it, because it costs a fraction of a new well. If your well is in alluvium, badly corroded, or has structural problems, a new well may be the smarter investment — and we will give you an honest recommendation either way.
