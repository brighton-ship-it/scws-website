Understanding Well Permit Exemptions in California

Very few true exemptions exist in California for well permits. County permits are required for nearly all water supply wells. State law (Water Code Section 13751) mandates well construction standards. The term 'exempt' often refers to SGMA reporting exemptions, not construction permit exemptions. Confusion between permit exemptions and water rights exemptions is common.

De Minimis Users Under SGMA

De minimis users extract under 2 acre-feet per year (about 650,000 gallons). This exemption applies to GSA fees and some reporting requirements, NOT to well construction permits. Single-family domestic wells often qualify as de minimis. GSAs can still require registration even for de minimis wells. This exemption does not eliminate county permit requirements.

Monitoring and Test Wells

Monitoring wells may have a simplified permit process in some counties. Test holes for geotechnical purposes are sometimes exempt from well permits. Cathodic protection wells have different requirements. Geothermal heat exchange wells may follow a different permitting path. Always verify with your county before assuming any exemption applies.

When You Always Need a Permit

A permit is always required for any well used for drinking water supply, agricultural or irrigation water supply wells, wells on any parcel regardless of size, modifying, deepening, or destroying existing wells, and converting any unpermitted well to legal status.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drill a well on my property without a permit in California?

In virtually all cases, no. California law requires permits for water supply wells regardless of property size or intended use. Some property owners confuse SGMA de minimis exemptions (for pumping fees/reporting) with permit exemptions—they are not the same. Always contact your county environmental health or building department before drilling.

What happens if I drill a well without a permit in California?

Unpermitted wells violate state and county codes. Penalties can include fines of $1,000-$5,000 or more, required destruction of the well at your expense, and difficulty selling your property. You may be required to bring the well up to current standards through a costly retrofit or destroy it entirely.

My property has an old well with no records—is it legal to use?

Wells drilled before permit requirements (generally pre-1950 in most counties) may be legal to use but often lack documentation. Contact your county to research permit history and determine what's needed. You may need a well inspection, water quality testing, and possibly retrofitting to meet current standards before the well can be approved for continued use.
