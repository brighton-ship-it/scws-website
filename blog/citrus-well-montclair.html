Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Citrus Well Service in Montclair, San Bernardino County

Growers here, whether they keep a backyard stand of trees or run a working orchard on the edge of town, rely on private agricultural wells to pull water from the basin below. What sets Montclair apart is its position over the Chino Basin, one of the most carefully managed groundwater systems in the region. The basin's long agricultural history, its mix of recharge and pumping, and its active management all matter to anyone who owns a well here. Understanding Citrus Irrigation Demand

Citrus does not take the winter off. As an evergreen, it transpires through every month of the year, and in Montclair's hot, dry summers the water demand climbs steeply. For planning purposes, a mature citrus grove generally consumes in the neighborhood of 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre each year. The precise figure shifts with tree age, variety, rootstock, canopy density, and the severity of the summer.

The number that drives well design, though, is peak flow. On the hottest days of July and August, the grove's daily water use jumps, and the irrigation system must keep pace or the root zone dries and the trees suffer. We help Montclair growers convert their acreage and tree counts into a target gallons-per-minute (GPM) figure built around that peak, so the well and pump are never the bottleneck when the weather turns brutal.

Planning for Frost Events

Even in the relatively mild Chino Valley, cold snaps can threaten citrus on clear winter nights. Growers who protect their fruit with water-based frost protection ask a great deal of their wells: micro-sprinkler frost systems can call for roughly 50 or more GPM per acre, delivered continuously for hours while temperatures hover near freezing. A well comfortably sized for ordinary irrigation can fall short during a frost event, so we factor frost demand into the design whenever a grower wants that protection available.

Building the Right Well, Pump, and Storage Combination

A citrus water supply is only as strong as its weakest link. Three components have to be balanced: the yield of the well, the capacity of the pump, and the buffer provided by storage.

Well Yield and Capacity

We assess what the well can sustainably produce through proper testing, looking at both flow and how far the water level draws down under load. Where an existing well comes up short, techniques such as hydrofracturing can sometimes restore or improve yield, depending on the geology.

High-Capacity Pumping Equipment

Commercial citrus typically calls for a high-capacity submersible pump, or a vertical turbine pump in larger or shallower installations. These pumps are engineered to move serious volume against the pressure an orchard system needs. Getting the selection right matters: a pump matched to the well and the grove runs efficiently and lasts, while a mismatched one wastes energy and wears out early.

Ponds and Tanks for Peak Demand

Because the sharpest demand, peak summer irrigation and frost protection, can outrun a well's instantaneous output, storage often makes the difference. A pond or a set of tanks lets the well fill steadily around the clock, and the grove then draws from that reserve at a high rate when it counts. For many Montclair operations, storage is the smartest investment they make.

Water Quality Considerations for Citrus

Citrus ranks among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so the chemistry of your well water is not a detail to overlook. We pay particular attention to three factors.

  • Salinity (EC): Total dissolved salts, measured as electrical conductivity, limit how readily citrus takes up water. High salinity stresses the trees and erodes yield over time, and managing it may require lower-salt water or deliberate leaching.
  • Chloride: Citrus tends to accumulate chloride in its leaves, producing the characteristic burn along leaf tips and edges. Irrigation water high in chloride can scorch foliage and set trees back, especially on sensitive rootstocks.
  • Boron: Helpful in trace amounts but toxic just above them, boron becomes a concern for citrus generally above about 0.5 to 1 part per million. Like chloride, excess boron shows up as leaf burn.

In the Chino Basin, groundwater in some historically agricultural and former dairy areas can also carry nitrate, which growers occasionally ask about. For citrus irrigation specifically, salinity, chloride, and boron usually drive the management decisions. We collect samples, explain the lab results clearly, and recommend blending, treatment, or rootstock and irrigation adjustments where the water calls for them.

Drought, the Chino Basin, and Groundwater Management

Drought is a recurring fact of life in Southern California, and it hits private wells directly. As the basin draws down through dry years, water levels fall and yields can slip at the very moment citrus is most demanding. The Chino Basin is an actively managed groundwater system, governed through arrangements such as the Chino Basin Watermaster that balance pumping against replenishment. For Montclair growers, that means using water efficiently, watching well performance over the seasons, and dealing with early signs of decline before they turn into emergencies.

Problems We Commonly See on Montclair Citrus Wells

  • Yield decline from an aging well or a dropping water table, leaving the grove short during the summer peak.
  • Pump failures driven by sediment, scale, age, or a pump poorly matched to the well's characteristics.
  • Plugged emitters and micro-sprinklers from sediment, mineral buildup, or biological growth in the irrigation lines.
  • Gradual water-quality shifts, with salinity or chloride climbing over a season until leaf symptoms appear.
  • Control and electrical faults on older systems that cut off irrigation without warning.

Keeping a Citrus Well Healthy

Routine maintenance is the cheapest insurance a grower can buy. We suggest periodic water testing, regular pump and pressure-system inspections, flushing of filters and irrigation lines, and ongoing tracking of flow and drawdown so a downward trend reveals itself early. A monitored well is a predictable well, and predictability is exactly what a grove needs.

When It Is Time to Call Us

Reach out when pressure or flow drops, when emitters sputter air, when the pump short-cycles or never shuts off, when energy costs climb unexpectedly, when leaf burn hints at a water-chemistry problem, or when frost season exposes a shortfall in supply. Each of these is a signal worth investigating promptly, and early action almost always means a smaller bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water will my Montclair citrus grove use?

Mature citrus generally uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year, with peak demand concentrated in the hot summer months. If you also want frost protection, plan for up to 50 or more GPM per acre during a freeze. We size your system to those peaks.

Is my well water safe for citrus?

It depends on the chemistry. Citrus is salt-sensitive, so we check salinity, chloride, and boron, and in the Chino Basin we can address nitrate questions as well. Testing tells us whether your water needs blending or treatment.

Why would I need storage if I already have a well?

Storage lets a steady-yielding well meet brief but intense demand, like a hot afternoon or an overnight frost event. A pond or tanks fill slowly and release fast, covering the gap your well alone cannot.

My well does not produce like it used to. What now?

Have it tested. Depending on the cause, options include pump service, well rehabilitation, or hydrofracturing to improve flow. Addressing decline early protects your trees through peak season.

Does Chino Basin management affect my pumping?

The Chino Basin is an actively managed groundwater system. We help growers use water efficiently and keep their wells productive within that framework.

How quickly can you come out for an emergency?

We provide same-day emergency service. When trees are losing water in summer heat, we respond fast to restore your supply.
