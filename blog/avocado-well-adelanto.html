Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Adelanto well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Honest Well and Irrigation Guidance for Adelanto Property Owners

Adelanto is a high-desert city in San Bernardino County, sitting at roughly 3,400 feet in the Victor Valley about nine miles northwest of Victorville. It is classic Mojave Desert country, with wide horizons, alkali flats, scattered Joshua trees, and long, dry summers punctuated by cold winter nights. We want to be upfront with anyone searching for avocado well service here: Adelanto is not avocado-growing territory. The combination of hard winter frosts and brutal summer heat makes commercial avocado production essentially impossible. Avocado trees suffer damage when temperatures fall into the high 20s, and Adelanto's winters drop well below that. Even a few backyard trees would face constant frost and water-quality stress.

So rather than sell you a fantasy, let us talk about what wells in Adelanto actually do: supply households on rural parcels, water livestock and horse properties, irrigate hardy desert landscaping, and support small-scale hobby growing of frost-tolerant plants. That is where our work matters in the High Desert, and we have been doing it across San Bernardino County for more than 30 years.

How a Desert Well System Works in the Victor Valley

Adelanto overlies the Mojave River groundwater basin, where water is drawn from deep alluvial deposits rather than shallow surface aquifers. A typical desert well system uses a submersible pump set deep in the borehole, sized for significant lift, often 1 to 5 horsepower depending on depth and flow. Water rises to a pressure tank and switch that maintain household pressure, and many properties add a large storage tank so a modest-yield well can fill overnight and meet daytime demand for irrigation or livestock. Because desert wells run hard in summer and pump against considerable depth, sizing the pump correctly is the single most important decision, since an undersized or oversized pump fails early.

Common Local Scenarios in Adelanto

The problems that bring us out to Adelanto and the surrounding Victor Valley are shaped by desert geology and a declining regional water table:

  • Falling water tables: Regional groundwater levels in the Mojave basin have declined over the years, leaving some older, shallower wells unable to reach water.
  • Pumps running dry or short-cycling as the water column drops below the pump intake.
  • High total dissolved solids: Sodium-bicarbonate and sulfate chemistry near Adelanto causes scale, salty taste, and staining.
  • Sediment and sand from desert formations wearing out pumps and clogging fixtures.
  • Heavy summer demand for any landscaping, animals, or hobby trees during 100-plus-degree heat.

Frost-protection irrigation is not part of the picture here in the way it would be for a frost-prone grove. The realistic Adelanto challenge is simply finding, lifting, and treating enough desert groundwater.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Pump behavior: Does it run continuously, cycle rapidly, or pump air and sputter? Sputtering often means the water level has dropped near the pump.
  • Pressure: Note whether pressure is steady or weak, and whether the tank feels waterlogged.
  • Water quality: Watch for salty taste, white scale, cloudiness, or sand in the water.
  • Well records: The original well log and pump depth, if you have them, tell us how much room there is to lower the pump.
  • Power: Rural desert circuits can suffer voltage swings that damage pump motors.

When to Call a Professional

Why High-Desert Expertise Matters in Adelanto

Drilling and servicing wells in the Mojave is a different discipline from coastal or valley work, and hiring a crew that understands the High Desert is the difference between a well that lasts and one that disappoints. Adelanto's water comes from deep alluvial deposits in the Mojave River groundwater basin, and the regional water table has been declining for years. That means depth, draw-down, and recovery rate all have to be read correctly before a pump is even selected. A pump set too high pulls air as the water level drops through the season; set with the wrong horsepower for the lift, it burns out early. Stretching a Desert Well Through the Dry Season

For Adelanto property owners with livestock, horses, or any landscaping, the summer is the real test. Daytime temperatures above 100 degrees push water demand to its annual peak exactly when a declining aquifer is least forgiving. The most cost-effective answer is rarely a brand-new well; more often it is smart system design. A generous storage tank lets a modest well run steadily overnight and bank water for the heat of the day, so the pump is not fighting peak demand in real time. Where yield itself has fallen, hydrofracturing can reopen water-bearing fractures and restore production at a fraction of the cost of redrilling. And when a pump genuinely needs to go deeper to follow a dropping water level, we can lower or replace it correctly. The goal is always to get you the most reliable water for the least money, and in the desert that means designing around scarcity rather than pretending it is not there.

Can avocados be grown commercially in Adelanto?

No. Adelanto sits in the high desert of San Bernardino County at roughly 3,400 feet, where hard frosts and intense summer heat make commercial avocado growing impractical. Avocado trees are damaged at temperatures below the high 20s, and Adelanto routinely drops well below that in winter. How deep are wells in the Adelanto area?

Adelanto overlies the Mojave River groundwater basin. Depths vary widely with the parcel; public-supply wells in the Mojave study area are commonly completed between 200 and 600 feet, and private wells on the valley margins often need to be deep to reach a reliable aquifer. Declining regional water tables mean some older, shallower wells no longer reach water.

Why does my Adelanto well water taste salty or leave deposits?

Groundwater near Adelanto can carry sodium-bicarbonate and sulfate chemistry with elevated total dissolved solids, which causes scale, salty taste, and staining. Treatment options include sediment filtration, softening, and reverse osmosis for drinking water. We test first, then recommend only the treatment your water actually needs.

My water table dropped and my pump runs dry. What now?

This is common in the high desert as groundwater levels decline. Options include lowering the pump, hydrofracturing to improve yield, or in some cases drilling deeper. Hydrofracturing typically runs $3,000-$8,000 and can restore production to an underperforming well at a fraction of the cost of a new one.
