Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Yield Wells in the Dulzura Backcountry

Dulzura is one of San Diego County's most rugged backcountry communities, spread along the western slopes of Otay Mountain in the rural southeast corner of the county, near Jamul, Potrero, and the Mexican border. There is no municipal water service out here, so a private well is essential to every property, and the local geology makes those wells genuinely variable. Dulzura's terrain mixes granite, metavolcanic rock, decomposed granite, and ribbons of alluvium along creek beds such as Dulzura Creek. Shallower wells often tap alluvial water near the drainages, while deeper wells push into the Peninsular Ranges batholith, hard granitic and metamorphic rock, to reach more reliable supply. Well depths in the area vary enormously, from a few dozen feet to well over a thousand, with averages around 500 feet. When a Dulzura property has a low-flow or low-yield well, the cause is almost always tied to which of these formations the well sits in.

Because there is no backup utility connection in Dulzura, getting a low-yield well sorted out matters more here than almost anywhere. The path forward starts with measuring what you actually have.

How to Test Your Well's Flow and Yield

Two numbers tell the story: the flow at your fixtures and the sustained yield of the aquifer. For a quick read, run an outdoor hydrant into a five-gallon bucket and time the fill. Twenty seconds is 15 GPM; one minute is 5 GPM; two or three minutes means you are at 2.5 GPM or less and have a genuine low-yield situation.

The number that really counts comes from a sustained yield (drawdown) test. Since the pressure tank stores water and can mask a weak well for a few minutes, we pump steadily, watch the water level drop, and time the recovery. A Dulzura home generally needs 3 to 5 GPM of dependable supply, and properties with livestock, orchards, or irrigation need much more, often 10 to 30 GPM from deeper fractured-rock zones. Fractured-rock wells here commonly settle at 1 to 3 GPM, which is workable with the right storage strategy but demanding on equipment.

Why Dulzura Wells Run Low

  • Low fracture yield in hard rock. Wells finished in Otay Mountain's granite and metavolcanic rock produce only what their fractures release. A parcel that crosses a sparse fracture network will run low regardless of pump size.
  • Seasonal and long-term water-table decline. Backcountry recharge depends on winter rain. After dry years, static levels fall and marginal wells lose flow first.
  • Clogged or encrusted screen. Mineral-rich backcountry water leaves calcium and iron scale on screen slots and gravel pack, gradually sealing the openings where water enters.
  • Biofouling. Iron and sulfur bacteria form slimes that plug the intake, often with staining or a sulfur odor.
  • Pump set wrong or sized wrong. A pump above the productive fractures loses prime when the level drops, and an oversized pump pulls a modest well down faster than it refills, causing short cycling and air at the taps.

It also helps to keep low yield and low pressure separate in your mind, because they feel identical at the faucet but have different cures. Pressure is how forcefully water moves through your plumbing; yield is how much the well can actually supply over time. A Dulzura home can show fine pressure for a few minutes, then fade as the well outpaces its fractures. Measuring sustained yield, not a quick burst, is what reveals which problem you really have.

Solutions and Their Tradeoffs

Well Rehabilitation

For an older Dulzura well that has slowly weakened, cleaning the screen and casing with brushing, surging, and chemical treatment often recovers meaningful yield lost to scale and bacteria. At roughly $1,500 to $5,000, it is the logical first step when a well used to produce more. It restores capacity but cannot exceed what the formation supplies.

Hydrofracturing

For the hard-rock wells common in Dulzura, hydrofracturing injects high-pressure water to open and connect existing fractures, frequently doubling or tripling yield. Because most wells here are finished in granitic or metavolcanic rock, it is a realistic option for many low-yield properties, at about $3,000 to $8,000.

Deepening

Drilling deeper can reach more reliable fractured-rock water below the shallow alluvium, and in Dulzura that often means going substantially deeper. It can pay off, but crystalline rock is unpredictable, so we weigh nearby well logs before recommending it.

Storage Cistern With a Booster or Constant-Pressure Pump

For chronic low yield in a place with no utility backup, this is the solution we recommend most. A slow well fills a large cistern, often 3,000 to 5,000 gallons or more on rural parcels, around the clock, and a booster or constant-pressure pump then delivers strong, steady household pressure on demand. A 2 GPM well running continuously produces nearly 2,900 gallons a day, well beyond typical household use, so the tank handles your peaks while the well refills it. A generous cistern also provides crucial reserve for fire protection in this wildfire-prone backcountry. Plan on roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed, more for very large agricultural tanks. The tradeoff is space and a little extra equipment, but it converts an unreliable well into a dependable one without touching the geology.

Right-Sizing the Pump

Sometimes the fix is simply matching the pump to the well, setting it at the correct depth, or adding a flow-control valve so it cannot outrun the fractures. A properly sized pump and pressure tank cost $2,500 to $5,500.

When to Call a Professional

Sputtering air at the taps, a short-cycling pump, cloudy or sandy water, or a clear drop in flow all mean it is time for a professional yield evaluation rather than guesswork. In Dulzura's varied terrain the cause is rarely obvious from the surface, and replacing the wrong component is an expensive mistake when you have no utility to fall back on. Frequently Asked Questions

Dulzura has no city water, so what happens if my well runs low?

Your property depends entirely on the well, which is why low yield needs prompt attention here. The most reliable safeguard is a storage cistern with a booster pump, giving you steady pressure and a water reserve even when the well produces slowly.

Why do well depths and yields vary so much in Dulzura?

The area mixes granite, metavolcanic rock, decomposed granite, and creek-bed alluvium. Shallow wells may tap alluvial water near drainages, while deeper wells reach fractured bedrock. Depths range from dozens of feet to over a thousand, averaging around 500.

Is hydrofracturing worth it for a Dulzura well?

Often yes. Most wells here are finished in hard granitic or metavolcanic rock, and hydrofracturing opens existing fractures to boost yield, frequently two- to threefold. It is one of the more effective options in this terrain.

Can a storage tank help with fire protection too?

Yes. A large cistern not only buffers a low-yield well for daily use, it also provides a valuable water reserve for fire defense, which matters in Dulzura's wildfire-prone backcountry. We can size the tank with that reserve in mind.

What does it cost to improve a low-yield well in Dulzura?

Rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, a storage-plus-booster system $3,000 to $7,000 (more for large agricultural tanks), and a right-sized pump and tank $2,500 to $5,500.
