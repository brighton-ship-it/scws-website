Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Bloomington Well Pump Won't Start? Let's Diagnose It

Bloomington is an unincorporated community in San Bernardino County, tucked into the San Bernardino Valley between Fontana, Rialto, and Colton along the historic Valley Boulevard corridor. Sitting on the broad alluvial floor of the valley, Bloomington's wells draw from sandy, gravelly aquifer deposits washed down from the surrounding mountains. The community's semi-rural character — with its horse properties, larger lots, and agricultural roots — means private wells are still a meaningful part of life here. When one of those pumps won't start, a household loses water fast, and understanding the system is the first step to getting it back.

Southern California Well Service has serviced wells throughout San Bernardino County since 2020. Here is a clear breakdown of what causes a Bloomington pump to go quiet.

The Six Parts That Keep Water Flowing

Your tap depends on a small, interconnected pressure system. A failure anywhere along it stops the water:

  • Power supply: a dedicated 240-volt circuit feeds the pump, so a tripped breaker or supply fault is the first thing to check.
  • Pressure switch: mounted at the tank, it senses pressure and tells the pump when to cycle. Contacts pit and corrode with use.
  • Control box and capacitor: three-wire submersibles rely on a control box whose start capacitor gives the motor its first push; a dead capacitor causes a hum with no flow.
  • Pressure tank: the bladder tank stores pressurized water and prevents constant cycling. When it waterlogs, the pump short-cycles and overheats.
  • Motor and wiring: the submersible motor and downhole splices can fail with age, sediment, or insulation breakdown.
  • Water level: the valley aquifer fluctuates with regional pumping and drought; a declining table can reach the pump intake and trip its low-water cutoff.

What Drives Bloomington Pump Failures

Bloomington's valley-floor geology gives its wells a distinct character. The sandy, gravelly aquifer means sediment and fine sand are a recurring theme — grit works into pumps, wears impellers, and can foul check valves, contributing to failures over time. Many wells here also serve irrigation for horse properties and gardens, and an irrigation pump left idle through the winter often won't restart in spring because contacts have corroded while resting. Summer heat in the inland valley stresses capacitors, producing afternoon failures, and the region's hard, mineral-rich water lays down scale on switch contacts and pump components year after year. On lots that have been developed for decades, an aging control box capacitor is one of the most common culprits we replace.

Safe Checks You Can Do

  1. Reset the breaker once. Locate the double-pole 240V well breaker and reset it a single time if tripped. If it trips again immediately, that's a fault — stop and call.
  2. Listen at the pressure switch. With a faucet open, a faint click means the switch is signaling the pump. Silence points to the switch or its power.
  3. Read the gauge. Note the PSI and whether the needle is steady, stuck, or erratic.
  4. Check for sediment and corrosion. Look for grit at fixtures, corroded terminals, or moisture at the junction box.
  5. Test other 240V appliances. If your dryer or range is also affected, the main panel may be at fault.

Safety: Never open the control box or touch energized terminals. The 240-volt circuit and the downhole motor can be lethal.

How We Track Down the Problem

In the field, our technicians follow a logical sequence rather than guessing. We first confirm that voltage is present at the breaker and the pressure switch, which separates an electrical-supply issue from a mechanical one. Then we test the switch and, on three-wire systems, the control box and capacitor — inexpensive parts behind many no-starts. If those check out, we examine the downhole motor, wiring splices, and check valve, and we verify the pressure tank's air charge and the well's water level. With Bloomington's sandy aquifer, we also look closely for sediment-related wear. This disciplined approach means you don't pay to replace parts that were never the problem.

Why Sediment Management Matters Here

Because Bloomington sits on sandy, gravelly valley fill, sediment is the recurring background issue behind much of the equipment wear we see. Fine sand pumped along with the water abrades impellers, scores seals, and can hold a check valve partly open, all of which shorten pump life and occasionally contribute to a no-start. When we replace a pump on a sandy Bloomington well, we frequently recommend pairing it with sediment filtration sized to the well's output. The filter is a small investment relative to a pump, and it protects not only the pump but every fixture and appliance downstream. For irrigation-heavy horse properties, keeping sand out of the system also protects valves and emitters.

Reading the Symptoms Before You Call

The way your pump fails tells an experienced technician a great deal, and a few seconds of observation can speed your repair considerably. A pump that is completely silent with a tripped breaker usually means an electrical fault. A pump that runs nonstop but never builds pressure suggests a downhole problem, a failed check valve, or a water level that has dropped below the intake. A pump that snaps on and off every few seconds is almost certainly short-cycling because the pressure tank has waterlogged. When you call us, describing which of these patterns you hear — along with the gauge reading and whether the well serves the house, irrigation, or both — lets us bring the right parts to Bloomington on the first trip.

What Each Component Does When It Fails

Understanding the failure modes removes much of the mystery. A pressure switch cycles thousands of times and eventually its contacts pit and stick. A start capacitor weakens with heat and age until it can no longer kick the motor into motion, which is why capacitor failures cluster on hot valley afternoons. A pressure tank's internal bladder can rupture, flooding the tank and forcing the pump into damaging rapid cycles. And a submersible motor, sealed deep in the casing, fails from insulation breakdown or from running dry against a low water table. Each mechanism leads to a specific, well-understood repair, which is why accurate diagnosis is so much more valuable than swapping parts at random.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Bloomington irrigation well won't start after the winter. Why?

Idle irrigation pumps often suffer corroded contacts or weakened capacitors over the off-season. The switch or capacitor frequently needs service rather than the whole pump.

Why does my pump hum without pumping water?

A hum means the motor is energized but cannot spin — usually a failed start capacitor, a seized motor, or a stuck check valve. It needs professional diagnosis.

Does Bloomington's sandy soil affect my well?

Yes. The valley's sandy, gravelly aquifer can introduce sediment that wears pumps and fouls check valves over time. Sediment filtration is often a worthwhile addition here.

Can I keep resetting the breaker?

No. Repeated tripping signals a genuine fault and risks fire or motor damage. Reset once; if it trips again, call a professional.
