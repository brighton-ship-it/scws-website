Well Pump Won't Start in Thermal?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Thermal homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

Common Reasons a Well Pump Won't Start in Thermal

In the low desert, the usual no-start causes are compounded by extreme heat and deep, hard-water wells. Here are the failures we see most around Thermal:

  • Tripped breaker or blown fuse: No power reaching the pump is the first thing to rule out. Summer grid strain and surges trip breakers on 240-volt pump circuits regularly.
  • Failed pressure switch or clogged sensing port: The hard, mineral-laden water of the Coachella Valley is notorious for scaling. Mineral deposits clog the pressure switch's sensing port and corrode its contacts, leaving the pump with no signal to start.
  • Burned-out capacitor or control box: Heat is the enemy of capacitors. A control box baking in 115-degree summer temperatures will see capacitors fail far faster than in a coastal climate, and a dead capacitor often leaves the motor humming but motionless.
  • Failed or seized motor or pump: Deep Coachella Valley wells—often hundreds of feet deep—put long submersible pumps under heavy load. Scale buildup and sediment accelerate bearing and impeller wear until the motor seizes.
  • Wiring and corrosion faults: Long drop-cable runs in deep wells and surface wiring exposed to heat and dust can corrode or break.
  • Stuck check valve: Scale can lodge a check valve open or shut, disrupting normal cycling.
  • Waterlogged or air-bound pressure tank: A tank that has lost its air charge short-cycles the system and can prevent a clean start.
  • Rural power problems: Agricultural Thermal is served by rural distribution lines feeding large irrigation loads. Voltage sags during peak demand and summer heat events can drop a pump offline or damage its motor.

Safety-First Step-by-Step Checks

Residential and agricultural well pumps run on 240 volts—enough to seriously injure or kill. In Thermal's heat, also be aware that metal equipment and panels can be dangerously hot to the touch. Work carefully.

  1. Turn the power OFF at the breaker first. Always switch off the pump's double-pole breaker before inspecting the pressure switch, control box, or wellhead. 240V is dangerous and is the cause of most well-service injuries.
  2. With power off, check the main panel for a tripped or scorched breaker. Reset it a single time only.
  3. Restore power and listen at the pressure tank for the pressure switch to click, then watch whether the gauge moves.
  4. Confirm other 240V loads on the property are working to separate a site-wide outage from a pump-only fault.
  5. Visually inspect surface wiring and the control box for heat damage, scale, or corrosion—never while energized.
  6. If the breaker trips immediately on reset, stop and call a professional. Repeated resets risk fire.

Electrical Causes Worth Understanding

Most Thermal no-start calls are electrical at heart. The valley's heat and hard water gang up on the two most failure-prone parts: the pressure switch and the start/run capacitor. Scale fouls switch contacts and sensing ports; heat cooks capacitors. Add the voltage instability common on rural agricultural feeders, and you have a recipe for thermal-overload trips and breaker faults that look like a dead pump but aren't. A technician will measure incoming voltage under load, test capacitor microfarads, and check motor winding resistance to determine whether the supply, the controls, or the pump itself is the real problem—critical before anyone pulls hundreds of feet of pipe out of a deep well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does hard water cause my Thermal well pump to stop?

The Coachella Valley's mineral-rich water leaves scale that clogs the pressure switch's sensing port and corrodes its contacts, so the pump never gets the signal to start. Scale also builds inside the pump and check valve, increasing wear. Regular service helps prevent these mineral-related failures.

Does the desert heat really shorten the life of pump parts?

Yes. Capacitors and control boxes are heat-sensitive, and a control box sitting in Thermal's summer sun fails much sooner than one in a mild climate. Shading and proper enclosure ratings help, but heat-related capacitor failure is one of the most common no-start causes we see locally.

How deep are wells around Thermal?

Coachella Valley wells are often quite deep—frequently several hundred feet—because the aquifer lies well below the valley floor. Your well's depth determines the pump, drop cable, and hoisting equipment required for service, which is why deeper wells cost more to repair.

My pump hums but won't run. What does that mean?

A motor that hums without turning usually points to a failed start capacitor or control box, a common heat-driven failure in Thermal. Turn the power off and call a professional; running it in that state can overheat and ruin the motor.

Can a summer power sag damage my pump?

It can. Rural agricultural feeders in the Coachella Valley can sag under peak summer load. Low voltage makes the motor overheat and draw excess current, which can damage the motor or trip the overload. Have the electrical supply checked if your pump quit during a hot, high-demand period.
