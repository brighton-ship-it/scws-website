How a Residential Well and Pressure System Actually Works

Before you can understand why pressure drops, it helps to know how the pieces fit together. Your well sits in fractured bedrock or sediment beneath the property, and a submersible pump near the bottom of the casing pushes water up to the surface. That water flows into a pressure tank, which holds a cushion of compressed air above a rubber diaphragm. As the pump fills the tank, air pressure builds; as you use water, the air pushes it back out to your fixtures. A pressure switch watches the tank and tells the pump when to start (typically around 40 PSI) and stop (around 60 PSI). When everything is healthy, you get steady flow without the pump cycling on and off every few seconds. When pressure feels weak in Del Cerro homes, the fault almost always lies in one of these four links: the pump, the tank, the switch, or the pipes between them and your tap.

Why Del Cerro Wells Lose Pressure

The geology under Del Cerro is classic coastal San Diego County: a skin of sandy loam and clay over granitic and metavolcanic bedrock, with groundwater stored in narrow fractures rather than a deep, generous aquifer. That setting, combined with the neighborhood's elevation and San Diego's hard, mineral-rich water, produces a recognizable set of pressure problems:

  • Worn or failing well pump: Pumps that have to lift water to a hilltop lot work harder than pumps on flat ground. After 10 to 15 years the impellers wear, output drops, and you notice it first as weak pressure at the highest fixtures in the house.
  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: When the air bladder in the tank ruptures or loses its charge, the pump short-cycles and pressure swings wildly between strong and weak. This is one of the most common calls we get in older San Diego neighborhoods.
  • Pressure switch out of adjustment or corroded: The switch contacts pit and corrode over time, or the cut-in/cut-out settings drift, leaving the whole system running at lower pressure than it should.
  • Mineral scale and sediment in pipes: Del Cerro's hard water deposits calcium and magnesium scale that gradually narrows older galvanized pipe and clogs inline filters, choking flow long before you ever look at the well itself.
  • Declining well yield: During the dry San Diego summer, a fractured-rock well can simply run short of water during peak demand, causing pressure to sag in the late afternoon and recover overnight.
  • Hidden underground leaks: A cracked supply line buried under a landscaped slope can bleed off pressure silently, sometimes showing up only as an unexplained rise in pump run time.

What You Can Check Before You Call

A few safe checks at home can tell you a lot and sometimes solve the problem outright:

  • Read the pressure gauge. Find the gauge on your pressure tank and watch it through a cycle. Healthy systems swing between roughly 40 and 60 PSI. If it barely moves, or the pump cycles every few seconds, note that for us.
  • Test whether the problem is everywhere or one fixture. If only one faucet or showerhead is weak, the issue is likely a clogged aerator or fixture, not the well. If the whole house is low, the system is the suspect.
  • Check the pressure tank's air charge. With the pump off and the tank drained, an empty tank should read about 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in setting. A reading far off, or water spitting from the air valve, points to a failed tank.
  • Look and listen at the wellhead. Rapid clicking, grinding, or a breaker that keeps tripping all point to pump or electrical trouble that needs a professional.
  • Inspect any sediment or filter cartridges. A filter that has not been changed in a year can quietly strangle your whole-house flow.

Preventing Pressure Problems Before They Start

The most affordable repair is the one you never need. A little routine attention keeps a Del Cerro well system delivering strong, steady pressure for years. Schedule a yearly pump and system inspection so worn parts are caught before they fail on a hot summer weekend. Check the pressure tank's air charge twice a year and keep it about 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in setting. Because Del Cerro's water is hard, change or clean inline sediment filters every three to six months and consider a softener to slow scale buildup in pipes and on pump parts. During drought summers, spreading out heavy irrigation and laundry across the day eases strain on a fractured-rock well that recharges slowly. Finally, watch for early warning signs such as spitting faucets, longer pump run times, or a creeping rise in your electric bill, all of which can flag a developing problem while it is still small and inexpensive to fix.
