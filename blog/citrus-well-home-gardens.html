Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Citrus Well Service for Home Gardens in the Heart of Corona's Citrus Country

Few places in California have deeper citrus roots. Corona was founded at the height of the Southern California citrus boom in the late 1880s, built its first lemon packing house in 1891, and by the mid-twentieth century was known as the "Lemon Capital of the World," shipping thousands of carloads of fruit every season. Home Gardens grew up alongside that industry, and the warm days, mild winters, and well-drained valley soils that made Corona's lemons famous still make this an excellent place to grow citrus today. Understanding Citrus Irrigation Demand

Citrus is a thirsty, year-round crop. A mature grove in the Corona and Temescal Valley area typically consumes roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, and the great majority of that is drawn down during the hot months from late spring into fall. For a working grove, that translates into a sustained need for a meaningful number of gallons per minute through the peak irrigation season, day after day, with no tolerance for a system that cannot keep up.

Frost protection adds a second, very different demand. On the clear, still nights when cold settles into the low ground near the river, sprinkler frost protection can call for roughly 50 or more gallons per minute per acre, running all at once for hours before sunrise. The lemon growers who built Corona's reputation understood frost intimately, and the same physics applies to your grove. A well that comfortably handles summer irrigation can still come up short on a freezing night if it has to drive frost sprinklers, so we plan capacity for both.

Designing Around Your Irrigation Method

Most modern Home Gardens groves use drip lines or micro-sprinklers, which deliver water straight to the root zone and use it efficiently. That efficiency depends on steady pressure and clean water, so we size the well and pump to your peak demand and protect the system with proper filtration.

Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage

A citrus grove asks more of a well than a house ever will. We design three components to work as one system:

  • Well yield: Your well must sustain peak flow without pulling the water level below the pump. We confirm real capacity with a pumping test, not assumptions.
  • High-capacity pump: Producing groves are usually served by a high-capacity submersible pump, or a vertical turbine where volumes are larger. We match the pump to your flow requirement, the depth to water, and the right motor and controls.
  • Storage: A pond or storage tank lets a moderate well fill overnight and then release water at high flow during irrigation or a frost event. Storage is frequently the most economical path to a high peak capacity, and it is often the difference-maker for frost protection.

Where freeze risk is real, we commonly pair a storage pond with a booster pump so the grove can deliver a short, intense burst of water without forcing the well itself to be oversized.

Water Quality Matters as Much as Quantity

Citrus ranks among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, and the chemistry of your well water can shape the health of your trees for years. Three issues deserve particular attention here:

  • Salinity (EC): Higher total salts build up in the root zone and gradually limit how much water trees can take up, dulling growth and yield. Citrus has a lower salinity tolerance than many crops.
  • Chloride: Citrus is especially sensitive to chloride, which collects in the leaves and produces the classic marginal leaf burn and leaf drop that growers dread.
  • Boron: A trace nutrient that turns toxic to citrus just above the level the trees need, with damage often beginning above about 0.5 to 1 part per million. The safe window is narrow.

We test your well water, read the results against the needs of your specific trees, and recommend treatment, blending, or leaching where the numbers call for it. Because every well is different, we keep specific recommendations grounded in your own lab results rather than generic claims.

Drought, Groundwater, and Long-Term Sustainability

Western Riverside County moves through wet years and long droughts, and groundwater is what carries a grove across the dry ones. California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act guides basins toward long-term sustainability, and growers who rely on wells are well served by understanding how their basin is managed and how that might shape pumping over time. The most drought-resilient Home Gardens groves combine efficient irrigation, a healthy well, and enough storage to absorb a hot, dry season. We help you build that resilience into your water system.

Common Citrus Well Problems Near Home Gardens

  • Pumps that cannot keep pace with peak summer irrigation, leaving trees water-stressed.
  • No dedicated frost-protection capacity for cold valley nights.
  • Drip emitters and micro-sprinklers clogged by sediment or mineral scale.
  • Aging wells that lose yield or begin producing sand.
  • Pressure tank and control issues driven by mineral-rich groundwater.
  • Gradual water-chemistry changes that harm trees before the cause is obvious.

Maintenance Keeps Your Legacy Growing

Preventive care is far cheaper than emergency repair. We suggest regular pump and motor inspections, annual water testing, routine cleaning of filters and emitters, and a pumping test every few years to track your well's yield. Spotting a fading pump or a fouling screen early keeps a small fix from becoming a lost harvest.

When to Call a Professional

Reach out when pressure or flow drops, when you see sand or air in the line, when the pump short-cycles or never shuts off, when pump-related power costs climb, or when your water changes in look, smell, or behavior. If you are replanting old lemon ground or putting in new trees, call before planting so the water system is right from the start. And if your grove ever loses water, we provide same-day emergency response.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a Home Gardens citrus grove use?

A mature grove typically needs about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre each year, most of it in the summer, plus high instantaneous flow for frost protection. We size your well and pump to your specific acreage and peak demand.

Does a citrus well need frost-protection capacity here?

If your grove sits in low ground near the river where cold air pools, frost protection can be essential. It demands far more flow than irrigation, often 50 or more gallons per minute per acre, which usually argues for storage and a booster pump.

My lemon trees have burned leaf edges. Is it the water?

Often, yes. Chloride, salinity, or boron in well water can accumulate in citrus leaves and cause marginal burn. A water test identifies the culprit and points to the right treatment.

How deep will my well need to be in Home Gardens?

That depends on your exact parcel and the local water table, so we do not quote a set depth. We evaluate site conditions before recommending a design.

How often should an agricultural well be serviced?

Aim for annual water testing and pump checks, with a pumping test every few years to monitor yield. Consistent maintenance prevents most costly failures.

Can you respond quickly if I lose water?

Yes. We offer same-day emergency service throughout Riverside County so your grove is never left dry during a critical stretch.
