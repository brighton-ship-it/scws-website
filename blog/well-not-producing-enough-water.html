Well Not Producing Enough Water? Here's Why

Updated February 2026 • By SCWS

When your well's water production drops, it's a problem that only gets worse if ignored. Low yield affects water pressure, pump longevity, and your daily life. Understanding the cause helps determine the right solution—and whether you need professional help.

8 Common Causes of Low Well Yield

1. Dropping Water Table

During droughts or periods of heavy local pumping, the aquifer level drops. If your pump was set near the static water level, it may now be drawing air mixed with water. Solution: Lower the pump deeper in the well, or wait for the aquifer to recover.

2. Clogged Well Screen

Over years of use, mineral deposits, bacterial growth, or sediment can clog the screened portion of your well casing. This restricts water flow into the well. Solution: Professional well rehabilitation—chemical treatment, mechanical cleaning, or hydrofracturing.

3. Failing Well Pump

Pump impellers wear down over time, reducing output. A pump that once delivered 10 GPM may now only produce 5 GPM. Other signs include higher power consumption and the pump running longer. Solution: Pump replacement.

4. Biofouling (Iron Bacteria)

Iron bacteria create slimy buildup that clogs well screens, pumps, and pipes. Signs include reddish-brown slime in toilet tanks and sulfur smells. Solution: Shock chlorination and ongoing treatment.

5. Mineral Encrustation

Calcium, iron, and manganese deposits build up on well screens and in the aquifer formation near the well. Solution: Acidizing treatment dissolves mineral buildup.

6. Sediment Accumulation

Sand and sediment can fill the bottom of the well over time, reducing the productive zone. Solution: Professional well cleaning and possible deepening.

7. Damaged Well Casing

Cracked or corroded casing can allow sediment infiltration or reduce well integrity. Solution: Casing liner installation or in some cases, a new well.

8. Aquifer Depletion

In some areas, long-term groundwater extraction has permanently lowered water levels. Solution: Deepening the existing well or drilling a new, deeper well.

Diagnosing Your Low Yield Problem

  1. Check recent changes: Did yield drop suddenly or gradually?
  2. Note when it happens: Low yield all the time, or just during heavy use?
  3. Check pump amp draw: Higher than normal indicates pump working harder.
  4. Measure actual output: Time how long to fill a 5-gallon bucket.
  5. Check water level: Has static water level dropped?

Solutions by Cost (Low to High)

  • Shock chlorination: $200-$500 — Treats bacterial clogging
  • Pump lowering: $500-$1,500 — If water level has dropped
  • Chemical rehabilitation: $1,000-$3,000 — Dissolves deposits
  • Hydrofracturing: $2,000-$5,000 — Opens new fractures in rock
  • Well deepening: $3,000-$10,000 — Access deeper water
  • New well: $15,000-$45,000 — When rehabilitation isn't viable
