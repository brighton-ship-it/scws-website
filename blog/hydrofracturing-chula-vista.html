What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

  • Well runs dry during peak usage

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Chula Vista's East-County Wells

Chula Vista is the second-largest city in San Diego County, stretching from San Diego Bay east toward the foothills below Otay Mountain. Western Chula Vista runs on municipal water from the Sweetwater Authority, and eastern neighborhoods are served by the Otay Water District, so within the developed city most homes are not on wells. The real well country sits on the eastern and southern edges: the Otay Mesa area, the agricultural and nursery parcels along the Otay River corridor, and the rural properties climbing toward Jamul and Otay Mountain. For those owners, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is often the most cost-effective way to restore a fading well without the much larger expense of drilling a new one.

Geology here is varied, and it matters. South Chula Vista mixes marine sedimentary formations such as the Otay and San Diego Formations, alluvial deposits in the Otay River valley, and metavolcanic rock in the hills to the east. Valley-floor wells near the river often find water at moderate depths in alluvium, while hillside wells toward Otay Mountain and Jamul commonly go several hundred feet into fractured rock. Those fractured-rock wells are the textbook candidates for hydrofracturing, while wells screened in loose valley sand are usually better served by other rehabilitation methods, which is exactly why an honest, upfront assessment matters before any work begins.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation process powered entirely by clean, high-pressure water. No chemicals are used, which matters in the Otay Mesa area, where a long history of nurseries and row crops has left nitrate concerns in some shallow groundwater. The sequence is straightforward:

  • Packers isolate a zone. Inflatable packers are set in the casing and inflated to seal off a target section of the well, focusing the pressure where the water-bearing rock is.
  • High-pressure injection. Clean water is pumped into that zone at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000-plus PSI, flushing silt and mineral scale out of existing fractures.
  • Fractures open and connect. The pressure widens fractures and can link the borehole to nearby water-bearing cracks, enlarging the volume of rock feeding the well.
  • Development and testing. Afterward we surge and pump the well to clear loosened material, then run a flow test to confirm the gain.

Why Chula Vista Wells Lose Yield

For the wells on Chula Vista's east and south sides, declining performance usually comes down to a few local causes:

  • Fracture plugging. Hillside wells in fractured metavolcanic and crystalline rock depend on thin fractures that slowly fill with fine sediment.
  • Mineral scaling. Calcium and iron deposits accumulate across the intake and along fracture faces, narrowing the flow path.
  • Sediment in alluvial wells. Valley-floor wells near the Otay River can take in fine sand that packs the screen and the formation around the borehole.
  • Seasonal recharge swings. After dry winters, water levels drop and a marginal well can struggle through late summer before recovering.
  • Aging well construction. Many east-county wells are decades old, and corroded or partially collapsed screens restrict the flow path even when water remains in the formation.

Signs Your Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Several of these together suggest a genuine yield problem rather than an equipment fault:

  • Output that has declined steadily over several years.
  • The well running short during summer irrigation or nursery watering, then recovering overnight.
  • The pump short-cycling more than it used to.
  • Air spitting from faucets when several fixtures or sprinklers run at once.
  • A newer hillside well, drilled deep into rock, that never delivered the gallons-per-minute you expected.
  • A well that can no longer keep up with a nursery or small farm the way it did a few seasons ago.

Before assuming the worst, rule out the cheaper culprits. A failing pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a worn pressure switch can each mimic a low-yield well and cost far less to repair.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Note the timing. A shortage that appears under heavy summer demand and clears in cooler months usually reflects recovery rate, which hydrofracturing targets.
  2. Test the pressure tank. If it feels solid with no air cushion, the bladder or air charge has likely failed, a cheap fix unrelated to the well.
  3. Account for demand. Expanded irrigation, a new greenhouse, or added livestock can outrun a well that was always near its limit.
  4. Watch the water. Sand or cloudiness can mean fines are moving through the formation and collecting at the intake.
  5. Test for nitrates. If your well is shallow and near former agricultural land on Otay Mesa, annual nitrate testing is wise regardless of yield.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure equipment, properly sized packers, and the experience to read a particular formation, so it is not a homeowner task. Contact a licensed well contractor when output has clearly fallen, when a flow test confirms production below your needs, or when you are weighing rehabilitation against the larger cost of a new well, especially on a steep east-county lot. A professional starts with a casing inspection, usually by camera, both to confirm the casing is sound and to judge whether your well draws from fractured rock that will respond. That inspection often distinguishes a true low-yield well from one suffering simple screen plugging or pump trouble.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil and gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water to reopen existing fractures in shallow rock. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no deep shale formations. It is a long-established well-rehabilitation method, completely separate from oil and gas extraction.

Do many Chula Vista homes have private wells?

Within the developed city, most do not, since western Chula Vista is served by the Sweetwater Authority and eastern neighborhoods by the Otay Water District. Private wells are concentrated on the east and south edges, including Otay Mesa, the Otay River corridor, and rural foothill properties toward Jamul.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Chula Vista well?

It depends on your geology. Hillside wells in fractured rock respond well, often improving two to five times, while wells screened purely in loose valley-floor sand may benefit more from other methods. We inspect the casing and formation before recommending the treatment.

How long does the process take?

Most residential jobs finish in a single day. The high-pressure injection itself runs roughly four to eight hours depending on how many zones we treat, and we flow-test afterward, usually confirming results within 24 to 48 hours.

Should I test my water afterward?

Because only clean water is used, the treatment introduces nothing harmful. Given Otay Mesa's agricultural history and known nitrate concerns, we recommend testing your water after any well work as good practice.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well in the Chula Vista area runs $18,000 to $42,000 or more, and a steep east-county lot can complicate siting. For most low-yield wells it is the smart first step.
