How a Pressure Tank Works

Your pressure tank is essentially an air spring wrapped in steel. Inside is a rubber bladder holding water, surrounded by a charge of compressed air. When the submersible pump runs, it forces water into the bladder and compresses that air; when you open a faucet, the air pushes the water back out through your plumbing without the pump cycling. The pump only restarts when pressure drops to its cut-in point — typically 40 PSI — and stops at cut-out, around 60 PSI. The usable water delivered in between is the tank’s drawdown, and it is what keeps your pump from firing every single time you draw water.

That air charge, the pre-charge, is set about 2 PSI below cut-in. When it leaks away over the years, or the bladder splits and water floods the air pocket, the tank can no longer store pressure — and the system starts to misbehave.

Waterlogged Tanks and Short-Cycling

A waterlogged tank is one whose air cushion is gone, filled instead with water. With almost no drawdown left, the pump short-cycles — snapping on and off every few seconds. It is the single most common pressure-tank failure we see in Wildomar, and it is brutal on pumps: every restart hits the motor with a surge of current, and a tank left this way can wear out a good pump in a season. Because Elsinore Valley wells sit in alluvial ground that can carry fine sediment, that grit also chews at bladders and switches, hastening the failure.

You can check it yourself. Turn off the pump breaker, open a faucet to bleed pressure, and press the Schrader air valve on top of the tank. Air is normal; water means the bladder has ruptured and the tank must be replaced. Rap on the shell too — if it rings solid all the way up instead of sounding hollow near the top, the air is gone.

Signs Your Wildomar Pressure Tank Is Failing

  • Rapid short-cycling whenever a tap is open
  • Pressure that surges then sags mid-shower
  • Water hammer — pipes banging as valves close
  • Faucets spitting air along with water
  • Rust or damp spots at the tank seams or base
  • Cloudy or sediment-laden water that fouls the switch and bladder

Sizing and Drawdown for Elsinore Valley Homes

The right tank size gives your pump a run time of at least a minute per cycle, which means matching drawdown to the pump’s flow rate rather than to bathroom count. Typical guidance for Wildomar-area properties:

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: a 20-32 gallon tank suits most 5-10 GPM pumps
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: a 44-86 gallon tank handles higher simultaneous demand and 10-20 GPM pumps
  • Rural and ranch lots toward the hills and Bundy Canyon: 86-120 gallon tanks or paired tanks for irrigation

DWR records show Wildomar with 222 wells on record and an average depth around 286 feet (ranging from about 15 to 950 feet) — consistent with the thick alluvial deposits of the Elsinore Valley. Those moderate depths still call for a well-matched tank; undersize it and even a healthy bladder will short-cycle and shorten the pump’s life.

Checking the Air Pre-Charge

Test your pre-charge yearly. Shut off the pump, open the lowest faucet until water stops flowing, and read the Schrader valve with a tire gauge — it should be 2 PSI below cut-in, roughly 38 PSI on a 40/60 system. Add air in short bursts if it is low, then re-check. If it will not hold, the tank is leaking through the valve core or a torn bladder and needs service. Given the sediment common in Elsinore Valley wells, we also suggest flushing the tank periodically so grit does not accelerate wear.

Diagnosis and When to Call a Professional

Short-cycling can come from more than a bad tank, so we test the whole system before recommending anything. On a Wildomar call we check the pressure switch and its port, verify cut-in and cut-out, read the pre-charge, inspect the bladder, and confirm the pump is still making rated flow — a fading pump can look just like a failing tank. Homeowners can safely check the pre-charge and tap the shell. Call a licensed C-57 contractor when water sprays from the air valve, when pressure never builds, when the switch smells hot or arcs, or when the tank shows rust. Repair or Replace? A Straight Answer for Wildomar

Before we ever recommend a new tank, we rule out the cheaper fixes. A failing pressure switch — one that chatters, sticks, or never reaches cut-out — runs $150-350 and is frequently the true cause of pressure that will not hold. A bad Schrader valve core can leak the air charge and be swapped in minutes. Replacement becomes necessary only when the bladder itself ruptures, at which point the tank can no longer store air and no repair will save it. For Wildomar homes, we typically advise replacing a bladder tank that has failed after a decade of service, since a sound tank shields the much costlier submersible pump from short-cycling. We always show you which parts are repairable and which are not before we start.

Because Elsinore Valley wells sit in alluvial ground that can carry fine sediment, we pay close attention to water clarity when replacing a tank. Grit accelerates bladder wear and clogs pressure switches, so on sediment-prone wells we often pair a new tank with filtration at $300-900 to protect your investment and keep the system running smoothly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won’t my well pump stop running?

If the pump never rests or cycles rapidly, the pressure tank has usually lost its air charge or the bladder has torn, leaving no drawdown. A sticking pressure switch or bad check valve can do it too. Either way it is worth diagnosing quickly, because constant running destroys pumps.

What air pressure should be in my pressure tank?

Set the pre-charge 2 PSI below cut-in — about 38 PSI on a typical 40/60 system — measured with the pump off and the tank drained. If it keeps dropping after you refill it, the tank is leaking and should be serviced.

How do I know if the bladder is ruptured?

Press the Schrader air valve on top of the tank with the pump off and pressure released. Air means the bladder is intact; a spray of water means it has torn, and the tank needs to be replaced rather than repaired.

Does sediment in my well water hurt the pressure tank?

It can. Fine sediment common in Elsinore Valley alluvial wells wears the bladder and clogs the pressure switch over time. A sediment filter and periodic tank flush protect both the tank and the pump.

How long do pressure tanks last in Wildomar?

Ten to fifteen years is typical for a quality bladder tank, though sediment and hard water can shorten it. An annual pre-charge check and good filtration are the best ways to reach the top of that range.
