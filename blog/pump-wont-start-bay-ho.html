Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

This guide explains how a well system works, why coastal-mesa pumps in San Diego County fail, what you can check safely, and when to call a licensed C-57 contractor.

The Parts That Have to Work Together

Every time you open a faucet, a sequence of components has to fire in order. If any one fails, the pump won't start:

  • Power supply: A dedicated 240-volt circuit from your panel, usually on a double-pole breaker.
  • Pressure switch: Reads tank pressure and tells the pump when to run. Its exposed contacts are vulnerable to coastal corrosion.
  • Control box and capacitor: Three-wire submersibles use a surface box with a start capacitor and relay; two-wire pumps house the capacitor in the motor.
  • Pressure tank: Stores pressurized water so the pump cycles less often.
  • Motor and drop cable: The submersible motor sits down the borehole, fed by a cable that runs the full depth.
  • Well and water level: If the level falls below the intake, protective controls shut the motor down.

Why Bay Ho Pumps Stop Starting

The defining factor in Bay Ho is the marine layer. Salt-laden humidity rolling off Mission Bay corrodes anything metal and exposed. The failures we see most often near the coast are:

  • Corroded pressure-switch contacts: The number-one coastal culprit. Pitted, oxidized contacts can't pass enough current to start the pump.
  • Oxidized wiring and wire nuts: Surface connections at the wellhead and control box degrade faster in salt air, causing intermittent or total no-starts.
  • Tripped breaker: Often the first symptom, sometimes the result of a deeper short.
  • Failed start capacitor: A worn capacitor leaves the motor humming but unable to spin up.
  • Motor burnout: After 10-15 years, windings fail and trip the breaker on startup.
  • Stuck check valve or low water: Less common on the relatively reliable coastal mesa aquifer, but possible.

Safe Checks for Bay Ho Homeowners

  1. Reset the breaker once. Find the double-pole pump breaker and reset it a single time. If it trips again, stop.
  2. Read the gauge. A pressure reading stuck at zero means the pump likely never ran.
  3. Inspect for corrosion. With the power off, look for green or white crust on the pressure switch terminals and wellhead connections, a classic coastal sign.
  4. Listen for a click and hum. A click at the switch followed by a hum and silence suggests a start-component or contact problem.
  5. Check the rest of the circuit. Confirm other 240-volt loads work so you know it's the pump and not the panel.

Caution: Do not open energized equipment, and never repeatedly reset a tripping breaker. Salt-corroded wiring plus 240 volts is a real shock and fire hazard.

How Coastal Air Quietly Destroys Pump Electronics

It is worth dwelling on just how aggressive the marine environment is in Bay Ho. Salt particles suspended in the morning fog settle on every exposed metal surface and draw moisture out of the air, creating a thin, conductive film. On a pressure switch, that film causes the contacts to arc and pit each time they open and close. Over months, the pitting builds into an insulating crust that prevents the switch from passing enough current to energize the pump. The same process attacks wire nuts, terminal screws, and the inside of control-box covers that aren't sealed tightly. This is why a pump that ran perfectly for years can suddenly refuse to start after a damp, foggy stretch: the corrosion finally crossed a threshold.

Because the failure is gradual, the early warning signs are subtle: an occasional delay before the pump kicks on, a faint buzzing at the switch, or pressure that wanders outside its normal range. Catching these signs early lets us replace a $200 switch before the same corrosion damages the more expensive control box or stresses the motor.

Choosing Corrosion-Resistant Replacement Parts

When we service a Bay Ho well, we don't simply install whatever standard part is on the truck. For coastal installations we favor pressure switches with sealed or coated contacts, stainless or marine-grade hardware at the wellhead, and weather-tight control-box enclosures. We also dielectric-grease the connections and make sure covers seal completely against the marine layer. These small choices dramatically slow the corrosion clock and spare you a repeat failure next foggy season.

Because Bay Ho's failures are driven by slow, predictable corrosion, they are unusually preventable. Why won't my well pump start in Bay Ho?

Can I reset my well pump breaker myself?

You may reset a tripped breaker one time. If it trips again right away, stop and call a professional. Repeated tripping indicates a short or a grounded motor and is a fire hazard.
