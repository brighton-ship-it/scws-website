What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Redlands Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Redlands: An Honest Look at Whether It Fits

Redlands is a historic city in San Bernardino County, set among the citrus groves and tree-lined neighborhoods between the Santa Ana River and the San Bernardino Mountains, with the Crafton Hills and Yucaipa to the east and the San Timoteo badlands to the south. For most Redlands homeowners the honest answer is straightforward: hydrofracturing is usually not the right tool here. The city's water comes largely from a managed municipal system drawing on local alluvial groundwater basins, and the geology beneath most of the valley is sand and gravel rather than the fractured bedrock hydrofracturing needs. This page explains why, and points you toward what actually helps.

What Hydrofracturing Is For

Hydrofracturing is a yield-enhancement technique for hard-rock wells. A contractor isolates a section of a well's borehole with inflatable packers, then injects clean water at high pressure, commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. In solid fractured crystalline or granitic rock, the pressure reopens and flushes the natural cracks that feed the well, boosting flow. It uses only water and is genuinely effective, but only where there are rigid fractures in bedrock to open. That requirement is the whole story for Redlands.

Why Most of Redlands Is a Weak Fit

The Redlands area sits over alluvial groundwater basins, the Bunker Hill and Redlands sub-basins among them, filled with the sand and gravel washed down from the surrounding mountains. Local water districts pump that groundwater from wells screened in the alluvium, disinfect it, and distribute it through the municipal system that serves most of the city. Two consequences follow for hydrofracturing. First, the great majority of Redlands homes are on municipal water and have no private well to treat. Second, the wells that do exist in the valley are typically completed in sand and gravel, not in fractured rock, and hydrofracturing cannot improve a sand-and-gravel well, because there are no rigid fractures to open. High-pressure water injected into loose alluvium simply dissipates.

So for the typical Redlands property, hydrofracturing is a poor match on both counts. We say so plainly rather than sell a procedure the geology cannot support.

What Usually Helps a Redlands Well Instead

  • Screen and well rehabilitation. Alluvial wells lose yield as the screen and gravel pack clog with fines and mineral scale. Redeveloping and cleaning the screen, not fracturing rock, restores capacity.
  • Pump and pressure-system repair. A drop in flow or pressure is often a worn pump, a failing pressure switch, or a waterlogged tank rather than the aquifer.
  • Well deepening or a new well. If groundwater levels have dropped in your sub-basin, deepening or drilling a new, properly screened alluvial well is the dependable fix.
  • Water treatment. For hardness or quality concerns in local groundwater, a treatment system targets the problem directly.

Where Hydrofracturing Could Apply Near Redlands

There is a real but limited exception. Toward the higher ground, the Crafton Hills, the Mentone foothills, and the mountain front above the valley, some properties have wells that reach fractured crystalline bedrock rather than valley alluvium. A well that genuinely bottoms in hard fractured rock is a legitimate candidate for hydrofracturing. The deciding document is always your well completion report. If it shows fractured rock, the technique is worth considering; if it shows sand and gravel, it will not help, and we will tell you so and recommend a better-suited fix.

  2. Review the well log. We check whether the well is screened in alluvium or completed in fractured rock.
  3. Separate the symptoms. Equipment, screen clogging, basin decline, or water quality, each calls for a different remedy.
  4. Recommend honestly. In Redlands that usually means a rehabilitation, a pump repair, or treatment, and only occasionally hydrofracturing.

Realistic Expectations

If you were hoping hydrofracturing would restore a Redlands well, the honest expectation is that it usually will not, because the valley's alluvial geology and municipal water service do not support it. That is generally good news, because the right fix is often simpler and cheaper. Where hydrofracturing is appropriate, on genuine fractured-rock wells such as those on the higher ground east and north of the city, it typically delivers two to five times the prior flow rate with success in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases. We will not imply those numbers apply to a sand-and-gravel basin, because they do not.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Are you on municipal water or a private well?
  • Your well completion report, showing whether the well is in alluvium or rock.
  • Is the issue flow, pressure, or quality? Each points to a different remedy.
  • Sudden or gradual change? Sudden usually means equipment; gradual can mean a clogged screen or a basin decline.

When to Call a Professional

The most valuable thing a licensed contractor offers a Redlands homeowner is an accurate diagnosis, which here often means explaining why hydrofracturing is not the answer and directing you to the repair that is. High-pressure well work is hazardous and is never a DIY task. Beyond safety, spending several thousand dollars to fracture alluvial ground would simply waste your money, and an honest contractor will tell you that before you commit.

Redlands Water in Context

It is worth understanding why Redlands developed its water system the way it did. The city grew up around citrus agriculture, and from the start its water came from the productive alluvial basins fed by the Santa Ana River and mountain runoff. Over more than a century, those basins were developed, managed, and connected into the municipal supply that now reaches most homes. The result is a community where a private domestic well is the exception rather than the rule, and where the few wells in service usually tap the same sand-and-gravel aquifer the city does. That history is the practical reason hydrofracturing rarely comes up here: the resource that makes Redlands water reliable, a large alluvial groundwater store, is exactly the kind of formation hydrofracturing cannot improve. Knowing where your property sits in that picture, fully municipal, valley well, or a foothill well in harder rock, is the first thing we establish, because it determines the right service and tells us immediately whether hydrofracturing belongs in the conversation at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hydrofracturing fix my Redlands well?

Usually not. Most Redlands homes are on municipal water, and local wells are typically completed in sand-and-gravel alluvium, which hydrofracturing cannot improve. We confirm your situation before recommending anything.

My well yield dropped. What is the likely cause?

In an alluvial setting like the Redlands valley, it is usually a clogged screen, a worn pump, or a declining basin level, none of which fracturing addresses. Screen rehabilitation, a pump repair, or deepening is the usual remedy.

Is there any Redlands-area well that could be a candidate?

Yes, mainly on the higher ground toward the Crafton Hills, Mentone, and the mountain front, where a well may bottom in fractured rock. Your well completion report tells us whether that applies.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. It uses only clean water under pressure and is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking despite the shared name.

My water is hard or tastes off. Can hydrofracturing help?

No. That is a water-quality issue best handled by a treatment system. Hydrofracturing only addresses water quantity, and only in hard-rock wells.

Will you charge me if hydrofracturing turns out to be wrong for my property?

The diagnostic is $125 and is credited toward whatever work you choose. An honest "you do not need hydrofracturing" still moves you toward the correct, usually cheaper, repair.
