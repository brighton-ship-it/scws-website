Understanding the Pressure Tank in Your Well System

A pressure tank is a buffer between your well pump and your plumbing. Rather than starting the pump every time you turn on a tap, the tank stores a reserve of water held under pressure by a cushion of compressed air. In a bladder-style tank — the type we install on Highland systems — a reinforced rubber diaphragm keeps the water and air separated. Water entering the tank compresses the air; when you open a fixture, that air pushes the water out to your home.

Two numbers govern the system. The pressure switch sets a cut-in and cut-out point, most often 30/50 or 40/60 PSI, and the pump runs only between them. The drawdown is the amount of usable water delivered between those points, and it depends heavily on the tank's air pre-charge. Set the pre-charge roughly 2 PSI below the cut-in pressure and the tank gives you its full drawdown with long, gentle pump cycles. Lose that air — through a slow leak or a failed bladder — and the whole system starts to struggle.

Waterlogged Tanks and Ruptured Bladders

The failure we're called out for most often in Highland is a waterlogged pressure tank. As a tank ages, its air pre-charge gradually escapes or the bladder splits, and water floods the space that should hold air. With no cushion left, the tank stores almost no usable water, and the pump is forced to short cycle — snapping on and off every few seconds. That rapid cycling is the single fastest way to destroy a well pump, because every restart draws a large surge of current that heats and wears the motor. Left alone, a $600–$1,500 tank problem can escalate into a $2,500–$5,500 pump replacement.

Warning Signs to Watch For

  • Pump short cycling — the number-one indicator of a waterlogged tank
  • Pressure that spikes and then sags mid-shower
  • Water escaping the air valve when you depress the Schrader valve on top
  • A tank with no hollow, air-filled sound near the top when tapped
  • Rust or moisture around the base, seams, or fittings
  • Banging pipes (water hammer) as fixtures close

How We Diagnose a Failing Tank

A correct diagnosis saves you money. On a service call, we power down the pump, bleed the system to zero pressure through an open faucet, and read the air pre-charge at the Schrader valve with a calibrated gauge. Water at the valve means a torn bladder and a tank that must be replaced. A low but stable pre-charge on an intact bladder can often be corrected by re-charging with a compressor. We also test the pressure switch settings and inspect the contacts, since a struggling tank frequently masks a worn switch. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited back when you have us perform the repair.

What You Can Check Before Calling

You can gather useful clues safely. Watch your pressure gauge while running one fixture — a pump that cycles more than once during a single draw is a strong sign of waterlogging. Rock or tap the tank to feel whether there's an air space up top. Do not attempt to add air while the system is pressurized or the pump is energized. If the readings look off or the pump won't build pressure at all, that's the point to bring in a professional rather than guessing.

Sizing a Replacement Tank for Highland Homes

Because Highland properties range from compact valley lots to larger foothill parcels with irrigation, tank sizing isn't one-size-fits-all. We size to your pump's real flow rate and your household's peak simultaneous demand, aiming for pump run cycles of one to two minutes to protect the motor. A modest 1-2 bathroom home is often well served by a 20-32 gallon tank; a 3-4 bathroom home usually needs 44-86 gallons; and estates or ranch properties with heavy irrigation may call for 86-120 gallons or multiple tanks. We match the tank to the system and precisely set the pre-charge on installation.

Repair or Replace? Making the Right Call

Not every pressure tank problem means buying a new tank, and a good technician will tell you the difference honestly. If your Highland tank simply has a low air pre-charge but a sound bladder, re-charging it with a compressor and re-checking the drawdown may return it to full service for the cost of a service call. The same is true when the real culprit turns out to be a worn pressure switch or a clogged snifter valve rather than the tank itself. On the other hand, once the internal bladder has split — the failure behind most waterlogging — there is no patch or repair; the tank has to be replaced. Age matters too: a tank already past the ten-year mark that has waterlogged is usually worth replacing outright rather than nursing along, because the next failure is rarely far behind. We walk you through the options and quote both paths so you can decide with full information.

Keeping Your Tank Healthy Longer

A little attention goes a long way toward reaching the upper end of a tank's lifespan. Once a year, with the pump off and the system depressurized, check the air pre-charge and top it off to about 2 PSI below cut-in. Keep an eye on the pressure gauge during everyday use and note any change in how often the pump cycles — a gradual increase is an early warning that pre-charge is bleeding off. In Highland's foothill water, adding sediment filtration ahead of the system can reduce the scale and grit that shorten bladder life. And if you're already replacing a pump or switch, it's often smart to inspect the tank at the same time while the system is open.

Part of a Complete Well System

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well pump short cycling in my Highland home?

Short cycling — the pump turning on and off every few seconds — is the classic symptom of a waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air cushion. It can also stem from a leaking check valve or a faulty pressure switch. Because rapid cycling wears out pumps fast, have it checked promptly.

How do I test whether my pressure tank bladder has failed?

With the pump off and the system bled to zero pressure, press the Schrader air valve on top of the tank. If water comes out instead of air, the bladder has ruptured and the tank needs replacement. No air pressure at all points the same direction.

What air pressure should my tank be pre-charged to?

Set the pre-charge about 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in point, with the tank drained of water. A 30/50 switch calls for roughly 28 PSI; a 40/60 switch about 38 PSI. Correct pre-charge maximizes drawdown and protects the pump.

How much does pressure tank replacement cost in Highland?

A typical bladder pressure tank replacement runs $600–$1,500 depending on size and fittings. A pressure switch alone is $150–$350. We provide upfront quotes and credit the $125 diagnostic toward any repair.

How long should a pressure tank last near the San Bernardino Mountains?

Quality bladder tanks generally last 10 to 15 years. Mineral content in foothill well water can shorten that, while a correctly set pre-charge and occasional inspection help you reach the upper end.
