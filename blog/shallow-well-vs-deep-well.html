Shallow Well vs Deep Well: Complete Comparison

Wondering about well depth? Whether you're planning a new well, troubleshooting an existing one, or evaluating a property with a well, understanding the difference between shallow and deep wells is essential. Depth affects everything from water quality and reliability to pump type and maintenance needs. Here's what every well owner should know.

⚡ Quick Answer

Deep wells (100+ feet) are generally better—they provide more reliable water supply, better natural water quality, and are less affected by drought. Shallow wells (under 50 feet) cost less but are more vulnerable to contamination and seasonal variations. In most of San Diego County, geology requires deep wells regardless of preference.

Defining Shallow vs Deep Wells

"Shallow" and "deep" aren't just casual descriptions—they refer to how wells access different underground water sources and the technologies required to bring that water to the surface.

Shallow Wells: Accessing the Water Table

Shallow wells tap into the water table—the upper surface of groundwater that fills spaces between soil particles and rock. Key characteristics:

  • Typical depth: Under 50 feet, often 15-30 feet
  • Water source: Unconfined aquifer (water table)
  • Recharge: Directly from rainfall and surface water infiltration
  • Fluctuation: Significant seasonal and drought-related changes
  • Construction: Can be dug, driven, or drilled

Deep Wells: Accessing Confined Aquifers

Deep wells penetrate through impermeable layers to reach confined aquifers—water trapped between rock layers under pressure. Key characteristics:

  • Typical depth: 100-400+ feet (sometimes 800+ feet)
  • Water source: Confined or semi-confined aquifer
  • Recharge: Slow, often from miles away over centuries
  • Fluctuation: Minimal seasonal variation, more stable long-term
  • Construction: Must be drilled with professional equipment

📍 San Diego County Context

Most of San Diego County's inland areas require wells 150-400+ feet deep due to our geology and deep water tables. True shallow wells are rare here except in a few valley-floor and riparian areas. See our guide on well depths throughout San Diego County for location-specific information.

Water Quality Differences

The depth your water comes from significantly affects its quality—though not always in ways you might expect.

Shallow Well Water Quality

Shallow well water comes from recently infiltrated surface water, which has both benefits and risks:

Potential Benefits

  • • Often lower mineral content (softer water)
  • • Less iron and manganese in some areas
  • • May have better taste initially

Common Concerns

  • • Higher bacteria contamination risk
  • • Susceptible to nitrate contamination
  • • Vulnerable to surface pollutants
  • • Pesticides and fertilizer runoff
  • • Septic system influence possible

Deep Well Water Quality

Deep well water has been naturally filtered through rock and soil for years to centuries:

Typical Benefits

  • • Naturally filtered (clearer)
  • • Much lower bacteria risk
  • • Protected from surface contamination
  • • Consistent quality year-round
  • • Stable temperature

Potential Concerns

  • • Higher mineral content (hard water)
  • • May have iron or manganese
  • • Possible hydrogen sulfide (sulfur smell)
  • • Some areas have naturally occurring arsenic

For more on water quality issues, see our guides on sulfur smell in well water and treating hard water.

Reliability and Drought Performance

In California's climate of droughts and wet cycles, reliability is a critical consideration.

Shallow Wells and Drought

Shallow wells are directly connected to recent rainfall patterns. Problems include:

  • Seasonal fluctuation: Water levels can drop 10-20+ feet between wet and dry seasons
  • Drought vulnerability: May run dry completely during extended dry periods
  • Competition effects: Nearby shallow wells or irrigation pumping lowers shared water table
  • Recovery time: Can take months to years to recover after drought

If you're experiencing well running out of water or slow well recovery, a shallow well tapping a stressed water table may be the cause.

Deep Wells and Drought

Deep wells accessing confined aquifers are much more stable:

  • Minimal seasonal change: Often just 1-5 feet of variation
  • Drought resilience: Confined aquifers take years to decades to show drought effects
  • Less competition: Fewer wells tap deep aquifers; each affects others less
  • Pressure maintenance: Artesian pressure keeps water available

💡 California Reality Check

During the 2012-2016 drought, thousands of shallow wells across California ran dry. Deep wells generally continued producing. Many property owners with failed shallow wells were forced to drill deeper replacements—at drought-inflated prices. A properly deep well initially costs more but provides genuine water security.

Pump and Equipment Differences

Well depth directly determines what type of pump system you need—and this affects efficiency, maintenance, and costs.

Shallow Well Pumps

For wells where water is within 25 feet of the surface:

  • Jet pumps: Installed above ground, use suction to pull water
  • Easier access: Maintenance and repairs are straightforward
  • Lower cost: Pump and installation typically $800-$1,500
  • Shorter lifespan: Usually 10-15 years
  • Lower efficiency: More energy per gallon pumped

Deep Well Pumps

For wells deeper than 25 feet to water:

  • Submersible pumps: Installed inside the well, push water up
  • Difficult access: Requires pulling pump for service
  • Higher cost: Pump and installation typically $2,000-$4,000
  • Longer lifespan: Usually 15-25 years
  • Higher efficiency: Less energy per gallon pumped

For a detailed comparison, see our jet pump vs submersible pump guide.

Long-Term Cost Considerations

Cheaper isn't always better. Factor in:

  • Water treatment: Shallow wells often need more treatment ($2,000-$5,000)
  • Pump replacement: Jet pumps replaced more often than submersibles
  • Deepening costs: If shallow well fails, drilling deeper costs $10,000-$20,000+
  • Property value: Deep wells with proven yield are worth more
  • Water hauling: If well fails during drought, hauled water costs $200-$400/month

💰 Real Cost Analysis

A shallow well at $8,000 that requires deepening after 10 years costs more than a deep well at $25,000 that runs reliably for 40 years. Always consider the full lifecycle cost and reliability, not just initial investment.

Maintenance Requirements

Shallow Well Maintenance

  • Annual water quality testing (bacteria, nitrates) recommended
  • More frequent pump priming issues
  • Periodic shock chlorination often needed
  • Wellhead inspection critical (contamination prevention)
  • May need seasonal adjustment of pump depth

Deep Well Maintenance

  • Water testing every 3-5 years typically sufficient
  • Pump service less frequent but requires professional pulling
  • Wellhead inspection important
  • Casing integrity checks every 10-15 years
  • Generally lower maintenance burden overall

Making the Right Choice

In most cases, you don't actually "choose" between shallow and deep—you drill to where adequate water exists. However, here's when each makes sense:

⚠️ When a Shallow Well Might Work:

  • • Your area has a documented high water table
  • • Neighbors have successful shallow wells
  • • You're near a stream or in a valley floor
  • • Hydrogeological survey confirms shallow water
  • • You have backup water source if it runs dry

✅ When to Drill Deep:

  • • You need reliable year-round water supply
  • • Your area is known for deep water tables
  • • Neighbors have deep wells
  • • You want better natural water quality
  • • Long-term reliability is priority over initial cost
  • • You're in most of San Diego County (reality)

Frequently Asked Questions

What depth defines a shallow well vs a deep well?

Generally, shallow wells are under 50 feet deep and tap into the water table near the surface. Deep wells are typically 100+ feet and access confined aquifers. The 25-foot mark is also significant—that's the practical limit for suction-based (jet) pumps, requiring submersible pumps for deeper wells.

Is water quality better from deep wells or shallow wells?

Deep wells typically have better natural water quality because the water has been filtered through more rock and soil, and they're less susceptible to surface contamination. However, deep wells can have more mineral content (hardness, iron, manganese). Shallow wells are more vulnerable to bacteria, nitrates, and surface pollutants.

How much does it cost to drill a shallow vs deep well?

In San Diego County, drilling costs $35-$85 per foot depending on geology. A 50-foot shallow well might cost $5,000-$8,000 total, while a 300-foot deep well can run $20,000-$35,000+. However, shallow wells aren't always an option—the depth is determined by where water exists, not budget preference.

Can I choose whether to drill a shallow or deep well?

Not really—you drill to where the water is. A hydrogeologist or experienced driller assesses your property's geology to estimate required depth. In most of San Diego County, adequate water requires drilling 150-400+ feet. Shallow wells are only possible where the water table is naturally high.

Are shallow wells more likely to run dry?

Yes. Shallow wells tap the water table, which fluctuates seasonally and during droughts. Deep wells access confined aquifers that are more stable and less affected by surface conditions. In California's drought cycles, shallow wells frequently run dry while deep wells maintain production.

What type of pump do I need for each well type?

Shallow wells (under 25 feet to water) can use jet pumps installed above ground. Deep wells require submersible pumps installed inside the well. For depths between 25-70 feet, deep well jet pumps may work but submersibles are generally preferred for better efficiency and reliability.

Which well type is better for long-term reliability?

Deep wells are generally more reliable long-term. They're less affected by drought, seasonal changes, and surface contamination. While they cost more initially, they typically provide consistent water supply for decades. Shallow wells may require deepening or replacement as water tables drop.

Do shallow wells require more maintenance?

Often yes. Shallow wells are more prone to bacterial contamination (requiring periodic chlorination), more likely to need water treatment systems, and more susceptible to sediment issues. They may also require more frequent pump work since jet pumps typically have shorter lifespans than submersibles.
