How a Well Pressure System Works

A private well is a simple chain, and low pressure almost always comes from one weak link. A submersible pump in the borehole lifts water to the surface and into a pressure tank, where compressed air over a bladder stores energy and delivers steady flow without the pump cycling constantly. A pressure switch starts and stops the pump at set cut-in and cut-out points, normally keeping household pressure between 40 and 60 PSI. When the tank, switch, pump, or supply line drifts out of spec, you feel it immediately at the tap.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Rolando-Area Wells

With more than 30 years serving San Diego County, we repeatedly trace weak pressure to a short list of causes in this coastal-plain terrain of decomposed granite and sedimentary deposits:

  • Waterlogged pressure tank — When the air charge is lost or the bladder ruptures, water fills the tank, the pump short-cycles, and pressure swings. This is one of our most common findings.
  • Failing pressure switch — Corroded contacts or a drifting spring setting make the switch cut out early, holding pressure chronically low.
  • Clogged sediment — Decomposed granite and fine sedimentary particles common to the San Diego coastal plain lodge in screens, foot valves, and filters, restricting flow.
  • Dropping water table — Shallow coastal-plain aquifers, already limited by paved urban surfaces that block recharge, can draw down during San Diego's dry cycles and reduce well output.
  • Worn pump — Many wells serving older Rolando-era and back-country properties run pumps installed decades ago that have simply worn out.
  • Leaks and closed valves — Aging, mid-century-vintage galvanized pipe corrodes and cracks, and a hidden leak or a partly closed valve drains pressure before it reaches the house.

What You Can Check Yourself First

A few safe checks help narrow things down before we arrive:

  • Whole house or one fixture? A single weak faucet usually means a clogged aerator, not the well.
  • Check the pressure gauge. A steady 40–60 PSI is normal; a low or rapidly bouncing needle points to the tank or switch.
  • Confirm valves are open. Follow the line from wellhead to house and make sure every shutoff is wide open.
  • Tap the pressure tank. Hollow near the top and solid near the bottom is healthy; solid all the way up means it is waterlogged.
  • Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking (short-cycling) or a pump running nonstop without building pressure both signal a real problem.

Avoid opening the tank, rewiring the switch, or pulling the pump yourself — these involve electrical and pressurized hazards for a licensed technician.

Preventing Low Pressure Before It Starts

Simple maintenance keeps a Rolando-area well dependable. Check the pressure tank's air charge once a year with a tire gauge, targeting roughly 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in setting. Flush an outdoor spigot at full flow every few months to clear sediment from the lines. Because older systems are prone to corrosion, inspect visible galvanized piping and the wellhead each season for leaks or rust. Replace sediment filter cartridges on schedule, and book a professional inspection every couple of years so worn pump parts and drifting switch settings are caught before they fail. A small investment in prevention beats an emergency call every time.

Frequently Asked Questions About Low Water Pressure in Rolando

Is my low water pressure coming from the well or the old plumbing in my house?

Both are possible in a neighborhood as established as Rolando. If pressure is weak at every fixture, the cause is usually the well's tank, switch, or pump. If it is isolated to certain fixtures or old galvanized branches, corroded interior piping may be the culprit. Our $125 diagnostic pinpoints exactly where pressure is being lost.

Why would a shallow coastal-plain well lose pressure in summer?

The shallow aquifers under San Diego's coastal plain get limited natural recharge because so much surface area is paved, and they draw down during the region's dry cycles. When the level falls near the pump, output drops. We can test your well's yield and recommend filtration, a pump adjustment, or storage to bridge dry months.

Could sediment be causing both my cloudy water and weak pressure?

Frequently, yes. Decomposed granite and fine sediment common to the area lodge in screens, foot valves, and filters, which both cloud the water and restrict flow. A sediment filtration setup ($300–$900) usually addresses both at once.
