Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • High-capacity agricultural wells
  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Ocean Beach well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Honest Well Service Guidance for Ocean Beach, San Diego

Ocean Beach is one of San Diego's most beloved coastal neighborhoods, a dense, walkable community wedged between the Pacific surf and the San Diego River mouth in the city's western corner. It is not, by any stretch, farm country. There are no commercial avocado groves in Ocean Beach, and the vast majority of homes and businesses here are connected to the City of San Diego's municipal water system rather than to private wells. We think the most useful thing we can do for an OB property owner searching for "avocado well services" is to be straight with you about what is and is not realistic on a coastal city lot, and where a well actually makes sense in this part of the county.

That said, water still matters here, and there are legitimate reasons a San Diego coastal property owner reaches out to a well contractor. Some larger lots in the surrounding peninsula and river-valley areas have legacy wells. Some owners are interested in non-potable irrigation supply for landscaping to offset rising city water costs. And many people who own a beach home in OB also own rural acreage in San Diego County's backcountry, where wells are the norm. Southern California Well Service, a licensed C-57 contractor with more than 30 years in the region, can advise on all of these.

Why Avocado Groves Belong Inland, Not at the Beach

Avocados are a genuine San Diego County crop, but they thrive on the cooler, frost-protected inland slopes around Fallbrook, Valley Center, Pauma Valley, and the backcountry hills, not on a salt-sprayed coastal lot. Ocean Beach lots are small, soils are sandy and saline near the shore, and the constant marine influence is poorly suited to a grove. If your real goal is to plant a few avocado trees on a San Diego property, we can point you toward the inland areas where they actually flourish, and where a private agricultural well is the right tool for the job.

Coastal Groundwater Realities

Even where wells exist near the coast, the groundwater picture is challenging. Shallow coastal aquifers in San Diego are vulnerable to saltwater intrusion, where over-pumping draws seawater inland and ruins water quality. Near the San Diego River, alluvial deposits can hold water, but quality varies and dissolved solids tend to run high. For these reasons, coastal-area wells are most realistically used for landscape irrigation and non-potable uses rather than drinking water, and any new well in the city is subject to strict permitting and water-quality oversight.

The San Diego River, which empties into the Pacific just north of Ocean Beach between OB and Mission Bay, has shaped the local hydrology for thousands of years. Its alluvial valley does hold groundwater, and historically the river was a significant water source for the young city. Today, though, that shallow water is heavily influenced by tidal mixing, urban runoff, and decades of development, so it is rarely a candidate for a clean domestic supply. Understanding this history matters because it explains why the coast is plumbed almost entirely with imported and treated municipal water rather than local wells, and why the few wells that remain near the coast are used cautiously.

What You Can Check Before Calling

If you have an existing well system on your property and it is acting up, a few quick checks help. Confirm that the pump has power and that no breaker has tripped. Tap the pressure tank to gauge its air charge: a hollow sound up top and a solid sound at the bottom is healthy, while a uniformly solid tank is likely waterlogged. Listen for a pump that short-cycles every few seconds, a classic sign of a pressure-switch or tank fault. If you are simply unsure whether an old, capped well on your lot is safe or legal to leave in place, take a photo of the wellhead and any visible casing, and we can advise on inspection or decommissioning.

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Anything involving the well casing, the pump, electrical work, water testing, or abandonment should be handled by a licensed contractor. An improperly abandoned well is a genuine groundwater-contamination hazard and a code liability. As a licensed C-57 water well contractor, Southern California Well Service can inspect, repair, test, or properly decommission a well and document the work for the county.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have an avocado grove in Ocean Beach?

Not practically. OB lots are small, coastal, and saline, which avocados dislike. Avocados thrive on inland San Diego County slopes such as Fallbrook and Valley Center. We are happy to advise on those areas instead.

Are there private wells in Ocean Beach?

Very few. Most properties use city water. A handful of older or larger parcels in the broader peninsula and river-valley area may have legacy wells, which we can inspect, service, or decommission.

Can I drill a well just for irrigation to save on water bills?

On a typical small coastal lot it is usually not feasible due to limited space, poor coastal water quality, and strict permitting. On a larger lot it may be worth evaluating. We will give you an honest feasibility assessment.

What is saltwater intrusion and does it affect me?

It is the inland movement of seawater into a coastal aquifer caused by over-pumping, which degrades water quality. It is a real risk near the coast and a key reason coastal wells are best limited to non-potable uses.

I have an old capped well on my property. What should I do?

Have it inspected. An abandoned well that is not properly sealed can contaminate groundwater and create code liability. We can decommission it to county standards, typically $1,500 to $5,000.
