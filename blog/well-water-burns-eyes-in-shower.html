Well Water Burns Eyes in Shower: Causes and Solutions

When your shower leaves you with burning, stinging, or irritated eyes, it's more than just uncomfortable—it's a sign that something in your water needs attention. While city water can cause eye irritation from chlorine, well water typically doesn't contain chlorine (unless recently treated). So what's causing the problem, and how do you fix it?

Common Causes of Eye Irritation from Well Water

1. High or Low pH Levels

The pH of healthy human tears is around 7.4. When water significantly differs from this:

  • High pH (alkaline, above 8.5): Causes burning and irritation
  • Low pH (acidic, below 6.5): Also irritates and can cause stinging

Well water pH can vary widely depending on local geology. Limestone aquifers tend to produce alkaline water; granite and sandstone areas often have more acidic water.

2. Residual Chlorine

If your well was recently shock chlorinated (disinfected), chlorine residual may still be present in your water. Chlorine is a known eye irritant, and concentrations that seem mild can cause problems during prolonged exposure like showering.

3. High Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)

Water with very high mineral content can irritate eyes. This includes calcium, magnesium, sodium, and other dissolved minerals that affect water chemistry.

4. Hydrogen Sulfide

The "rotten egg" gas can irritate eyes even at low concentrations. You may notice the smell in the shower as steam releases the gas from the water.

5. Contaminants

More seriously, certain contaminants can cause eye irritation:

  • Ammonia from agricultural runoff
  • Chemical contamination from nearby sources
  • Bacterial or algae toxins

⚠️ Seek Medical Attention If:

  • Eyes remain red or painful hours after showering
  • Vision changes or blurring occurs
  • Irritation is severe or worsening
  • Other symptoms like breathing difficulty or skin rash appear

Stop using the water until tested if symptoms are severe or your water has changed suddenly.

Testing Your Water

To identify the cause, test for:

  • pH: Should be between 6.5 and 8.5 (ideal is 7.0-7.5)
  • TDS: High levels may indicate overall mineral issues
  • Chlorine residual: Should be minimal unless you have a chlorination system
  • Hydrogen sulfide: Requires special testing (gas escapes quickly)
  • Basic water quality panel: Screens for common contaminants

Home pH test strips can give you a quick reading, but laboratory testing provides more accurate results.

Solutions Based on Cause

For pH Problems

  • High pH: Acid injection system (typically CO2 or citric acid injection)
  • Low pH: Calcite or soda ash neutralizer to raise pH
  • Both: pH adjustment should target 7.0-7.5 range

For Chlorine Residual

  • Continue flushing system after shock treatment
  • Install activated carbon filter to remove chlorine
  • Adjust continuous chlorination system if over-dosing

For High TDS/Minerals

  • Water softener addresses hardness minerals
  • Reverse osmosis for comprehensive mineral removal
  • Blending with lower-TDS water if available

For Hydrogen Sulfide

  • Aeration to off-gas the hydrogen sulfide
  • Oxidizing filter (greensand or similar)
  • Chlorination followed by carbon filtration

When to Call a Professional

Contact a water quality specialist when:

  • Eye irritation is severe or persists despite flushing
  • You need comprehensive water testing
  • Test results show pH or contaminant issues
  • You need help selecting appropriate treatment
  • The problem appeared suddenly (possible contamination event)
  • Other family members also experience irritation

Temporary Relief

While addressing the underlying cause:

  • Keep eyes closed or wear swim goggles while showering
  • Shorten shower time to reduce exposure
  • Use lower water temperatures (reduces chemical volatilization)
  • Rinse eyes with clean bottled water after showering
  • Install a basic shower filter (helps with some issues)

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hard water cause eye irritation?

Hard water itself (calcium and magnesium) typically doesn't cause eye burning, but very hard water can affect other water chemistry factors that do. Hard water also leaves mineral residue that may irritate sensitive skin around eyes.

Why does the irritation seem worse with hot water?

Hot water releases more dissolved gases (like hydrogen sulfide) and increases chemical volatility. Steam carries irritants directly into your eyes and respiratory system. Try slightly cooler showers as a test.

My well water just started burning my eyes recently—what changed?

Sudden changes often indicate: recent shock chlorination, seasonal water table changes affecting water chemistry, new contamination source, or equipment malfunction (like over-dosing chlorination system). Test promptly.

Will a water softener help with eye irritation?

Possibly. Water softeners address hardness but also affect pH slightly. If your irritation is from specific contaminants like chlorine or high pH, you may need targeted treatment rather than softening.
