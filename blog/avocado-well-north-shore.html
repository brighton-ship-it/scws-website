Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in Riverside County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your North Shore well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Agricultural Well Service for North Shore and the Eastern Coachella Valley

North Shore sits in southeastern Riverside County along the northeastern edge of the Salton Sea, at the tail end of the Coachella Valley. This is desert agriculture country in its truest form: a place where, thanks to deep groundwater and imported Colorado River water, growers turn blistering heat and long sunshine hours into some of the most productive farmland in the state. The eastern Coachella Valley is famous for its date palms, which account for roughly 95 percent of U.S. date production, along with table grapes, citrus, bell peppers, and winter vegetables. What it is not known for is the Hass avocado, which simply cannot tolerate the summer extremes and salt-laden water of the Salton basin.

We want to be honest with North Shore property owners about that. The coastal-style avocado grove belongs to the cooler hills near the ocean, not to the shore of the Salton Sea. But the underlying need is identical: any farm, ranch, or rural home out here lives and dies by the reliability of its well. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years keeping desert wells running, and we bring that same care to the eastern valley whether you are irrigating dates, grapes, citrus, or a backyard garden.

How Wells Work Around the Salton Sea

The Coachella Valley draws nearly all of its water from a large groundwater basin, an aquifer that is several thousand feet deep and capable of storing tens of millions of acre-feet. Agricultural and public-supply wells in the valley are commonly completed to depths between roughly 490 and 900 feet, far deeper than the shallow river wells found in places like Needles. At that depth, water is reached through layered sand, gravel, and clay deposits laid down over thousands of years.

The Coachella Valley is structurally complex, divided into subbasins by the San Andreas fault, and groundwater conditions can shift noticeably from one part of the valley to another. Near the Salton Sea, the combination of a long pumping history, agricultural drainage, and the chemistry of the receding sea makes water management more demanding than in the upper valley around Palm Springs. Practically speaking, that means a well design that works beautifully a few miles north may need adjustment here. We account for those local differences rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach, because a pump and treatment package matched to your actual water chemistry is the difference between a system that lasts and one that fails early.

A working agricultural system here typically pairs a deep-set submersible pump or turbine with a pressure tank, a pressure switch, and frequently a large open or closed storage reservoir that lets a grower bank water and irrigate on schedule. Because pumping lifts are long in a deep basin, energy efficiency matters enormously, and properly sized pumps and constant-pressure controls can meaningfully cut a grower's power bill over a season.

Common Well Issues in the North Shore Area

Decades of desert service have taught us what fails out here, and the eastern valley has its own signature problems:

  • Salinity and high total dissolved solids in groundwater near the Salton Sea, which stress salt-sensitive plantings and corrode equipment.
  • Scale and mineral fouling on pump components and drip emitters from hard, mineral-rich water.
  • Deep-set pump wear from long lifts and continuous irrigation duty.
  • Land subsidence stress in parts of the valley where heavy groundwater pumping has affected the ground.
  • Sediment intrusion from fine valley deposits clogging screens and filters.
  • Pressure tank and switch failures causing rapid pump cycling and inconsistent delivery.

What You Can Check Yourself

Before you call, a quick self-check can save time. If you have lost water entirely, confirm power at the well and check the breaker and any control-box reset. If pressure has fallen, tap the pressure tank: a hollow ring near the top and a solid sound near the bottom indicate a healthy air charge, while a uniformly solid tank suggests it is waterlogged and likely failing. A pump that clicks on and off every few seconds points to a pressure-switch or tank problem rather than the pump itself. On the irrigation side, walk the drip lines for clogged emitters and look for sections that have lost pressure, which often signals scale or a salt-related deposit. Note any sudden change in taste, smell, or color of the water, since timing is a valuable diagnostic clue.

When to Bring in a Pro

Pulling a deep-set pump, performing electrical work at the well head, testing water chemistry, or troubleshooting a yield decline are all jobs for a licensed contractor. Southern California Well Service holds a C-57 water well drilling license, which means we are qualified to service the well itself and not just the plumbing. In a region this dependent on irrigation, a well failure during the growing season can threaten an entire crop, so it pays to call at the first sign of trouble rather than waiting for a complete loss of water.

Salt management deserves special attention out here. Because the eastern valley irrigates heavily and evaporation is intense, salts steadily concentrate in the soil unless growers flush them downward with periodic leaching irrigation. A well that delivers ample water at the right pressure makes that leaching practical; a marginal well makes it a constant struggle. When we design or rehabilitate a system for a North Shore grower, we think about the whole picture, from the producing zone in the aquifer down to the emitter at the base of each plant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can avocados grow near North Shore?

Not commercially. The summer heat and the salty groundwater of the Salton basin are hostile to avocado trees. The eastern Coachella Valley excels instead at dates, grapes, citrus, and winter vegetables, and that is where we focus our agricultural well work in this area.

How deep are wells in the eastern Coachella Valley?

Agricultural and supply wells are commonly drilled between about 490 and 900 feet to reach reliable producing zones in the deep valley aquifer. We assess each parcel before recommending a depth and pump configuration.

Why is my well water so salty or hard?

Groundwater near the Salton Sea naturally carries elevated dissolved solids and minerals. We test for total dissolved solids, chloride, and hardness, then recommend treatment or blending so your irrigation and household water work as intended.

Is land subsidence a real concern for my well?

In parts of the Coachella Valley, heavy pumping has caused measurable subsidence that can stress well casings over time. We inspect for casing damage during service and can advise on protecting your investment.

Do I need a permit to drill a new well in Riverside County?

Yes. New wells in Riverside County require permitting and inspection through the county environmental health department. As a licensed C-57 contractor, we manage permits and build to code.

What does a diagnostic visit cost?

Our diagnostic is $125, credited toward any repair we complete.
