Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Bernardino County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • High-capacity agricultural wells
  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Skyforest well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Fractured-Granite Mountain Geology

Unlike the deep sandy aquifers of the desert or the alluvial basins of the valleys, the San Bernardino Mountains are built on hard crystalline and granitic bedrock. Mountain wells here typically produce from fractures and joints in the rock rather than from a thick saturated sand layer. That means yield depends heavily on whether a borehole intersects good fractures - two wells a short distance apart can produce very differently. Mountain groundwater is often of excellent quality, though some wells carry hardness, iron or natural sediment from the rock. Snowmelt recharge is a real seasonal factor, and freeze protection for above-ground equipment is essential at this elevation.

How a Mountain Well System Works

A Skyforest setup is the familiar combination - submersible pump in the casing, pressure tank, pressure switch and controls - but with mountain-specific demands. Because fractured-rock wells can have modest sustained yield, a storage tank that the well fills slowly, paired with a booster pump, is a smart way to guarantee strong household pressure. Just as important up here is winterization: heat tape, insulated well houses, and properly buried lines keep your system from freezing during the snow season. We design with hard winters in mind.

Common Mountain Well Problems

  • Freeze damage to pressure tanks, switches and exposed lines during cold snaps.
  • Low or seasonal yield from fractured-rock wells, especially in dry years.
  • Sediment and fine rock from the fractures clouding water or wearing the pump.
  • Pump set too high for the producing fractures, causing air and short-cycling.
  • Hardness and iron on some mountain wells.
  • Power outages common in mountain storms, stressing pumps on restart.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Power. Mountain outages are frequent - confirm the breaker and control box first.
  • Freeze. In winter, check whether exposed components or lines have frozen.
  • Gauge. See whether pressure cycles normally or has gone flat.
  • Tank. A short-cycling, solid-sounding tank is likely waterlogged.
  • Clarity. Cloudy water or new sediment can signal a dropping level in a fractured well.

Winterizing Your Mountain Well System

At Skyforest's elevation, freeze protection is not optional - it is the difference between a comfortable winter and a burst tank in January. Pressure tanks, switches and any above-ground plumbing need insulation, heat tape, and an enclosed or buried setup that keeps them above freezing through extended cold snaps. Water lines should run below the frost line, and well houses should be sealed and, where needed, gently heated. We winterize mountain systems before the snow arrives and respond quickly when a cold snap catches an older setup off guard. A modest investment in freeze protection saves a far larger repair bill - and spares you a freezing weekend hauling water.

Getting the Most From a Fractured-Rock Well

Mountain wells live and die by their fractures. Because Skyforest wells produce from cracks and joints in granite rather than from a thick sand aquifer, two nearby boreholes can yield very differently, and a well that produced well for years can slow as fractures silt up or the water level shifts. When yield falls, the answer is not always a new well. Lowering the pump to reach producing fractures, cleaning the well, or hydrofracturing to open new water-bearing cracks can often restore flow at a fraction of the cost of drilling. We assess the well honestly and recommend the least expensive option that genuinely solves the problem.

Can you grow avocados in Skyforest?

No. At about 5,700 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, Skyforest has cold, snowy winters that would kill frost-sensitive avocados. We focus on dependable mountain household and cabin well service instead.

How do mountain wells differ from desert wells?

Skyforest wells produce from fractures in granite bedrock, not from sandy aquifers. Yield depends on whether the borehole hits good fractures, so output can vary sharply between nearby wells.

How do I protect my well from freezing?

Heat tape, insulated well houses and properly buried lines prevent freeze damage to tanks, switches and pipes. We winterize mountain systems for the snow season as part of our service.

My mountain well yield dropped - what can be done?
