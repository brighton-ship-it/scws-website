Immediate Steps After Positive Test Results

Stop all consumption immediately—this includes pets. Post reminders on faucets. Switch to bottled water for drinking, cooking, baby formula, and brushing teeth. Contact your testing laboratory to understand exactly what was found and at what levels. Some contamination is more urgent than others.

Understanding Different Contamination Types

Bacterial (E. coli, coliform): Often remediable with shock chlorination and source correction. Nitrates: Common from fertilizers and septic; dangerous for infants, requires treatment system. Heavy metals (arsenic, lead): May require treatment system or new well. Chemicals (VOCs, pesticides): Complex remediation, possibly professional environmental assessment. Each type requires different responses.

Shock Chlorination Procedure

For bacterial contamination, shock chlorination involves adding concentrated chlorine to the well, circulating it through all plumbing, letting it sit 12-24 hours, then flushing completely. This kills bacteria but doesn't fix how they entered. If contamination returns after shock chlorination, you have an ongoing source that must be identified and corrected.

When Professional Intervention Is Required

Call a well professional if: contamination returns after shock chlorination, you can't identify the contamination source, chemical or heavy metal contamination is present, well casing damage is suspected, or multiple contaminants are detected. Some situations require well rehabilitation, new casing, treatment systems, or in severe cases, drilling a new well in a different location.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do immediately if my well tests positive for bacteria?

Stop drinking the water immediately. Don't use it for cooking, brushing teeth, making ice, or washing produce. For bathing, E. coli contamination poses risk through ingestion, so avoid getting water in eyes, nose, or mouth. Use bottled water until resolved.

Can I treat contaminated well water myself?

For bacterial contamination, boiling water for 1 minute makes it safe for emergency use. Shock chlorination can clear bacteria, but should address the contamination source. For chemical contamination (nitrates, arsenic, VOCs), boiling doesn't help—you need treatment systems or alternative water sources.

How did my well get contaminated?

Common sources: failing septic systems, agricultural runoff, surface water infiltration through damaged casing, flooding, nearby construction or drilling, corroding well components, or naturally occurring contaminants in your aquifer. Identifying the source is critical to prevent recontamination.
