Sand in Your Corona Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Corona well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Corona well.

Sand and Sediment in Corona Well Water: A Local Guide

If you own a private well in Corona, you already know the value of independent water in a fast-growing corner of western Riverside County. Corona sits in the Temescal Valley at the western edge of the Inland Empire, cradled between the Santa Ana Mountains and the Cleveland National Forest to the southwest and the Temescal Wash and Santa Ana River corridor to the north. That setting is beautiful, but it also shapes exactly what comes out of your well. Many homeowners here draw their water from the alluvial sand-and-gravel deposits of the Temescal and Bedford basins, and those same loose, water-bearing sediments are the reason a glass of well water can suddenly turn cloudy, gritty, or downright sandy.

When you start seeing sand settle at the bottom of a glass, hear grit rattling through your faucet aerators, or find sediment building up in the toilet tank, it is more than a nuisance. In Corona's alluvial aquifers, sand in the water is usually an early warning that something in your well or pump system has changed. Catching it early protects your pump, your pressure tank, and your household plumbing. This guide explains why Corona wells produce sand, how Southern California Well Service diagnoses the problem, what repairs actually fix it, and what you can expect to spend.

Why Corona Wells Produce Sand

The single biggest factor is geology. Unlike wells drilled into the fractured granite of the surrounding foothills, most domestic wells across the Corona basin floor tap alluvial sand and gravel laid down over thousands of years by the Santa Ana River and Temescal Wash. These Coldwater and Bedford subbasin formations are productive and recharge well, but they are inherently loose. Fine sand particles sit right alongside the water you are pumping, so any weakness in the well's defenses lets that sand migrate into your system. A well drilled into solid bedrock rarely makes sand; a well finished in young alluvial sediment is always at some risk, which is why local experience matters so much here.

On top of that baseline geology, several specific mechanical and design issues push sand into Corona well water:

  • A worn or failing pump: As a submersible pump ages, its impellers and wear rings erode. A pump that is already passing a little sand grinds itself down faster, and the damage feeds on itself.
  • An oversized pump that over-draws the well: A pump that moves more water than the aquifer can comfortably yield pulls fine sand out of the surrounding gravel pack and into the casing. Bigger is not better with a sandy alluvial formation.
  • A failed, corroded, or collapsed well screen: The screen is the slotted barrier that keeps sand out while letting water in. After years in the ground, corrosion or mineral encrustation can open it up and let sediment pour through.
  • The wrong screen slot size: Corona's fine alluvial sand demands a correctly sized slot and a properly graded gravel pack. If the original driller used too coarse a slot, the well will produce sand for its entire life until it is reworked.
  • Excessive drawdown: When the water level is pulled down too far during pumping, water rushes toward the screen at high velocity and carries sand with it. This is common in dry years when regional water levels drop.
  • A pump set too low: A pump hanging near the bottom of the well sits in the very sediment that settles there, drawing it straight into the system.
  • A new, undeveloped well: A freshly drilled well naturally produces some sand for the first weeks until it is properly developed. If sand never stops, the development step was incomplete.
  • A breach in the casing: A crack, split seam, or corroded hole in the casing lets sand bypass the screen entirely.

In practice we often find more than one of these at work in a single Corona well. The combination of naturally sandy ground and an aging or mismatched pump system is the classic local recipe for gritty water.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand is abrasive, and water-driven sand acts like liquid sandpaper inside your equipment. The first casualty is almost always the pump itself. A submersible pump pushing sand wears through its impellers, seals, and bearings in a fraction of its normal lifespan, and what should have been a fifteen-year pump can fail in two or three. Replacing a pump is far more expensive than addressing the sand that destroyed it.

Downstream, the damage spreads. Sand settles in your pressure tank, reducing its usable capacity and causing the pump to short-cycle. It scores the seats of pressure switches, check valves, and faucet cartridges, so you start chasing drips and leaks all over the house. It clogs aerators, showerheads, and the fine passages in water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines. Sediment can also bake onto the bottom of an electric or gas water heater, cutting efficiency and shortening its life. Over time, gritty water etches fixtures and leaves a persistent cloudy or tan tint. None of this resolves on its own, and every week of pumping sand makes the eventual repair bigger.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Corona

Guesswork is expensive, so we start every sandy-water call with a structured diagnosis built for Corona's alluvial conditions. Our goal is to find out exactly where the sand is coming from before we recommend a single repair.

  • The jar test: We capture water in a clear container and let it settle, measuring how much sand drops out and how coarse it is. The grain size tells us a lot about whether it is fine aquifer sand or coarser material from a screen or casing failure.
  • Sand concentration over time: We check whether sand appears constantly, only at startup, or only under heavy demand. The timing points toward drawdown, pump position, or screen problems.
  • Amp draw and pump depth: We read the pump's electrical draw and confirm how deep it is set relative to the water level and the screen. A pump set too low in the sediment is a frequent, easily corrected culprit.
  • Pulling and inspecting the pump and screen: When the data points underground, we pull the pump and examine it, the drop pipe, and the screen for sand-cut wear, corrosion, and encrustation.
  • Downhole camera inspection: A waterproof camera lets us see the inside of the casing and screen directly, identifying cracks, holes, slot damage, and sediment fill without guesswork.
  • Flow and drawdown testing: We measure how far the water level drops at a given pumping rate to learn whether the well is being over-pumped relative to what this stretch of the Temescal Valley aquifer can deliver.

Our full diagnostic is a flat $125, and we credit that amount toward any repair work you authorize. You get a clear answer about your well, and if you move forward, the diagnosis effectively costs nothing.

How We Fix It

The right fix depends entirely on the diagnosis, which is why the inspection comes first. For Corona wells, our solutions fall into a few categories, and we frequently combine them:

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters: A centrifugal sand separator spins sand out of the water before it reaches your tank and plumbing. For wells in naturally sandy alluvium, this is often the most cost-effective long-term protection.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration: A spin-down filter with a flushable screen, paired with cartridge sediment filtration, polishes out the finer grit that a separator does not catch, protecting fixtures and appliances.
  • Correct pump sizing and placement: If an oversized pump is over-drawing the well or a pump is set too low, we resize and reposition it so it draws water gently and stays above the sediment zone.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment: For a screen choked with mineral encrustation or a well that was never fully developed, we clean and redevelop the well to restore the gravel pack and stop sand at the source.
  • Screen repair or relining: When the screen or casing is breached, we repair, reline, or sleeve the damaged section so sand can no longer bypass it.

A surface filter alone will not save a well that is structurally failing, and a downhole repair alone will not stop sand a naturally sandy aquifer will always produce. We recommend the combination that actually solves your situation rather than the one that is easiest to sell.

Preventing Sand Problems

Some sand risk is built into Corona's geology, but good habits keep it from becoming a crisis. Avoid over-pumping by matching your pump to the well's real capacity rather than installing the biggest pump available. Have your system checked periodically, especially during the dry late-summer and fall months when regional water levels in the Temescal Valley drop and drawdown increases. Pay attention to early warning signs: a faint grittiness in the water, a slowly shrinking pressure tank, or a pump that runs more often than it used to. If you are drilling a new well or replacing a pump, insist on a correctly sized screen, a properly graded gravel pack, and thorough well development. Spending a little extra on the right screen and development up front prevents a lifetime of sandy water and premature pump failures.

When to Call a Professional

A trace of sand right after a new well is developed can be normal and may clear on its own. Anything beyond that is a reason to call. Reach out promptly if sand is constant rather than fading, if grit suddenly appears in a well that was previously clean, if you notice falling water pressure or a short-cycling pump, or if you can see visible sediment accumulating in tanks, toilets, or filters. Sand is a downhole problem with electrical equipment in deep water, and probing it without the right tools risks both your safety and an expensive pump. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Corona well water normal?

A small amount of sand right after a new well is drilled and developed can be normal and usually clears within a few weeks. Persistent or sudden sand in an established well is not normal in Corona's alluvial aquifers and should be diagnosed before it damages your pump.

Will sand ruin my well pump?

Yes. Sand is abrasive and grinds down a submersible pump's impellers and seals, often cutting its lifespan from fifteen years to just a few. Stopping the sand is almost always cheaper than repeatedly replacing pumps.

Can I just install a filter and ignore the cause?

A sand separator or sediment filter is an important part of many solutions, but it does not fix a failed screen, a breached casing, or an over-drawn well. We diagnose the source first so a filter protects your home rather than masking a worsening downhole problem.

Why does my water turn sandy only when demand is high?

Heavy demand pulls the water level down quickly, and the fast flow toward the screen drags fine alluvial sand along with it. That pattern usually points to over-pumping, an oversized pump, or excessive drawdown, all of which we can correct.
