Why Wells Go Dry

  • Drought Conditions: Extended dry periods lower the water table
  • Increased Demand: More wells in the area drawing from the same aquifer
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: Water table naturally drops in summer/fall
  • Pump Position: Pump may be positioned above current water level
  • Well Age: Mineral buildup reducing water entry points
  • Aquifer Depletion: Long-term overdrawing of groundwater

Solutions for Dry Wells in Alpine

1. Well Deepening

If the water table has dropped, deepening your existing well may reach water again. This is often less expensive than drilling a new well.

2. Hydrofracturing

High-pressure water treatment can open new fractures in bedrock, allowing more water to flow into your well.

3. Pump Lowering

Sometimes simply lowering your pump deeper in the well can restore water production.

4. New Well Drilling

When rehabilitation isn't viable, we can drill a new well in a better location on your property.

Dry and Low-Yield Wells in Alpine: What's Happening Underground

Alpine sits in the foothills east of El Cajon along the Interstate 8 corridor, at around 1,800 feet, where suburban San Diego County gives way to rural ranch and grove country. Many Alpine and neighboring Jamul properties — especially the larger lots along Japatul, Tavern Road, and the back canyons — depend on private wells rather than the water district. When one of those wells goes dry or slows to a trickle, it disrupts the whole household. The encouraging news is that Alpine's wells are well understood, and most "dry" wells can be brought back without drilling new.

With more than 30 years of experience across the San Diego foothills, Southern California Well Service has worked countless Alpine wells. Understanding the local granite geology is the foundation of any good repair decision.

Why Alpine Wells Go Dry

Alpine and Jamul sit squarely on the Peninsular Ranges batholith — granite bedrock. Wells here tap fracture systems in the granite, and production varies dramatically from one property to the next. Records for the area show an average well depth around 500 feet with a static water level often near 130 feet below the surface, and yields that range widely from a few gallons per minute to twenty-five or more. That fracture-driven variability shapes dry-well causes:

  • Fracture-fed wells and drought. Because water moves through cracks rather than a uniform aquifer, a multi-year drought that lowers the regional table can sharply reduce flow into a well's fractures. One property can hold up while a neighbor's well struggles.
  • Wells drilled too shallow or into poor fractures. Some older wells never intersected strong fractures, or were completed shallow; as levels drop, they are the first to fail.
  • Seasonal and long-term decline. Levels fall through the dry season and over consecutive dry years, leaving marginal wells short.
  • Pump set above the water. A pump hanging above the current pumping level pulls air well before the well is truly dry.
  • Mineral fouling. Hard Alpine water scales pump components and fractures over time, gradually choking yield.

Confirming the Well Is Really Dry

With Alpine's fracture variability, measurement is everything. Our technician sounds the well for the static and pumping water levels, tests the pump's amperage, and measures recovery after a controlled drawdown. A well that draws down hard and recovers slowly is yield-limited; one with standing water but no output has an equipment or placement issue. We review your driller's report for the fracture intervals and check the pressure tank and controls. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward any repair.

Solutions for a Dry Alpine Well

Lower the Pump

If the well still holds water but the pump sits above it, resetting the pump deeper in the casing is the quickest, cheapest fix — $800 to $2,500 — when there is depth and water column to spare.

Hydrofracturing

Hydrofracturing is especially effective on Alpine's granite wells. High-pressure injection clears and opens fractures, and many low-yield wells gain several gallons per minute. At $3,000 to $8,000, it is frequently the best value for a fracture-fed well that has slowed rather than stopped.

Deepen the Existing Well

Where the bore no longer reaches productive fractures, deepening to intersect new water-bearing zones can revive the well for $5,000 to $15,000, far below the cost of a new well.

Storage Tank and Booster Pump

For a well with low but steady yield, a storage tank and booster pump let it fill a cistern around the clock while the booster delivers strong household pressure on demand — roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed.

Flow-Management Controls

Low-water cutoffs, timers, and flow controllers protect the pump and let a modest fracture system keep up with demand.

Drill a New, Deeper Well

For a worn-out or poorly producing well, a new well drilled to current depths — commonly 400 to 600+ feet in Alpine — is the durable answer, at $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey. We treat it as a last resort after rehabilitation is ruled out.

When to Call a Professional

Air in the faucets, sand, a short-cycling pump, or whole-house pressure loss mean you should shut the pump off and call a licensed C-57 contractor. Running a pump dry in a deep granite well is a fast way to destroy an expensive motor. We are licensed and equipped to work inside the well, which a general plumber is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are wells in Alpine, and how much water do they make?

Records for the Alpine area show an average depth around 500 feet, with static water levels often near 130 feet. Yields vary widely — from a few gallons per minute to twenty-five or more — because production depends on intersecting fractures in the granite bedrock.

Why does my neighbor's well work fine while mine went dry?

Alpine wells draw from fracture networks in granite, and those fractures vary dramatically from parcel to parcel. One property can intersect strong water-bearing fractures while a nearby well taps weaker ones, so drought affects them unevenly. Hydrofracturing can often improve a weaker well.

Is hydrofracturing worth it for an Alpine granite well?

Usually yes. Hydrofracturing is particularly effective in fractured-granite terrain like Alpine's, clearing and opening fractures so more water flows in. Many low-yield wells gain several gallons per minute, and at $3,000 to $8,000 it is often the best value short of a new well.

Can I lower my pump instead of drilling deeper?

If the well still holds water and the casing has remaining depth, yes — resetting the pump deeper costs just $800 to $2,500. We always check this first before considering deepening ($5,000 to $15,000) or a new well ($18,000 to $42,000).

My Alpine well makes water slowly. Will a storage tank help?

Yes. A storage tank and booster pump let a low-yield well fill a cistern continuously while the booster delivers strong pressure on demand. At roughly $3,000 to $7,000 it is a cost-effective fix for the slow, steady wells common in Alpine.
