What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Nuevo Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Nuevo

Nuevo is a small unincorporated community in the San Jacinto Valley of Riverside County, tucked against the southern flank of the Lakeview Mountains just east of Perris and north of Menifee. It is genuine well country. Many homes, small farms, and ranches here sit beyond municipal water lines and depend entirely on their own groundwater. The geology around Nuevo is a tale of two zones. The flat valley floor is filled with alluvial sand and gravel, while the low hills of the Lakeview range expose the granitic and metamorphic rock of the Peninsular Ranges batholith. Local well records reflect that variety, with depths ranging from very shallow alluvial wells near the drainages to bedrock wells drilled well past 800 feet up against the hills.

That split matters because hydrofracturing only works in rock that holds fractures. If your Nuevo well is drilled into the granite and granodiorite along the mountain margin, you are a strong candidate. If it draws from the loose valley-floor sediments, you probably are not, and we will say so before you spend a dollar. We have built our reputation on that kind of straight talk, and it is why neighbors keep referring us.

The Mechanics of Hydrofracturing

We seal a section of your open borehole with an inflatable packer, then pump clean water into that isolated zone at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. In the fractured granodiorite common along the Lakeview foothills, that pressure widens the natural fractures already present in the rock and scours out the clay, fine sediment, and mineral scale that has accumulated in them over the years. Those cleaner, wider fractures then deliver more water into the borehole. The process is purely mechanical and uses only water, with no additives of any kind going into your well. From the first packer set to the final flow test, a Nuevo treatment usually takes four to eight hours, almost always within a single working day.

Because the name sounds alarming, we should be clear that this has nothing in common with the deep-shale oil-and-gas fracking people read about. Water-well hydrofracturing works a few hundred feet down, at lower pressures, with no sand or chemical mixture. The only material entering the ground is clean water, and the goal is simply to reconnect and clean the natural fractures your well already relies on.

Common Reasons Nuevo Wells Slow Down

  • Mineral scaling in bedrock fractures — the hard groundwater here leaves carbonate and iron deposits that gradually choke off a granite well's feeder fractures, and this is the textbook problem hydrofracturing reverses.
  • Marginal original yield — bedrock wells along the batholith can come in low simply because drilling happened to miss the most productive fracture zones.
  • Seasonal drawdown — in dry years the water table across the San Jacinto Basin drops, and shallow valley wells feel it first during the summer irrigation season.
  • Encrustation at the intake — buildup on the screen or perforations restricts flow even when the surrounding aquifer is still healthy.

What a Typical Nuevo Service Visit Looks Like

On site, we run a baseline flow test to measure exactly how many gallons per minute the well is producing now. Being Straight About Valley-Floor Wells

Plenty of Nuevo properties sit out on the open valley floor where the well bottoms in sand and gravel rather than rock. Hydrofracturing is the wrong tool there. Pressurized water does not create lasting fractures in loose sediment. It just rearranges the grains, and the well returns to its old yield within days. For those wells we recommend more durable solutions: deepening the borehole to chase a better-producing zone, drilling a new well at a geologically smarter spot, or, if the aquifer is simply thin, managing demand with larger storage tanks and a constant-pressure pump so the water you do have arrives at the house at steady pressure. Recommending the right fix matters more to us than booking a treatment we know will disappoint.

A Practical Pre-Visit Checklist

  1. Pull the well completion report. It states total depth and the formation at the bottom, rock or sand, which tells us right away whether hydrofracturing is even on the table.
  2. Document the decline. Is it gradual over years (often scaling) or sudden (often a pump or pressure issue)? The pattern guides the whole diagnosis.
  3. Check inexpensive culprits first. A bad pressure switch or a waterlogged tank can imitate a low-yield well at a fraction of the cost of any downhole work.
  4. Note water hardness and staining. Heavy scale on fixtures is exactly what hydrofracturing clears from bedrock fractures.
  5. Watch your storage. If you run out only during heavy irrigation, the answer may be more storage rather than more well.

Nuevo Hydrofracturing FAQ

Is my Nuevo well a good candidate?

If it is drilled into the granitic rock of the Lakeview Mountains margin, very likely yes. If it draws from the valley-floor sand and gravel, probably not, and we will recommend deepening or a new well instead. The diagnostic settles the question.

How deep are wells around Nuevo?

They vary widely, from shallow alluvial wells near the drainages to bedrock wells past 800 feet along the foothills. Your completion report will tell you which type you have.

Will the treatment affect my water quality?

No. We use only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals. If anything, flushing the fractures often improves clarity by clearing accumulated sediment from the well.

How much more water might I get?

In suitable bedrock wells, two to five times the prior flow is common, with improvement in about 70-80% of well-chosen candidates. We document the before and after flow rates for you in writing.

What happens if hydrofracturing is not right for my well?

We tell you during the diagnostic, before any treatment, and lay out the alternatives: deepening, a new well, or better storage and pressure equipment.

Can you also service my pump and tank?

Yes. Pump, tank, switch, filtration, and pressure systems can all be handled in the same visit by the same crew.
