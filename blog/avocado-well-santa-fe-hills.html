Avocado Water Demands

  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Santa Fe Hills well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How a Hillside Well System Works

A well on the granite slopes around Santa Fe Hills draws water from fractures in the rock rather than from a sandy aquifer, so yields vary sharply from one parcel to the next. A submersible pump set in the casing lifts water to a pressure tank, which holds a pressurized reserve so the pump is not forced to restart on every demand; a pressure switch controls the cycle. Properties with significant landscaping or a few fruit trees often add a storage tank or a constant-pressure system so the well's modest steady flow can be delivered as a strong burst when irrigation runs in the afternoon heat. Even homes on city water sometimes use booster systems to push pressure to distant hillside zones.

Where there are avocado or citrus trees, the demand is real even on a backyard scale. A mature avocado can drink 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, so a homeowner watering a handful of trees on a fracture-fed well benefits from the same storage-and-pump logic a grower uses, just sized smaller.

Most of what we do around Santa Fe Hills falls into a few predictable categories, and knowing them helps you spot trouble early. The most common call is a failed or failing submersible pump on a rural-residential well, often after years of hard-water wear. Close behind are pressure-tank failures, which show up as rapid pump cycling and inconsistent pressure at the house and irrigation. We also install constant-pressure and booster systems for properties with sprawling hillside landscaping, set up sediment and mineral treatment for wells producing grit or scale, and rehabilitate or properly decommission older wells on long-held parcels. Because we stock the full range of pumps, tanks, and treatment gear, most of these jobs are finished in a single visit rather than strung out over several trips.

For homeowners with a few avocado or citrus trees, the advice is the same as for any grower, just scaled down: know what your well can actually deliver, store enough water to comfortably cover the hottest hours of the day, and keep the water chemistry in check so that salt-sensitive trees do not slowly decline over the years.

Common Well Scenarios in the Area

  • Hard, mineral-rich water. Granite-country groundwater scales pressure tanks, fittings, and drip emitters over time.
  • Low yield in late summer. Fracture-fed wells recharge slowly and can struggle by September after a dry winter.
  • Pressure-tank waterlogging. A failed bladder makes the pump short cycle, the fastest way to burn out a motor.
  • Sediment and iron. Fines from decomposed granite and dissolved iron clog filters and stain hardscape.
  • Booster-system faults. Properties relying on pressure boosting lose coverage when a pump or tank fails.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  • Read the pressure gauge — healthy systems cycle around 40 to 60 psi; a bouncing needle suggests a waterlogged tank or failing switch.
  • Listen for short cycling — the pump kicking on every few seconds — which threatens the motor.
  • Determine whether the problem is everywhere or at one irrigation zone; the former points to the pump or well, the latter to a filter or emitter.
  • Note any change in taste, smell, or color that signals a water-quality shift.
  • Confirm breakers and the pressure switch before assuming the worst.

Seasonal Care That Prevents Emergencies

The San Marcos hills swing from a damp, foggy spring to a parched autumn, and a hillside well feels every bit of it. After winter rains, when the water table is highest, run a flow test to learn your true yield before summer. In spring, check the pressure-tank air charge and clean sediment filters and emitters fouled by hard-water scale. Through summer, watch pump run times — rising run times mean the well is drawing down. Every couple of years, a water test catches slow shifts in hardness, iron, and chloride before they damage equipment or salt-sensitive plantings. For properties with fruit trees, sizing storage to the hottest week keeps the well from becoming the bottleneck.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there private wells in the Santa Fe Hills area?

Inside the community most homes are on municipal water, but larger lots and rural-residential parcels in the surrounding San Marcos hills do run on private wells, booster systems, and irrigation we service.

How deep are wells in these hills?

Granite-country wells around San Marcos commonly run from roughly 200 to 600 feet, chasing water-bearing fractures. Depth varies sharply parcel to parcel, so a site assessment matters more than a neighbor's figure.

Can you service a booster system on a city-water home?

Yes. We install and repair booster and constant-pressure systems that push adequate pressure to distant hillside irrigation zones and large landscaped lots.

My well runs low by late summer. Can you help?
