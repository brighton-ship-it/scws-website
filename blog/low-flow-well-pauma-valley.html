Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Flow Well Solutions for Pauma Valley Groves and Homes

Pauma Valley is avocado and citrus country, tucked at the foot of Palomar Mountain in northern San Diego County. When a well here runs low, it is not just an inconvenience — for many properties it threatens the trees that pay the bills. If your Pauma Valley well cannot keep your sprinklers and microjets running, drops off mid-cycle, or has slowly faded over several seasons, you have a low-yield well. Whether you are on the lush valley floor or a decomposed-granite hillside, there are dependable ways to get more water.

The local geology splits sharply. Valley-floor parcels near the San Luis Rey River draw from generous alluvial aquifers that can produce 30 to 80 GPM or more. Climb into the surrounding hills and the picture changes: wells there penetrate the Peninsular Ranges batholith — granitic and metamorphic rock with far less storage — where natural yield is often modest and heavy grove irrigation strains it further.

Well Yield and GPM Testing Explained

Yield is the sustained gallons-per-minute your well can deliver, and for an irrigating property it is the number that matters most. We determine it with a flow test plus drawdown and recovery monitoring, pumping at a steady rate while tracking how far the water level falls and how quickly it returns. A hillside well that draws down hard and recovers slowly is storage-limited in the bedrock; a valley well that holds level but underperforms is more likely fouled or under-pumped. Knowing the real number lets us size storage and pumps correctly for your trees.

Causes of Low Flow in Pauma Valley

  • Fractured-rock storage limits. Hillside wells in granitic and metamorphic rock simply hold and transmit less water.
  • Heavy irrigation demand. Avocado and citrus groves draw large volumes, exposing a marginal well quickly.
  • Drought and a dropping water table. Dry years lower regional groundwater and shrink fracture saturation.
  • Scaled or biofouled screens. Mineral and bacterial buildup chokes inflow over time.
  • A pump set too high above the productive zone, leaving available water untapped.

Solutions for a Low-Yield Pauma Valley Well

Rehabilitation and redevelopment clean a fouled screen and gravel pack to recover lost capacity — the logical first step. Lowering the pump recaptures flow when the water table has dropped. Hydrofracturing shines on hillside fractured-rock wells, reopening and linking fractures to boost yield. Deepening or a new well reaches better zones when the existing bore is tapped out. And a storage tank with a booster or constant-pressure pump is often the key to grove irrigation: a modest well fills a large tank around the clock, then delivers the high on-demand flow your irrigation blocks require.

When to Call a Professional

Call if your flow has dropped, your trees are showing water stress, you are pulling sand or air, or you plan to expand plantings. A flow and drawdown test tells us exactly what your well can support and what it will take to meet your demand affordably.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should a Pauma Valley well produce?

It depends on whether you are on the valley floor or the surrounding hills. Valley-floor wells near the San Luis Rey River often produce 30 to 80+ GPM, excellent for groves. Hillside wells drilled into the Peninsular Ranges batholith may yield only 2 to 8 GPM, which can fall short for avocado and citrus irrigation.

Why is my hillside Pauma Valley well low on flow?

Deeper wells around Pauma Valley penetrate granitic and metamorphic bedrock with limited storage. Low natural yield is common, and it is intensified by the heavy water demand of irrigating groves. Drought years and a dropping water table compound the problem, as can a scaled screen or a pump set too high.

Will hydrofracturing help an agricultural well in Pauma Valley?

On fractured-rock wells, yes. Hydrofracturing reopens and connects fractures in the granitic bedrock and can substantially raise yield. For valley-floor alluvial wells it is less relevant; there, rehabilitation or a properly sized pump usually matters more.

How can a storage system help my grove during peak irrigation?

A large storage tank lets a modest well collect water continuously, then deliver a high flow rate on demand for irrigation cycles. Pairing storage with a booster or constant-pressure pump effectively multiplies a low-flow well's usable capacity during peak avocado and citrus watering.

How deep are Pauma Valley wells?

Valley-floor wells often reach productive aquifers at 150 to 300 feet, while hillside properties may require 400 to 600 feet through decomposed and fractured granite. Pauma Valley's average is close to the San Diego County average of about 450 feet.

What does well work cost in Pauma Valley?

Rehabilitation typically runs $1,500 to $6,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, and storage-plus-booster systems $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey agricultural well ranges from $18,000 to $42,000. Our diagnostic is $125, credited toward approved work.
