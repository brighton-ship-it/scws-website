What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

  • Well runs dry during peak usage

What Hydrofracturing Is and How It Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation technique that uses nothing but clean, high-pressure water to reopen and extend the natural fractures in bedrock surrounding a well. It is completely different from the oil-and-gas "fracking" that makes headlines. There are no chemicals, no sand proppants, and no deep-shale targets — just water pushed hard enough to clear out mineral scale and sediment that have plugged the cracks feeding your well.

The process is straightforward once the equipment is on site. A technician lowers an inflatable device called a packer into the well casing and inflates it to seal off a target section of the borehole. Clean water is then injected below the packer at pressures that typically run from about 1,500 PSI up to 3,000 PSI or more. That pressure flushes debris out of existing fractures and can open new hairline pathways in the rock, giving groundwater more routes to seep into the well. On wells with several productive zones, the packer can be repositioned to treat each one separately. Start to finish, a hydrofracturing job usually takes about four to eight hours.

The key point — and the one that matters most in Pedley — is that this only works in rock that holds fractures. Hard, fractured crystalline rock and bedrock can be opened up and cleaned. Loose sand and gravel cannot; there is nothing to fracture, and high-pressure water simply pushes the material around without creating lasting flow paths.

The Local Nuance: Valley-Floor Wells vs. Bedrock Wells

Here is where Pedley's geography becomes the deciding factor. The community straddles two very different settings, and the difference determines whether hydrofracturing can help you.

Down on the valley floor along the Santa Ana River, the ground is made up of alluvial deposits — unconsolidated sand, silt, gravel, and clay washed down and laid out by the river over thousands of years. This is part of the broader Riverside and Arlington groundwater area. Wells drilled into these alluvial materials draw water from the spaces between sand and gravel grains, not from cracks in solid rock. Alluvial wells on the valley floor are usually not good candidates for hydrofracturing, because there is no bedrock to fracture. Forcing high-pressure water into loose sediment does not create the durable new flow paths that fracturing relies on.

On the higher ground and the slopes around the valley, you are more likely to hit bedrock — decomposed granite and crystalline rock. Bedrock wells on these higher-elevation, large-lot and equestrian properties may be good candidates, because the formation holds the kind of fractures that respond well to pressure treatment.

So how do you tell which kind of well you actually have? Two clues:

  • Location. If your property is low on the valley floor close to the Santa Ana River, you are probably on alluvium. If you are up on higher or sloping ground away from the river bottom, bedrock becomes much more likely.
  • The driller's log. The single best source of truth is your well completion report (the driller's log). It records the materials the drill passed through. Words like "sand," "gravel," and "clay" point to alluvial formation; words like "granite," "bedrock," or "fractured rock" point to a formation that can be fractured. If you can't find your copy, the California Department of Water Resources keeps well completion reports on file.

If you are not sure, that is exactly what a professional assessment is for. We would rather tell a Pedley homeowner honestly that fracturing won't help than take money for a treatment that can't work in their formation. Typical private wells in this area run roughly 150 to 400 feet deep, and the driller's log for that depth usually makes the formation obvious.

Signs a Bedrock Well May Need Hydrofracturing

If you have confirmed (or strongly suspect) that you are on a bedrock well, the following symptoms are the classic signs that fracturing might restore your yield:

  • Declining yield over time — your well simply produces less water than it did a few years ago.
  • Slow recovery after pumping — the water level takes a long time to come back up after the pump shuts off, so you run short during heavy use.
  • Dry-season shortfalls — the well struggles during the hot Inland Empire summer or in drought years when the water table drops.
  • An under-producing new well — a recently drilled bedrock well that never delivered the flow you expected. Fracturing a fresh bedrock well is one of the most common and successful uses of the technique.

Better-Fit Solutions for Alluvial Wells

If your Pedley well turns out to be in alluvial sand and gravel, don't be discouraged — low yield and low pressure in these wells almost always have a fix, just not hydrofracturing. The right tools for valley-floor wells include:

  • Well redevelopment and cleaning. Over time, fine sediment and mineral scale clog the screen and the gravel pack around an alluvial well. Surging, jetting, and bailing can clear that material and often restore a meaningful share of lost capacity.
  • Screen and casing work. A corroded or encrusted well screen chokes off flow. Repairing, replacing, or re-screening the well can reopen the formation to the borehole.
  • Deepening the well. If the water table has dropped, extending the well deeper into the saturated zone can recover lost production.
  • Pump upgrade or correct placement. Sometimes the formation is fine and the pump is the bottleneck — undersized, worn, or set at the wrong depth. A properly sized submersible can transform the situation.
  • A booster pump for irrigation. On large-lot and equestrian properties, a booster system can deliver the pressure you need for pastures, arenas, and landscaping even when the well's natural pressure is modest.

Safety First: What to Check Before Assuming the Worst

Before you spend money on any major well work, rule out the simple stuff. A surprising number of "my well is dying" calls turn out to be an equipment problem above the water table. Check these first:

  • The pump. A failing or aging submersible can mimic a low-yield formation. Listen for short-cycling or a pump that runs constantly.
  • The pressure tank. A waterlogged tank with a failed bladder causes erratic pressure and rapid pump cycling.
  • The pressure switch. A dirty or worn switch can shut the system down or hold pressure too low.
  • Leaks. A leak in the line, fittings, or a stuck-open irrigation valve can drain pressure and make a healthy well look weak.
  • A flow test. The definitive step is a professional flow test that measures how much water your well actually produces and how fast it recovers. That single number tells us whether the formation is the problem at all.

If the pump, tank, switch, and plumbing all check out and a flow test confirms the formation itself is under-producing, then it is time to look at hydrofracturing (for bedrock wells) or the redevelopment options above (for alluvial wells).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure to reopen natural fractures in shallow bedrock. There are no chemicals and no sand proppants, and it does not target deep shale formations the way oil-and-gas fracking does.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Pedley well?

It depends on your formation. If your well is drilled into bedrock on higher ground, it may be a good candidate. If it draws from alluvial sand and gravel on the valley floor near the Santa Ana River, it usually is not — there is no rock to fracture. Your driller's log and your property's location are the best way to tell.

How do I find out whether my well is in bedrock or alluvium?

Check your well completion report (driller's log), which lists the materials drilled through. "Sand," "gravel," and "clay" indicate alluvium; "granite," "bedrock," or "fractured rock" indicate a fracturable formation. If you can't find the log, the California Department of Water Resources keeps these records on file, and we can help interpret it.

What does hydrofracturing cost compared to a new well?

For a qualifying bedrock well, hydrofracturing typically runs about $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well in this area runs roughly $18,000 to $42,000. That large gap is why fracturing is worth exploring first when the formation supports it.

What if my well isn't a good candidate for fracturing?

For alluvial wells, better-fit solutions include well redevelopment and cleaning, screen or casing repair, deepening the well, a pump upgrade, or a booster pump for irrigation. These options usually restore capacity at a lower cost than fracturing or drilling.

How long does hydrofracturing take and how soon will I see results?

The treatment itself usually takes about four to eight hours. We measure flow before and after, and verification testing is typically completed within 24 to 48 hours so you can see the actual improvement.
