How Your Well Pressure System Works

A private well system depends on three parts working together: a pump in the well, a pressure tank, and a pressure switch. The pump pushes water into the tank, where a captive cushion of air keeps it under pressure so flow stays steady between pump cycles, typically across a 40/60 psi range. The switch starts the pump at 40 psi and stops it at 60. On a steep grade like Golden Hill, elevation is a major factor: every foot of rise between the tank and a fixture costs about 0.43 psi, so a top-floor bathroom in a tall older home sees noticeably less pressure than a ground-floor tap. Add the narrow, corroded galvanized pipe common in homes a century old, and pressure can drop well below comfortable.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Golden Hill

  • Corroded galvanized plumbing: The most common culprit in Golden Hill's historic homes. A century of internal rust narrows pipes and strangles flow.
  • Elevation and head loss: Steep lots and multi-story Victorians lose pressure to vertical lift unless the system is sized for it.
  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: A ruptured bladder or lost air charge causes short-cycling and swinging pressure.
  • Misadjusted or failing pressure switch: A switch set to 20/40 instead of 40/60 directly lowers household pressure.
  • Worn or undersized pump: A tired pump cannot build pressure; units past 10 to 15 years are prime suspects.
  • Hidden leaks: Old supply lines crack and leak, bleeding off pressure before water reaches the tap.

What You Can Check Before You Call

A few simple checks help you describe the problem and can sometimes solve it. Put an inexpensive pressure gauge on an outdoor spigot; a reading well below 40 psi points to a pump, tank, or switch issue. Listen for a pump that rapidly clicks on and off, the classic sign of a waterlogged tank. Check whether weak pressure affects every fixture or just one; a single slow faucet is usually a clogged aerator or a corroded line to that fixture. Notice if upper floors are worse than lower ones, a sign of elevation and undersized supply. Watch your water meter with everything off; a creeping dial means a hidden leak. None of this requires special tools.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my Golden Hill water pressure so low in a historic home?

Many Golden Hill homes still have original galvanized steel pipe that corrodes and narrows from the inside over a century, throttling flow. Repiping with PEX or copper, together with checking the pump, tank, and switch, usually brings pressure back.

Can a booster pump fix my low pressure?

If the supply and tank are healthy but elevation or a tall, multi-story home is robbing pressure, a booster pump can add 20 to 30 psi where you need it. If the real problem is corroded pipe or a failing pump, we fix that first.

What is the ideal water pressure for a home?

Most homes run best at 40 to 60 psi. On steep lots and in tall homes we account for elevation when setting the pressure switch or specifying a booster so upper fixtures still get adequate flow.

How do I know if I have a hidden leak?

Turn off every fixture and watch the water meter; if the dial keeps moving, water is escaping somewhere. Damp spots, a constantly running pump, or a sudden jump in your bill are other signs. We can pinpoint and repair it.

Do you service both wells and home pressure systems?

Yes. We diagnose and repair the whole chain from the well and pump to the pressure tank, switch, and household plumbing, and we recommend only what your system actually needs.
