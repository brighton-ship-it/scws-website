What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Jacinto Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for San Jacinto Water Wells: An Honest Local Guide

Living in San Jacinto means depending on groundwater that sits beneath a genuinely complicated landscape. The city anchors the San Jacinto Valley in the heart of Riverside County, with the rugged San Jacinto Mountains rising to the east, Hemet just to the southwest, and the Valle Vista and Soboba areas filling out the surrounding country. The San Jacinto River threads across the valley floor, and the land tells two very different stories depending on exactly where your well sits. That distinction matters enormously when someone asks whether hydrofracturing is the right fix for a struggling well, and being honest about it is the only way to give a homeowner an answer they can trust.

At Southern California Well Service, we have spent more than 30 years working wells across the Inland Empire, and we will tell you upfront: hydrofracturing is a powerful tool for the right well and a poor choice for the wrong one. This guide explains how the process works, which San Jacinto wells benefit and which do not, and what to do when hydrofracturing is not the answer.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing, sometimes shortened to hydrofracking, is a well-rehabilitation method that uses nothing but clean water under high pressure. There are no chemicals involved, which makes it fundamentally different from the oil-and-gas fracking that makes headlines. Here is the sequence in plain terms.

A technician lowers inflatable packers into the well casing to seal off a target section of the borehole. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated zone at pressures that typically run from 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure does two useful things at once: it flushes out the silt, mineral scale, and fine debris that have clogged the natural cracks feeding the well, and it gently widens existing fractures and connects them to other water-bearing seams nearby. The result, when conditions are right, is a network of cleaner, more open pathways that let groundwater move into the well far more freely than before. A typical treatment runs roughly four to eight hours, and a same-day flow comparison usually tells us whether it worked.

The single most important thing to understand is this: hydrofracturing works on fractured rock. It opens and cleans cracks in solid stone. It does almost nothing useful in loose, unconsolidated material like sand and gravel, because there are no fractures to open. There is nothing rigid to crack apart, only granular sediment that simply shifts. This is exactly why San Jacinto is such an interesting and divided service area.

Two San Jacinto Geologies, Two Different Answers

The valley floor and the surrounding foothills are not the same beast, and the right repair depends on which one your well draws from.

Valley-Floor Wells: The Alluvial Basin

Most of the city of San Jacinto sits on the valley floor, which is a sediment-filled basin, a structural trough that has slowly collected layers of young and old alluvium over a very long time. Beneath these properties you find sand, gravel, silt, and clay rather than solid rock. This is the San Jacinto groundwater basin, and the eastern portion of it is formally adjudicated and managed by the Hemet-San Jacinto Watermaster, so pumping here is also a regulated matter.

For a well finished in this loose alluvial material, hydrofracturing is generally a weak fit. There simply are no bedrock fractures to clean or expand. When an alluvial-basin well slows down, the cause is almost always something else: the perforated screen has become encrusted with iron, manganese, or calcium scale; fine sand is migrating into the well and choking it; the regional water table has dropped during dry stretches; or the pump and screen have simply aged out. Pumping high-pressure water into sand does not create lasting new flow paths and can even draw more sediment in.

If your well is on the valley floor, the honest and effective remedies are different ones. Well and screen rehabilitation can mechanically and chemically scrub incrustation off the screen and restore the original open area. Deepening the well or drilling a new, properly screened well can reach a more productive zone when the water table has genuinely fallen. And when the water comes up but carries sand or hard minerals, the fix lives at the surface: sediment filtration, or iron and manganese treatment, rather than anything done downhole. We would rather send you toward the repair that works than sell you a treatment that will not.

Foothill and Bedrock-Margin Wells: Fractured Granite

Now move uphill. The parcels that climb toward the San Jacinto Mountains, out past Valle Vista, and along the higher margins of the valley sit on a very different foundation: fractured granitic and crystalline bedrock. Wells drilled into this hard rock do not draw water from a sponge of sand; they draw it from the cracks and fissures running through the stone itself. The yield of one of these wells is only as good as the network of fractures it happens to intersect.

For these foothill wells, hydrofracturing is a strong candidate and often the single best option. If a bedrock well was disappointing from the day it was drilled, or if its fractures have silted closed over the years, high-pressure water can reopen those existing cracks, clear the debris packed inside them, and link the borehole to water-bearing seams it missed before. This is precisely the scenario the technique was invented for, and it is where San Jacinto owners see the most dramatic improvements.

What to Check Before You Call

You can gather useful clues yourself before any technician arrives. A few practical things to note:

  • Where does your property sit? Roughly speaking, valley-floor and lower city lots point toward alluvial remedies; higher foothill parcels toward bedrock and hydrofracturing.
  • Pull your well completion report. The original driller's log records the depth and whether the well was finished in rock or in sand and gravel. This single document usually answers the bedrock-versus-alluvium question outright.
  • Track the symptom pattern. Sediment in the water or a screen that whistles points to incrustation or sand intrusion. A well that pumps clean but simply runs short, especially a foothill well, points toward low fracture yield.
  • Note the timing. A slow, year-over-year decline reads differently than a sudden drop, which can signal a pump or pressure-switch problem rather than the aquifer.

When to Call a Professional

Diagnosing a well from the surface is genuinely hard, and guessing wrong is expensive. If your water has slowed to a trickle, if the pump is short-cycling, if you are seeing sand or rusty staining, or if you are weighing a costly new well, that is the moment to bring in a licensed contractor. A proper diagnosis starts with a flow test and, where useful, a downhole camera survey that shows the casing condition and, in a bedrock well, the fracture zones themselves. Only then can anyone honestly say whether hydrofracturing, rehabilitation, deepening, or surface treatment is the right call. Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing help my San Jacinto Valley well?

Honestly, probably not if your well sits on the valley floor in the alluvial basin. Hydrofracturing works on fractured rock, not on loose sand and gravel, and most low-flow problems down on the valley floor come from screen incrustation, sand intrusion, or a falling water table. Those call for rehabilitation, deepening, or treatment instead. If your well is up in the foothills in granitic bedrock, then yes, it is often an excellent candidate. The completion report and a flow test tell us which situation you are in.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure. It is not the chemical-laden process used in oil and gas extraction, and it does not introduce anything into your drinking water.

How long does the process take?

The high-pressure treatment itself usually runs about four to eight hours. We measure flow before and after, and the improvement on a successful bedrock well is typically clear within a day or two.

How much can my well yield improve?

On a suitable fractured-rock well, owners often see flow climb to several times the pre-treatment rate. We are deliberately cautious about promising a specific number, because the gain depends entirely on the fracture network we are able to reach and clean.

Is hydrofracturing cheaper than drilling a new well?

For a bedrock well, almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000. Trying the lower-cost option first is sensible when the geology supports it. For an alluvial well where hydrofracturing will not help, the comparison instead favors rehabilitation or treatment.

Will my well need to be treated again later?

Possibly, over a span of years. Fractures can slowly silt up again, just as a screen can re-incrust, so some wells benefit from periodic maintenance. A good flow baseline makes it easy to know when that day arrives.
