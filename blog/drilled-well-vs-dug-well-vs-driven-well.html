Drilled vs Dug vs Driven Well

When installing a well, understanding the differences between drilled, dug, and driven wells helps you choose the right type for your property. In Southern California, drilled wells are the overwhelming choice due to depth requirements, geology, and regulatory standards. Here's what you need to know about each type.

Drilled Wells: The Modern Standard

Drilled wells are created using rotary or percussion drilling rigs that bore deep into the earth, typically 100-700+ feet in Southern California.

How Drilled Wells Work

  • Drilling rig bores a hole 6-10 inches in diameter
  • Steel or PVC casing is installed to prevent collapse
  • Annular space around casing is sealed with grout
  • Submersible pump is installed hundreds of feet down
  • Sanitary well seal prevents surface contamination

Advantages of Drilled Wells

  • ✓ Can reach deep water sources (200-700+ feet)
  • ✓ Higher yields – typically 10-50+ GPM
  • ✓ Better water quality from protected deep aquifers
  • ✓ Less susceptible to surface contamination
  • ✓ Not affected by seasonal water table changes
  • ✓ Meets California well construction standards
  • ✓ Longer lifespan (30-50+ years)
  • ✓ Can be drilled through rock formations

Disadvantages of Drilled Wells

  • Higher upfront cost: $20,000-$50,000 complete installation
  • Requires professional drilling contractor
  • Longer installation time (typically 3-7 days)
  • Requires heavy equipment and good site access

Best For

Drilled wells are ideal for: Permanent residential water supply, properties where water table is more than 50 feet deep, areas with bedrock or hard geology, any property in San Diego, Riverside, or San Bernardino counties (virtually required by regulations).

Dug Wells: The Old-Fashioned Method

Dug wells are large-diameter holes (3-10 feet across) excavated by hand or with a backhoe, typically 10-30 feet deep, lined with stone, brick, or concrete.

How Dug Wells Work

  • Excavated with shovels, backhoe, or bucket auger
  • Lined with stacked stone, concrete rings, or tile
  • Extends just below the water table
  • Water seeps in through the bottom and lower sides
  • Covered with a well cap or pump house

Advantages of Dug Wells

  • Lower cost: $3,000-$10,000 if conditions are ideal
  • Can be dug by hand if water table is shallow
  • Large diameter provides significant water storage
  • Simple construction in suitable locations

Disadvantages of Dug Wells

  • ✗ Can only access shallow water (usually <50 feet)
  • ✗ Highly susceptible to surface contamination
  • ✗ May run dry during drought or heavy use
  • ✗ Limited yield – often just 3-10 GPM
  • ✗ Turbid (cloudy) water common
  • ✗ Difficult or impossible in rocky soil
  • ✗ Not permitted for new construction in most of California
  • ✗ Safety hazard during construction (cave-ins)
  • ✗ Shorter lifespan, prone to collapse

Best For

Dug wells are suitable for: Emergency or backup water supply, properties with very shallow water table (10-20 feet), temporary water needs, areas with sandy/gravelly soil free of rock. NOT recommended in Southern California due to depth requirements and regulations.

Driven Wells: The Middle Ground

Driven wells (also called sand point wells) are created by driving a small-diameter pipe with a screened point into the ground, typically to depths of 15-50 feet.

How Driven Wells Work

  • 1.25 to 2-inch diameter pipe with screened "well point"
  • Driven into ground with a sledgehammer or mechanical driver
  • Sections of pipe added as it's driven deeper
  • Shallow jet pump or pitcher pump extracts water
  • Water enters through screen at the point

Advantages of Driven Wells

  • Relatively inexpensive: $500-$3,000 DIY, $2,000-$8,000 professional
  • Quick installation (hours to a day)
  • No heavy drilling equipment required
  • Can be relocated if first attempt fails
  • Useful for temporary water needs

Disadvantages of Driven Wells

  • Limited depth capability (usually <50 feet)
  • Only works in soft soil – can't penetrate rock
  • Low yield – typically 3-10 GPM maximum
  • Susceptible to contamination (shallow depth)
  • May go dry during drought
  • Sand and sediment problems common
  • Not permitted for permanent residential use in most CA counties
  • Shallow pumps limit efficiency

Best For

Driven wells work for: Temporary construction water, seasonal irrigation needs, properties with shallow sandy aquifers, backup/emergency water. Rarely permitted for permanent residential use in Southern California.

Why Drilled Wells Dominate Southern California

In San Diego, Riverside, and San Bernardino counties, over 95% of new wells are drilled wells for good reasons:

  • Deep water tables: Water is typically 150-600+ feet deep, far beyond the reach of dug or driven wells
  • Rocky geology: Granite and hard rock formations require rotary drilling – can't be dug or driven
  • California Well Standards: State regulations (Bulletin 74-90) essentially require drilled well construction for permanent residential use
  • Health department requirements: Counties require proper casing, grouting, and sanitary seals – difficult to achieve with dug/driven wells
  • Reliable year-round supply: Shallow wells (dug/driven) often fail during California's drought cycles
  • Better water quality: Deep aquifers provide cleaner water with less contamination risk

Which Well Type Is Right for Your Property?

If you're in Southern California and need a permanent residential water supply, a drilled well is your only practical option. The depth to water, geology, and regulatory requirements make dug and driven wells unsuitable for permanent use.
