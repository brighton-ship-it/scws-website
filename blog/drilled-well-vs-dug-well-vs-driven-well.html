Understanding Well Construction Types

⛏️

Drilled Well vs Dug Well vs Driven Well

Not all wells are created equal. The method used to construct your well significantly affects water quality, reliability, maintenance needs, and lifespan. Whether you're buying a property with an existing well, troubleshooting problems, or planning a new well, understanding these three major well types is essential. Here's what you need to know.

⚡ Quick Answer

Drilled wells are the modern standard—deeper, more reliable, better protected from contamination. Dug wells are older, shallow, wide wells that are more vulnerable to problems. Driven wells are the simplest and cheapest but only work in limited conditions. For most properties, drilled wells are the only practical option.

Drilled Wells: The Modern Standard

Drilled wells are constructed using rotary or percussion drilling equipment that bores through soil, rock, and geological formations to reach deep aquifers. They're the standard for modern residential water supply.

How Drilled Wells Are Constructed

  1. Site preparation: Drill rig is positioned, permits verified
  2. Drilling begins: Rotary or air percussion cuts through formation
  3. Casing installed: Steel or PVC pipe lines the borehole
  4. Grout seal: Cement fills space between casing and borehole
  5. Screen placement: Allows water entry at producing zone
  6. Development: Pumping clears drilling debris, maximizes yield
  7. Pump installation: Submersible pump lowered into casing
  8. Surface completion: Sanitary well seal, protective casing

Drilled Well Advantages

  • ✓
    Deep water access: Can reach water hundreds of feet below surface
  • ✓
    Excellent contamination protection: Sealed casing prevents surface water entry
  • ✓
    Reliable water supply: Deep aquifers less affected by drought
  • ✓
    Higher yields: Can produce 5-50+ GPM typically
  • ✓
    Long lifespan: 30-50+ years with proper construction
  • ✓
    Works anywhere: Can penetrate any geology with right equipment
  • ✓
    Meets all codes: The only type permitted in most areas today

Drilled Well Disadvantages

  • ✗
    Higher cost: $15,000-$50,000+ for complete system
  • ✗
    Requires professional drilling: Can't be DIY
  • ✗
    Pump service requires pulling: Submersible pump access is difficult
  • ✗
    Depth uncertainty: May drill deeper than estimated before finding water

Dug Wells: The Traditional Approach

Dug wells (also called bored wells) are excavated by hand or machine to shallow depths. They were the standard before modern drilling technology and many still exist on older properties.

How Dug Wells Are Constructed

  • Excavation: Hole dug by hand, shovel, or backhoe to water table
  • Lining: Stacked stone, brick, concrete rings, or tile installed
  • Bottom: Often left open or has gravel for water entry
  • Cover: Concrete cap or wooden cover (often inadequate)
  • Depth: Usually stops shortly below water table (10-30 feet)

Identifying a Dug Well

Signs you have a dug well:

  • Large diameter opening (3-6+ feet across)
  • Concrete ring or stone lining visible
  • Shallow depth (typically under 30 feet)
  • Jet pump or shallow well pump (not submersible)
  • Older property, well predates 1970s
  • May have visible water surface when looking down

Dug Well Advantages

  • ✓
    Lower construction cost: Less expensive than drilled wells
  • ✓
    Can be self-constructed: Historically dug without professionals
  • ✓
    Large storage capacity: Wide diameter holds significant water
  • ✓
    Easy pump access: Jet pump on surface is accessible

Dug Well Disadvantages

  • ✗
    High contamination risk: Shallow depth, large opening, poor sealing
  • ✗
    Drought vulnerable: Shallow water table drops in dry periods
  • ✗
    Lower yields: Limited by water table recharge rate
  • ✗
    Safety hazard: Large opening risks falls, drowning
  • ✗
    Bacteria problems: Surface water infiltration common
  • ✗
    Not permitted: Can't construct new dug wells in most areas

⚠️ Important for Dug Well Owners

If you have a dug well, test water quality annually for bacteria and nitrates. Inspect the well cover and sealing regularly. Consider upgrading to a drilled well for improved safety and reliability, especially if you're experiencing water quality issues or quantity problems.

Driven Wells: The Simplest Type

Driven wells (also called sand point wells or well points) are the simplest well type—essentially a pointed screen attached to pipe that's driven into the ground.

How Driven Wells Are Constructed

  • Well point: Steel pipe with screened point attached
  • Driving: Point pounded into ground with post driver or sledge
  • Pipe additions: Sections added as point goes deeper
  • Depth: Driven until water-bearing sand is reached
  • Pump: Suction (jet) pump pulls water through screen

Driven Well Limitations

  • Soil requirement: Only works in sandy, unconsolidated soil
  • Depth limit: 25-50 feet maximum (suction pump limitation)
  • Can't penetrate rock: Stops if hitting hardpan or bedrock
  • Small diameter: 1.25-2 inch pipe limits yield

Driven Well Advantages

  • ✓
    Lowest cost: $1,000-$4,000 complete
  • ✓
    Quick installation: Can be done in a day
  • ✓
    DIY possible: Simple enough for handy homeowners
  • ✓
    Minimal equipment: No heavy machinery needed

Driven Well Disadvantages

  • ✗
    Very limited application: Only sandy soils with high water table
  • ✗
    Shallow depth: Maximum 50 feet, usually less
  • ✗
    Low yield: Typically 1-5 GPM
  • ✗
    Screen clogging: Sand point clogs over time
  • ✗
    Short lifespan: 10-25 years typical
  • ✗
    Contamination risk: Shallow, vulnerable to surface pollution
  • ✗
    Not permitted: Illegal for domestic use in most jurisdictions

How to Identify Your Well Type

If you're unsure what type of well you have, here's how to determine it:

You Have a DRILLED Well If:

  • • Casing is 4-8 inches diameter
  • • Well depth is 100+ feet
  • • Sealed wellhead at surface
  • • Submersible pump inside well
  • • Well log exists from drilling
  • • Steel or PVC casing visible

You Have a DUG Well If:

  • • Opening is 3-6+ feet across
  • • Depth is under 30 feet
  • • Stone, brick, or concrete rings
  • • Jet pump on surface
  • • Can see water surface
  • • Property is older (pre-1970)

You Have a DRIVEN Well If:

  • • Pipe is 1.25-2 inches diameter
  • • Depth is under 50 feet
  • • Small pipe sticking from ground
  • • Suction/jet pump used
  • • Sandy soil area
  • • Low water yield

If you're buying a property, request the well completion report (well log) which documents construction details. Learn about reading well logs to understand what they show.

When to Upgrade or Replace

Signs Your Old Well Needs Replacement

  • Repeated bacteria contamination despite treatment
  • Running out of water during dry seasons
  • Structural deterioration (caving, crumbling lining)
  • Unable to meet household water demands
  • Water quality problems you can't resolve
  • Safety concerns with open or poorly covered well

Upgrading from Dug/Driven to Drilled

When upgrading, you'll typically:

  1. Drill a new well nearby (not through the existing one)
  2. Test the new well for yield and quality
  3. Connect plumbing to the new well
  4. Properly abandon the old well (required by code)
  5. Optional: Repurpose old well for irrigation only

Proper abandonment prevents the old well from becoming a contamination pathway. See our well abandonment cost guide for details.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between drilled, dug, and driven wells?

Drilled wells use rotary or percussion equipment to bore through rock to 100-1000+ feet. Dug wells are excavated by hand or machine to 10-30 feet and lined with stone or concrete. Driven wells use a pointed screen hammered into the ground to 25-50 feet. Each accesses water differently and has distinct advantages.

Which type of well is best?

Drilled wells are best for most modern residential use—they reach deeper, more reliable water sources, have better contamination protection, and last longer. Dug and driven wells can work in areas with high water tables but are more vulnerable to drought, contamination, and have shorter lifespans.

How can I tell what type of well I have?

Check well depth and casing: Drilled wells have narrow casings (4-8 inches), are deep (100+ feet), and have a sealed wellhead. Dug wells are wide (3+ feet diameter), shallow (under 30 feet), often lined with concrete rings. Driven wells have small diameter pipe (1.25-2 inches) and are shallow with a sand point at bottom.

How much does each well type cost?

Costs vary by depth and location: Driven wells are cheapest ($1,000-$3,000) but rarely used today. Dug wells cost $3,000-$10,000 if hand-dug. Drilled wells cost $15-$50+ per foot, totaling $15,000-$50,000+ for a complete modern system. Drilled wells are the only option for most California properties.

Are dug wells safe?

Dug wells have higher contamination risk because they're shallow, wide, and access surface water. They're more vulnerable to bacteria, nitrates, pesticides, and surface pollutants. With proper construction and maintenance they can be safe, but require more frequent testing and treatment than drilled wells.

Can I convert a dug well to a drilled well?

Yes, but it's typically better to drill a new well nearby rather than convert. Drilling through an existing dug well is complicated and risks collapse. Most well drillers recommend abandoning the old dug well properly and drilling fresh. The old well can sometimes be repurposed for irrigation.

Why are drilled wells the standard today?

Drilled wells reach deeper, more reliable aquifers; have superior contamination protection from sealed casings; produce more water; work in any geology; meet modern health and permit requirements; and last 30-50+ years. They're the only type that meets code in most jurisdictions today.

How long do different well types last?

Drilled wells typically last 30-50+ years with proper maintenance. Dug wells may last 30+ years structurally but often require rehabilitation or deepening sooner. Driven wells have the shortest lifespan at 10-25 years due to screen clogging and limited depth. All well types require pump replacement periodically.
