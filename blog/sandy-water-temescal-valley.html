Sand in Your Temescal Valley Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Temescal Valley well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Temescal Valley well.

Sand and Grit in Temescal Valley Well Water: A Local Guide

Temescal Valley stretches along the Elsinore Trough in western Riverside County, a long fault-bounded graben running between the Santa Ana Mountains to the southwest and the Perris block to the northeast. Communities like Horsethief Canyon, Sycamore Creek, Dos Lagos, and the rural stretches along Temescal Canyon Road sit atop a mix of valley alluvium in the low ground and decomposed granite on the surrounding slopes. Water has been pulled from this valley for well over a century — the first artesian wells here were drilled around 300 feet deep back in the 1880s before the water table dropped and pumping became necessary. That long history and layered geology are exactly why sand or grit appearing at your tap is worth taking seriously.

Wells in Temescal Valley draw from the Temescal groundwater subbasin, part of the larger Upper Santa Ana Valley system. Where a well sits in sandy valley fill, it is drawing water through loose, mobile sediment; where it reaches into fractured and decomposed granite along the hillsides, weathered granitic sand is often present too. In both settings, the well screen and the pump's setting depth are what keep that sand out of your water. When those defenses weaken, grit follows the water straight into your home.

How Sand Gets Into Your Water

A healthy, properly constructed well should not produce sand. When it does, the cause is usually one of these:

  • A failing well screen. The screen holds back the surrounding sand pack while admitting water. In Temescal Valley's alluvial and granitic zones, corrosion or erosion can widen the slots over the years, and once that barrier gives way, sand pours in.
  • A pump set too low. Sediment settles to the bottom of the borehole over time. A submersible pump hanging near that layer stirs it up and delivers it to your faucets on every cycle.
  • An oversized or worn pump over-drawing the well. Many valley and hillside wells are modest producers. A pump that moves more water than the aquifer can supply creates high velocity at the intake, pulling sand through the screen and out of the formation.
  • A dropping water table. Western Riverside County sees long dry summers and recurring drought. As groundwater levels fall — a pattern documented in this valley since its earliest wells — a pump may begin drawing from sandier zones it never touched before.
  • Casing corrosion or a breach. A hole or cracked joint in aging steel casing lets sand enter directly, bypassing the screen. Older Temescal Valley wells are especially prone to this.

Common Scenarios in Temescal Valley

Certain patterns repeat in this terrain. Homes on the valley floor near Temescal Wash and the Sycamore Creek area often have wells in sandy alluvium; these tend to show sand first when heavy summer demand pulls the water level down. Properties up the slopes toward Horsethief Canyon and the Santa Ana foothills more often have fractured-rock or decomposed-granite wells with modest yields, where an oversized pump is the usual culprit. And any well that has had a replacement pump dropped in without checking the sediment depth can start producing grit simply because the new intake now sits in the settled material at the bottom of the hole.

The valley's long groundwater history matters here too. Because levels in the Temescal subbasin have risen and fallen with drought cycles and regional pumping for more than a century, a well that ran clean for years can begin producing sand after a run of dry seasons drops the water table past a sandier layer. That is not a sign you did anything wrong — it is the aquifer changing around a fixed pump, and it is usually straightforward to correct once diagnosed.

What to Check Before You Call

A few minutes of observation speeds up the diagnosis:

  1. Take a settling sample. Run cold water into a clear jar and let it stand. Pale, fine sand points to formation infiltration; rusty flakes suggest casing corrosion.
  2. Note the timing. Sand all the time, only at startup, or worse under heavy use each point to a different cause — settled sediment, screen failure, or over-pumping.
  3. Watch the pressure. Grit that arrives with falling pressure or a short-cycling pump often means the pump is straining against a low water level.
  4. Check filters and the toilet tank. Sand collecting in a filter housing or the bottom of the tank confirms it is coming from the well.

Whatever you do, don't keep running the system in hopes it clears on its own. Sand is abrasive and grinds down a pump quickly, turning a modest repair into a full replacement.

Professional Solutions

The right fix depends on the cause, which is why diagnosis comes first. For Temescal Valley wells we commonly install:

  • Centrifugal sand separators. Mounted at the wellhead, these spin grit out of the flow before it can reach your pressure tank, water heater, and appliances.
  • Spin-down and sediment filters. A flushable spin-down screen catches coarse sand while downstream cartridge filters remove finer material.
  • Pump repositioning. Raising the pump above the settled sediment, or resetting it to the well's true water level, often stops sand at its source.
  • Correct pump sizing. Matching pump output to the well's yield lowers intake velocity so sand is no longer dragged through the screen.
  • Screen and casing repair. Installing a new liner or screen where the original has failed, or sealing and lining a breached casing section.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sandy well water normal in Temescal Valley?

A brand-new well may shed a little sand for its first week or two, which is normal. An established well that suddenly starts producing sand is not — it usually points to a worn screen, a pump problem, or a falling water table, and should be checked.

Will sand hurt my pump?

Yes. Sand is abrasive and wears down impellers, seals, and the housing. Running a submersible pump in sandy water can shorten its life from fifteen-plus years to just a few, so it pays to act quickly.

Why does sand appear more in summer?

Western Riverside County water tables drop during hot, dry stretches. As the level falls, the pump can begin drawing from sandier zones. Seasonal sand often traces directly to water-level changes — a pattern seen in this valley since its earliest wells.

Is a filter enough to solve it?

A sediment filter or separator protects your plumbing and appliances and is often a good first move, but it does not repair a failed screen or a cracked casing underground. A full diagnosis shows whether the well itself needs work.

We just replaced the pump — why sand now?

If the new pump sits deeper than the old one, its intake may now rest in the sediment at the bottom of the hole. Raising or resetting it to the correct depth usually resolves it.
