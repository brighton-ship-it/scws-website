Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Near Me well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts

How Your Avocado Well System Delivers Water to the Trees

Understanding the chain of components helps you spot trouble early:

  1. Pump and casing: A submersible pump lifts groundwater from depths that commonly range from 200 to 600 feet in our foothill terrain.
  2. Pressure tank and switch: These regulate cycling and protect the pump. On a grove that irrigates for hours, they wear faster than on a home.
  3. Storage and boosting: Many growers store water and re-pressurize it with a constant-pressure or booster system to keep drip pressure uniform.
  4. Filtration and treatment: Sediment filters guard emitters; iron, manganese, or chloride treatment protects both trees and hardware.
  5. Drip and micro-sprinklers: The final delivery to the root zone, tuned to tree age and ground slope.

The Problems We Get Called About Most

Across the grower communities we serve, the recurring issues are remarkably consistent:

  • Hard-water scale that crusts on pumps, tanks, and emitters and quietly reduces flow.
  • Rising chloride in aging wells and dry years, showing up first as leaf-tip burn on the trees.
  • Drought-driven yield decline as the water table drops below where a well once drew comfortably.
  • Pressure-switch and tank failure causing short-cycling that burns out motors.
  • Sediment and turbidity from fine granitic material plugging filters and emitters.
  • Electrical faults from rural power fluctuations tripping breakers and stressing motors.

What to Check Before You Call

A quick walk-around helps us help you faster:

  • Watch the pressure switch and tank: rapid clicking or constant cycling means a tank or switch problem.
  • Reset the breaker once; if it trips again, stop and call, because that points to a motor or wiring fault.
  • Compare emitter output across the block to separate a filtration issue from a well issue.
  • Note any new color, smell, or grit in the water.
  • Look at the trees: midday wilt, tip burn, or sudden fruit drop confirm the root zone is starved.

A Seasonal Care Calendar That Keeps Avocado Wells Reliable

The growers who avoid mid-summer emergencies are the ones who service the well on a schedule instead of waiting for a failure. We help our clients build a simple seasonal rhythm. In late winter and early spring, before peak demand arrives, we recommend a full system check: pump amperage draw, pressure-switch operation, tank pre-charge, and a water test for chloride and salinity so any treatment can be in place before the trees are stressed. Spring is also the right time to flush and inspect drip lines and replace worn emitters, because clogged emitters waste water and starve individual trees long before a grower notices uneven canopy color.

Through the hot months, the priorities shift to monitoring. We coach growers to watch run times and flow, since a pump that gradually runs longer to deliver the same water is the earliest warning of a dropping water table or a wearing pump. Keeping a basic log of those run times turns a vague "something feels off" into a precise diagnosis when we arrive. After the harvest, fall is the season for the bigger projects, such as deepening a pump set, installing a constant-pressure system, hydrofracturing a weak well, or adding storage, all done while demand is low and a few days of downtime will not cost a crop. Spreading this work across the calendar protects both the trees and the budget, and it is exactly the kind of planning a local crew that knows your grove can help you put in place.
