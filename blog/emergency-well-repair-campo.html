Why Campo Wells Are Different

Campo groundwater is hard-won. Backcountry wells here historically ran 600 to 800 feet deep, but as the water table has dropped, new and deepened wells increasingly reach 1,200 to 1,400 feet into fractured granite. Groundwater in East County is essentially unregulated, so neighboring properties draw from the same slow-recharging aquifer with no limit — which means water levels can fall during dry stretches and heavy-use seasons. Deep wells mean long pump columns, higher pressure demands, and pumps that work hard, all of which raise the stakes when something fails. This is exactly the terrain we specialize in, and our trucks are equipped for the depths Campo wells demand.

Because these wells are so deep and the aquifer recharges slowly — the water pulled from the deepest Campo borings can be many centuries old — restoring a failed system quickly and correctly the first time matters more here than almost anywhere else in the county. A botched or half-finished repair on a 1,300-foot well is not something you want to discover at 10 p.m. with no water and a long wait for a return trip.

Common Well Emergencies in Campo

  • Burned-out submersible pump — The signature deep-well failure. A pump lifting water 1,000-plus feet and running hard through a dry summer eventually overheats and fails. Replacement runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower — deeper Campo wells trend toward the higher end.
  • Heat-driven drawdown and dry-running — During drought and peak demand, the water level can drop below the pump intake. You get air, sputtering, and eventually nothing. Running a pump dry destroys it, which is why we tell backcountry callers to shut the breaker off the moment they suspect it.
  • Failed pressure switch — Pitted contacts stop the pump from cycling correctly. A $150 to $350 fix, and one we often complete on the spot.
  • Waterlogged pressure tank — A dead bladder causes rapid short-cycling that can burn out a costly deep-well pump. Tank replacement runs $600 to $1,500.
  • Tripped breaker or fried control box — Rural power quality and lightning during backcountry storms take out control boxes and capacitors. Repairs typically run $400 to $900.
  • Sediment and sand — Fractured-rock wells can surge sand that clogs fixtures and wears pumps. Emergency sediment filtration runs $300 to $900.

Before We Arrive: Safe Backcountry Triage

While you wait, check the well pump breaker and reset it once; if it trips again, leave it off — on a deep well, a repeat trip often means a shorted motor. If your water sputtered and quit on a hot afternoon, suspect drawdown and turn the pump breaker off to protect the motor from dry-running until we arrive. Look at the pressure gauge for clues, and confirm no valve was closed. Because you may be far from town, note your tank's remaining water and conserve it for essential use. Never open the well cap or attempt to pull a deep pump yourself.

Protecting a Backcountry Well Before It Fails

When help is 45 minutes or more away, prevention is worth even more in Campo than in town. Because East County wells run deep and share an unregulated aquifer, small problems escalate quickly. A few habits go a long way: watch your water level indirectly by noting whether pressure sags during the hottest part of the day, listen for a pump that runs longer than it used to, and keep an eye on your electric bill — a climbing bill often means a laboring pump. Surge protection is a smart investment given rural power quality and summer lightning. And an annual pump performance check — measuring flow rate and amp draw — lets us catch a declining deep-well pump and schedule replacement on your terms instead of during a July emergency. On a Campo well, a planned replacement can save you days without water and hundreds in avoided damage.
