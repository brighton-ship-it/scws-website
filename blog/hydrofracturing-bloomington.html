What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Bloomington Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Well Service in Bloomington, California

Bloomington is an unincorporated community in San Bernardino County, tucked between Rialto, Colton, and Fontana in the Inland Empire's San Bernardino Valley. It sits above the Rialto-Colton and Riverside-Arlington groundwater area, where a groundwater divide runs beneath the community and the Rialto-Colton Fault forms a nearby basin boundary. The Santa Ana River and its historic washes laid down the deep sand, gravel, silt, and clay that make up this aquifer—layers that locally exceed 1,000 feet thick. That alluvial geology shapes how Bloomington wells are built and which rehabilitation methods will actually help when production falls off.

Southern California Well Service has serviced San Bernardino County wells for more than 30 years. We are straightforward with valley clients about when hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) is the right tool and when another approach fits better—because the loose, water-bearing sands and gravels of the San Bernardino Valley behave very differently from the fractured bedrock found in the surrounding mountains.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing uses clean water and pressure—no chemicals—to reopen the fractures feeding a well:

  • Packers isolate a zone. An inflatable packer is set in the casing or open hole and inflated to seal off the target section so the pressure works on the formation rather than escaping up the borehole.
  • High-pressure injection. Water is pumped at 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI to clear silt and scale from existing fractures and widen the cracks that carry groundwater to the well.
  • No chemicals. Only water enters the well—no additives, no proppant, no risk to water quality.
  • Best in hard rock. The technique excels in fractured granite and crystalline rock. In purely loose sand-and-gravel formations it is generally not the right tool, which is why an honest assessment comes first.

For Bloomington properties whose wells reach fractured bedrock at depth, or for parcels nearer the basin margins and foothills, hydrofracturing can be an excellent option. For wells screened entirely in valley alluvium, we will recommend more fitting solutions such as screen rehabilitation, redevelopment, or pump work.

Why San Bernardino Valley Wells Lose Capacity

Production problems around Bloomington usually trace to causes tied to the alluvial aquifer:

  • Screen and gravel-pack plugging. Fine sand and mineral scale accumulate on the well screen and surrounding gravel pack, throttling flow even when water remains plentiful in the formation.
  • Falling or shifting water levels. Pumping demand and fault-related flow barriers can lower or redistribute groundwater, leaving a shallow-screened well behind.
  • Encrustation and corrosion. Local groundwater chemistry can encrust or corrode screens over time, reducing the open area for water to enter.
  • Pump and equipment wear. In deep valley wells, lower output is frequently a worn pump or dropped pump setting rather than the aquifer itself.

What to Check — Signs Your Well Needs Service

Watch for these signs that your Bloomington well needs professional attention:

  • Output has dropped noticeably from past years or from the original well log.
  • The pump short-cycles, runs continuously, or cannot hold pressure.
  • Sand or sediment appears in the water, hinting at screen or gravel-pack issues.
  • Water levels in the well fall faster during summer demand.
  • House pressure has weakened despite a sound pressure tank and switch.

Pull your well completion report if you can and compare the original tested yield, depth, and screen interval to today's performance—it tells us a great deal before we arrive.

Wells in the San Bernardino Valley vary widely in depth, with the older alluvium locally exceeding 700 feet and reaching far deeper elsewhere in the basin. Knowing your well's depth, screen interval, and construction helps us recommend the right fix—whether that is hydrofracturing a fractured-rock interval at a basin margin, redeveloping a plugged screen, or replacing tired equipment.

The Rialto-Colton and San Jacinto faults that cross this area do more than mark map lines—in many places they act as partial barriers to groundwater movement, which can make water levels on one side of a fault behave very differently from the other. For a well owner, that means a neighbor's strong well is not a guarantee your own will perform the same way, and it underscores why a site-specific assessment beats assumptions. We look at your well's position, depth, and construction together before recommending a course of action.

When to Call a Professional

Well work in this basin is best handled by a licensed contractor who understands both deep alluvial wells and fractured-rock wells. Call when output drops, when sand appears, or before you assume you need a costly new well. We start with a flow and water-level test, inspect the casing and screen—often by camera—and identify the true cause. If your well reaches fractured rock, hydrofracturing may be ideal; if it is a valley-alluvium well, we will recommend the rehabilitation or pump solution that fits. You get an honest plan, not a one-size-fits-all pitch.

Timing matters as well. Addressing a steady decline early—rather than after the well fails during a hot Inland Empire summer—gives us more options and a better chance at a durable result, and spares you the disruption of losing water at the worst time of year.

One more practical note for Inland Empire well owners: sand in the water is a warning sign worth acting on promptly. Beyond being a nuisance at the tap, pumped sand abrades pump components and shortens their life, turning a screen problem into a pump replacement if it is ignored. Catching it early during a diagnostic visit is far cheaper than replacing a sand-damaged pump later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Bloomington well a good fit for hydrofracturing?

It depends on construction. Hydrofracturing works in wells that reach hard, fractured rock. Many valley-floor wells here are screened in sand and gravel, where other rehabilitation methods work better. We assess your well before recommending anything.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure is used. There are no chemical additives, so there is no contamination risk to your drinking water.

My output dropped—is my well failing?

Not necessarily. In deep valley wells, lower output is often a worn pump, a dropped pump setting, or a plugged screen rather than a dead aquifer. A diagnostic visit pinpoints the real cause.

How deep are wells in this area?

Wells in the San Bernardino Valley vary widely, with productive alluvium often several hundred feet thick and deeper in places. Your well log lists the exact depth and screen interval.

Is fixing my well cheaper than a new one?

Almost always. Rehabilitation and pump work cost far less than a new well, which can run $18,000 to $42,000 or more, so we diagnose and repair the existing well first whenever possible.
