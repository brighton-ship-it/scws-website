Sand in Your Ontario Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Ontario well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Ontario well.

Sand and Sediment in Ontario Well Water: A Local Guide

If you live in Ontario and your private well has started spitting grit into the kitchen faucet, leaving a fine layer of sand in the bottom of the bathtub, or clouding up the toilet tank, you are dealing with one of the most common and most misunderstood well problems in the Inland Empire. Ontario sits in San Bernardino County on the broad alluvial plain that fans out below the San Gabriel Mountains, and the very geology that gives this area its abundant groundwater is also the reason sand and sediment turn up in so many wells. Understanding why it happens here specifically is the first step toward fixing it for good.

At Southern California Well Service, we have served the region since 2020 pulling pumps, redeveloping wells, and diagnosing sandy water for property owners across Ontario, Rancho Cucamonga, Upland, Montclair, Chino, and Fontana. The aquifer beneath your feet is the Chino Basin, a large alluvial sand-and-gravel groundwater basin fed by the Santa Ana River and by the Cucamonga and San Antonio Creek fan deposits washing down out of the foothills. That loose, granular formation is wonderful for storing water, but it also means a well that is not built, screened, or pumped correctly will draw fine sand right along with the water. This guide explains exactly why Ontario wells produce sand, what damage it causes, how we diagnose it, and how we fix it.

Why Ontario Wells Produce Sand

Most sand problems in Ontario trace back to the same root cause: the Chino Basin is an alluvial aquifer built from sand, gravel, silt, and cobbles deposited over thousands of years by the Santa Ana River and the alluvial fans pouring out of the mountains near Upland and Rancho Cucamonga. Unlike the fractured granite bedrock you find higher up in the San Gabriel foothills, this basin material is unconsolidated. The water-bearing zones are full of fine to medium sand, and only a properly designed and maintained well keeps that sand outside the casing where it belongs. When something changes, the sand comes in.

Here are the most frequent reasons we find sand and sediment in Ontario wells:

  • A worn or failing pump pulling sand — As a submersible pump ages, its impellers and wear rings erode. A pump already passing some sand wears even faster, and the increased clearances let it suck in more sediment with every cycle, creating a destructive feedback loop.
  • An oversized pump over-drawing the well — A pump rated for more gallons per minute than the aquifer can comfortably deliver pulls water through the formation too aggressively. In loose Chino Basin sand, that high velocity drags fine grains straight through the screen and into the casing.
  • A failed or corroded well screen — The screen is the filter that holds back sand. Decades of mineral-rich Inland Empire water can corrode steel screens, open up slots, or rot out sections entirely, and once that happens the formation sand flows in freely.
  • The wrong screen slot size for fine sand — Many older Ontario wells were screened with slot openings too large for the fine alluvial sand in this part of the basin. If the slot size does not match the actual grain size of the formation, the well will pump sand from day one.
  • Excessive drawdown — When the water level inside the well is pulled down too far during pumping, the increased pressure differential across the screen yanks sand out of the surrounding gravel pack. This is common when demand spikes or when a well is run hard during dry months.
  • A new, undeveloped well — A freshly drilled well naturally produces some sand for the first days or weeks until it is properly developed. If a driller skipped or rushed the development step, that sand never settles down on its own.
  • The sandy aquifer itself — Sometimes the formation around a well simply contains more fine sand than the original gravel pack and screen were designed to handle, especially in zones close to the old river channels and creek fans that thread through Ontario.

Because the Chino Basin is alluvial sand and gravel rather than solid rock, sand intrusion is a genuinely real and recurring local risk here in a way it is not in the harder, fractured-rock foothills to the north toward Upland and Cucamonga. That is why a local diagnosis matters so much.

The Damage Sand Does

Sand in your water is never just a cosmetic nuisance. It is abrasive, and it is constantly grinding away at every component it touches. Left unaddressed, even a small amount of sand causes expensive cascading failures throughout your Ontario water system.

  • It destroys your pump. Sand chews through impellers, wear rings, and bearings on a submersible pump. A pump that should last 10 to 15 years can fail in two or three when it is constantly passing grit.
  • It scores valves and fittings. Pressure switches, check valves, and control valves all develop leaks and stick open or closed when sand cuts into their sealing surfaces.
  • It fills your pressure tank. Sand settles in the bottom of the pressure tank, reducing capacity, waterlogging the bladder, and forcing the pump to short-cycle, which shortens its life further.
  • It ruins appliances and fixtures. Water heaters collect sediment that bakes onto the heating elements, while dishwashers, washing machines, faucet aerators, and toilet valves clog and wear out prematurely.
  • It signals a structural problem. A sudden increase in sand can be the first warning sign of a corroded screen or breached casing — a far more serious issue that only gets worse and more costly the longer it is ignored.

How We Diagnose Sandy Water in Ontario

Guessing at a sand problem wastes money. Our approach is methodical, and it starts with measurement, not assumptions. When we come out to an Ontario property, we work through a clear diagnostic sequence to pinpoint exactly where the sand is coming from.

Our diagnostic process

  • Jar test and sand concentration check — We run the water and capture samples to measure how much sand is actually present, letting it settle so we can see the volume and the grain size. Fine, uniform sand points to formation or screen issues; coarser grit can point elsewhere.
  • Amperage draw and pump depth review — We check the pump's amp draw against its rating and confirm how deep it is set. An overworked or improperly set pump shows up clearly in these numbers.
  • Pull and inspect the pump and screen — When warranted, we pull the pump to inspect it directly for sand wear, and we evaluate the condition of the screen and casing.
  • Downhole camera survey — A camera lowered into the well lets us see corrosion, screen damage, casing breaches, and sediment buildup with our own eyes rather than guessing.
  • Flow and drawdown testing — We measure how the water level responds under pumping load to determine whether excessive drawdown is pulling sand from the formation.

How We Fix It

Once we know the source of the sand, we match the solution to the actual problem. There is no single cure for sandy water, and anyone who quotes you a fix before diagnosing the well is guessing. Depending on what we find, the right repair for your Ontario well may be one or a combination of the following.

  • Sand separators and centrifugal filters — A centrifugal sand separator spins sediment out of the water before it ever reaches your pressure tank and plumbing. This is an excellent first line of defense when the formation simply carries some fine Chino Basin sand.
  • Spin-down and sediment filtration — Spin-down filters and graduated sediment cartridges catch the finer particles a separator misses, polishing the water and protecting appliances and fixtures downstream.
  • Correct pump sizing and placement — If an oversized pump is over-drawing the well or a pump is set too low in the sediment zone, we right-size and reposition it so it draws water gently from a cleaner part of the water column.
  • Well rehabilitation and redevelopment — For wells that were never fully developed or have packed off with fine sand and mineral scale, we clean and redevelop the well to stabilize the gravel pack and restore proper flow.
  • Screen repair or relining — When the screen itself has failed, we repair, reline, or replace it, sometimes installing a properly sized liner and gravel pack matched to the fine sand of the Ontario portion of the basin.

Preventing Sand Problems

The best sand problem is the one that never starts. A few sensible habits go a long way toward keeping Ontario wells sand-free for the long haul.

  • Do not oversize your pump. When it is time to replace a pump, size it to what your well can actually deliver, not to the biggest pump that fits. Restraint here protects the formation.
  • Avoid hammering the well during dry months. Spreading out heavy demand — irrigation, filling tanks — reduces drawdown and keeps the formation stable, which matters in Southern California's long dry season.
  • Service your filtration regularly. Sand separators and sediment filters only work if they are cleaned and serviced. A neglected filter quickly becomes useless.
  • Act on the first signs of grit. A little sand today is a warning. Addressing it early — before it wrecks the pump — is far cheaper than a full emergency replacement later.
  • Schedule periodic well checks. A routine inspection catches screen corrosion and rising sand concentrations long before they become failures.

When to Call a Professional

Some sand situations call for immediate professional attention. Call us right away if you notice any of the following at your Ontario property:

  • A sudden increase in sand when there was little or none before — this often signals a screen or casing failure.
  • Sand combined with dropping water pressure, sputtering faucets, or air in the lines.
  • Visible sand accumulating in the pressure tank, water heater, or toilet tanks.
  • A pump that is short-cycling, running hot, or drawing more amperage than normal.
  • Any sand at all in a well that has just been drilled and should have been developed.

Sandy water does not fix itself, and running a pump full of grit is one of the fastest ways to turn a manageable repair into a complete system replacement. A professional evaluation protects your investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a little sand in my Ontario well water normal?

A trace of fine sand can be normal in a newly drilled well for a short break-in period, and the alluvial Chino Basin formation does carry some fine sand. But persistent or increasing sand is never normal in an established well and should be diagnosed before it damages your pump.

Why does my well in Ontario produce sand when my neighbor's does not?

Wells just a short distance apart can tap different zones of the Chino Basin, with different grain sizes and proximity to old river and creek channels. Differences in screen slot size, pump sizing, well age, and development also explain why one well runs clean while another nearby produces sand.

Will a sediment filter alone solve my sand problem?

A filter or sand separator protects your plumbing and appliances, and for many Ontario wells that carry light formation sand it is the right answer. But if the sand is coming from a corroded screen or breached casing, filtration only treats the symptom — the underlying well issue still needs repair.

How quickly can sand damage my pump?

Abrasive sand can dramatically shorten pump life. A submersible pump that should last well over a decade can fail in just a few years when it is constantly passing grit, which is why early diagnosis is so cost-effective.
