When Your Wildomar Well Needs Rehabilitation

Wildomar sits in the Elsinore Valley of southwestern Riverside County, an incorporated city of rolling terrain at roughly 1,300 feet of elevation tucked between Lake Elsinore to the north and Murrieta to the south. Out here, against the backdrop of the Santa Ana Mountains and the Cleveland National Forest, a private well is often the difference between a working homestead and a dry one. But no well lasts forever on its own. Most of the wells we service in and around Wildomar draw from the valley-fill alluvium of the Elsinore groundwater basin, while properties closer to the foothills tap fractured granitic bedrock. Both settings put a well under steady chemical and physical stress, and over a decade or two that stress shows up as lost capacity. The good news is that a tired well is rarely a dead well. Rehabilitation restores most of the performance you have lost, usually for a fraction of the cost of drilling new.

The trick is recognizing the warning signs early, before a slow decline turns into a no-water emergency. Here is what tells us a Wildomar well is ready for attention:

  • Falling yield: The well simply does not deliver what it once did. Your pump runs noticeably longer to refill the pressure tank, and high-demand mornings leave you waiting on water that used to be there instantly.
  • Sand, grit, or silt in the water: Decomposed-granite sand and fine sediment working into your fixtures, filters, or the bottom of the toilet tank is a classic sign that the screen, gravel pack, or borehole wall is fouling or breaking down, common in this corner of the Elsinore basin.
  • Climbing electric bills: When scale and biofilm choke the screen, the pump fights harder against the added resistance and pulls more power to move less water. A creeping power bill with no lifestyle change is often the well, not the utility.
  • New tastes, odors, or staining: A rotten-egg or metallic smell, cloudy water, or fresh rust-colored and black staining on fixtures points to iron, manganese, or iron-bacteria activity that thrives in this region's groundwater.
  • Short-cycling pump: A pump that snaps on and off in quick bursts is reacting to a well that can no longer keep up with demand, a strong hint that capacity has dropped.
  • A dropping static level: Static water levels across much of the Elsinore basin have trended downward in recent years, so a pump that now breaks suction or pulls air may be reacting to a falling water table as much as to a clogged screen. Sorting out which is which is exactly what an inspection is for.

Rehabilitation Methods We Use in Wildomar

There is no single fix that suits every well. The right program depends on how your well was built, how deep it is, what is fouling it, and whether it is set in the soft alluvial valley fill or the hard granitic rock toward the mountains. A well plugged by calcium-carbonate scale needs a very different treatment than one choked with iron bacteria or buried in decomposed-granite sand. That is why we diagnose first and treat second. Here are the methods we rely on, in the order we typically apply them.

Video Inspection

Every job starts with a downhole camera. We lower a high-resolution video tool into the casing and watch the entire borehole on a monitor, top to bottom. This tells us the true condition of the casing, the integrity of the screen, where the perforations are clogging, and whether we are looking at scale, biofilm, sediment, or a structural problem like a split or collapsed section. In hard-water country like Wildomar, the camera frequently reveals a glassy crust of calcium-carbonate scale across the screen, or the slick, rust-streaked sheen of iron-bacteria colonies. Seeing the actual problem keeps us from guessing, and it keeps you from paying for a treatment that was never going to work. We can also share the footage so you understand exactly what you are dealing with.

Mechanical Cleaning

Once we know what is on the screen, we knock it loose. Wire brushing scours encrustation off the casing and screen face, while surge blocks and high-pressure jetting drive water in and out of the perforations to break up the hardened buildup that chemicals alone cannot reach. Mechanical cleaning is especially effective against the dense calcium-carbonate scale and the sticky iron-bacteria slime that armor a fouled screen. We usually pair it with chemical treatment, because loosening the deposits physically lets the chemicals penetrate deeper into the gravel pack and the formation beyond.

Chemical Treatment and Acidizing

Chemical treatment dissolves what brushing leaves behind. A controlled acid blend attacks the calcium-carbonate scale, iron, and manganese deposits that plug the screen slots and cement the gravel pack, while specialized biocides and dispersants break apart iron-bacteria biofilm and flush it out. This acidizing step is often the single most cost-effective move we make, and in many Wildomar wells it alone recovers a large share of the lost yield. We size and time the chemistry to the specific fouling the camera revealed, then let it dwell and agitate it so it works through the full thickness of the formation rather than just the casing wall. Proper neutralization and removal afterward protect both your water quality and the aquifer.

Well Redevelopment

Redevelopment pulls the loosened material back out of the well and re-establishes a clean, free-flowing pathway from the aquifer into the casing. Using air-lift pumping and surging, we draw water and debris in and out of the perforations until the discharge runs clear. This step is critical for wells in Wildomar's sandy alluvial zones, where years of pumping migrate fine decomposed-granite sediment into the gravel pack and screen slots. Redevelopment also re-sorts the gravel pack so the finest particles are carried away and the coarser, more permeable material is left behind, which improves flow and cuts down on future sanding.

Screen Cleaning

The screen is the heart of the well, and when its open area is plugged, nothing else matters. Targeted screen cleaning combines focused jetting, brushing, and chemical contact directly at the screen interval to reopen slots sealed by mineral crust, bacterial mat, or packed sediment. On older steel screens we work carefully to avoid stressing already-thinned metal, and the video inspection tells us in advance how aggressive we can safely be. A clean screen with restored open area is what turns a sluggish, short-cycling well back into one that keeps up with your household.

Rehabilitation vs. a New Well: Which Makes Sense?

The honest answer is that rehabilitation is the right call far more often than people expect. A typical rehabilitation in Wildomar runs a fraction of what a new well costs, and when the casing is structurally sound and the aquifer still holds water, restoring the well you already have is almost always the smarter investment. Drilling new in this area means a fresh permit through Riverside County, a drill rig, casing, a gravel pack, development, and a new pump installation, a project that easily climbs into the tens of thousands of dollars. Rehabilitation skips most of that.

A new well earns its keep only in specific situations: when the casing has corroded through or collapsed, when the screen has failed beyond cleaning, or when the static level in your part of the basin has fallen below what your current well depth can reliably reach. Because static levels across the Elsinore basin have been declining, the deepening-versus-new question comes up here more than it used to. We will not push you toward the bigger ticket if the smaller one solves the problem. After the video inspection we give you a straight assessment, with the camera footage to back it up, of whether rehab will bring your well back or whether your money is better spent on new construction.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes wells to lose production?

Wells lose production from mineral scale buildup, biofouling (bacteria), sediment accumulation, or casing deterioration. In the Wildomar area that usually means calcium-carbonate scale from hard groundwater, iron and manganese deposits, iron-bacteria biofilm, or decomposed-granite sand working into the screen. Rehabilitation can often restore flow without drilling a new well.

How much does well rehabilitation cost in Wildomar?

Most rehabilitation projects in Wildomar run between $1,500 and $6,000 depending on well depth and the severity of the problem. A video inspection is $300 to $600, and our $125 diagnostic fee is credited back when you approve rehab work. That is dramatically less than the $18,000 to $42,000 a new well typically costs in this area.

How do I know if I need a new well instead of rehab?

If your casing is sound and the aquifer still holds water, rehabilitation is almost always the better value. A new well makes sense only when the casing has failed, the screen is beyond cleaning, or the static level in your part of the Elsinore basin has dropped below your current depth. Our downhole video inspection settles the question before you spend a dollar on the wrong fix.

Why does my Wildomar well produce sand and grit?

Sand is common in Wildomar's alluvial valley-fill wells, where decomposed-granite sediment migrates into the gravel pack and screen slots over years of pumping. It can also signal a corroding or split screen. Well redevelopment removes the accumulated fines and re-sorts the gravel pack, while the camera tells us whether the screen itself needs repair.

How often should a well be rehabilitated?

Most wells benefit from rehabilitation every 10 to 20 years, or sooner if you notice declining pressure, reduced flow, or changes in water quality. Given the hard water and declining basin levels around Wildomar, a periodic video inspection is the cheapest way to catch scale and biofouling before they cost you capacity.

Will rehabilitation improve my water quality?

Often, yes. Clearing iron-bacteria biofilm and flushing out iron, manganese, and sediment frequently reduces odors, staining, and cloudiness along with restoring flow. If a quality issue persists after rehab, we can recommend appropriate water treatment to address what remains.
