What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Forest Falls Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

What Hydrofracturing Does

Hydrofracturing injects clean water under high pressure into the fractured rock surrounding your well bore. In the hard granite and metamorphic rock of the San Gorgonio high country, the pressure widens and reconnects the natural cracks that carry groundwater, flushes out the sediment and mineral scale plugging them, and opens new pathways into the well. The typical result is a measurable jump in both how fast water enters the well and how quickly the well recovers after the pump runs.

This is not the oil-and-gas "fracking" that makes headlines. Water-well hydrofracturing uses clean water only, with no chemicals and no proppants, and it has been used safely on residential and small-system wells for decades. In fracture-fed mountain wells like those around Forest Falls, where the entire water supply depends on cracks in the rock, it is one of the most effective rehabilitation tools available.

For a community like Forest Falls, where water sometimes has to be hauled in during emergencies and a replacement rig may struggle to reach a narrow canyon driveway, getting an existing well back into service is often far more than a convenience. It can be the deciding factor in whether a property stays livable through a dry summer.

How the Process Works on a Mountain Well

We begin by pulling the pump and running a flow test to measure your well's true starting yield in gallons per minute. An inflatable packer, often paired with a second to bracket a target zone, is lowered into the borehole to seal off the interval we want to treat. Clean water is then injected through that isolated zone at pressures typically between 1,000 and 3,000 PSI, opening the existing fracture network. We frequently treat several zones in sequence, then redevelop and re-test the well to confirm the gain. Most jobs are completed in a single day, generally four to eight hours on site, though steep mountain access can add time.

Why Mountain Wells Lose Yield

Low production in the Forest Falls area usually traces to one or more local causes:

  • Tight or healed fractures. Wells in granite and metamorphic rock rely entirely on cracks for flow, and sediment gradually packs those cracks until the well chokes down.
  • Seasonal snowmelt swings. Mountain groundwater is heavily fed by snowpack, so a string of dry winters can drop levels and shrink a well's productive zone.
  • Mineral scale. Dissolved minerals precipitate over time and constrict the openings feeding the well.
  • A well that came in marginal. On steep lots, the borehole sometimes misses the best fracture set and never produces as well as hoped.

Hydrofracturing addresses each of these, and it is especially effective on the fracture-dependent wells that dominate this terrain.

Signs Your Well May Need Hydrofracturing

  • Production has dropped noticeably compared with previous years.
  • The well runs low or dry during peak demand or late in a dry season.
  • Recovery is slow, taking hours to refill after heavy use.
  • The pump short-cycles, switching on and off every few minutes.
  • A newly drilled well came in below the yield you expected.

What to Check Before You Call

A little observation up front helps us pinpoint the problem and can save you a service call if the cause is simpler than yield:

  1. Rule out the equipment. A waterlogged pressure tank, a failing pressure switch, or a worn pump can all imitate low yield. A pressure switch is a $150-$350 part and a tank typically runs $600-$1,500, both far cheaper than well work.
  2. Note the timing. Trouble only under heavy simultaneous demand points toward yield; an all-day pressure drop more often points toward equipment.
  3. Find your well log. The total depth, static water level, and the rock the driller logged tell us whether your well is the fractured-rock type that responds to hydrofracturing. Mountain wells in this area often run from roughly 150 to 500 feet deep.
  4. Watch the water. Sand, cloudiness, or air spitting at the faucets are useful clues to mention when you call.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires high-pressure pumps, packer assemblies, and the experience to judge how a particular formation will respond, and on steep mountain lots it also takes a crew comfortable working tight access. It is strictly a job for a licensed contractor. We start with a diagnostic visit ($125, credited toward the work if you proceed) so you get answers rather than guesswork. Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing safe for my Forest Falls well?

Yes. It uses clean water only, no chemicals, and has been performed on residential and small-system wells for decades. It does not contaminate the aquifer and, done correctly, does not damage a sound casing.

How much can it improve my yield?

Most wells that respond see a two to five times increase in flow rate, depending on the rock formation, the well's depth, and what is limiting flow.

Will it work on every mountain well?

No. It works best in fractured hard rock, which is exactly what most Forest Falls wells are drilled into. Wells in loose sand or gravel are poor candidates, and we will tell you up front if yours is one.

How long does the job take?

Most residential treatments finish in a single day, usually four to eight hours on site, though steep access may add some time. We verify the result with a flow test, typically within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well up here?

Almost always, and the gap is even wider on a steep mountain lot where a new well is harder to drill. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000-$8,000 versus $18,000-$42,000 or more for a new turnkey well.
