What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Egger Highlands well.

Where Rust Comes From

Rust is iron that has oxidized. It reaches your faucet through a handful of distinct pathways, and identifying the right one is what prevents you from paying for the wrong repair.

Corroding Galvanized Pipe

Many of the original homes in Egger Highlands were plumbed with galvanized-steel pipe. The protective zinc coating wears away over the decades and the steel beneath rusts from the inside out. The classic sign is rusty water on the first morning draw that clears after the tap runs for a minute — proof the rust is sitting in your pipes overnight rather than arriving from the city main.

A Failing Water Heater

When a water heater's sacrificial anode rod is used up, the tank itself begins to rust. If discolored water appears only on the hot side and never the cold, the heater is almost certainly the source. Tanks older than 12 to 15 years are prime suspects.

Iron in Well Water

On a private well, dissolved iron rises from the ground clear and turns orange as it oxidizes in air. A related well problem is iron bacteria — harmless microbes that feed on iron and leave a slimy, reddish buildup in the casing, pump, and pressure tank. Both are common on the granite-fed wells of eastern San Diego County and both require treatment rather than re-piping.

Common Local Scenarios

  • City main work nearby: Hydrant flushing or a water-main repair stirs up sediment that reaches taps for a few hours, then clears on its own.
  • Salt-air corrosion: Egger Highlands sits close to the coast, and the marine environment can accelerate corrosion of older metal plumbing and fittings.
  • Original-construction plumbing: In homes that have never been re-piped, galvanized supply lines corrode unevenly, so rust may appear at only some fixtures.
  • Rural wells to the east: For the county properties we serve outside the city, iron and iron bacteria from groundwater dominate, and treatment equipment is the answer.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Hot only, cold only, or both? Hot-only means the water heater. Both sides means supply piping or, on a well, the source.
  2. Does it clear after running? If yes, the rust is in your pipes. If it never clears, it is coming in continuously.
  3. Whole house or one tap? A single rusty faucet usually means a corroded supply line or clogged aerator at that location.
  4. Check the toilet tank: Orange staining or slime there points to ongoing iron or iron bacteria.
  5. The glass test: Cloudy-orange water that settles clear from the bottom is sediment and rust flakes; uniformly tinted water that stays tinted is dissolved iron from a well.

Solutions That Match the Cause

For City-Water Homes

If corroded galvanized pipe is shedding rust, the durable fix is re-piping the affected runs with PEX or copper. A rusting water heater is replaced, and we recommend keeping the new tank on an anode-rod maintenance schedule given the coastal air. A point-of-entry sediment filter can smooth out occasional discoloration from main flushing.

For Well Water

Treatment is matched to the iron type and level. An oxidizing or catalytic media filter handles clear-water iron; chlorine or peroxide injection followed by filtration tackles iron bacteria and sulfur odor; a water softener can manage low iron together with hardness. If iron bacteria has colonized the well, shock chlorination of the well is the necessary first step.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when rust will not clear after flushing, when it appears on both hot and cold lines throughout the house, when stains keep returning on fixtures and laundry, or when a well changes color, smell, or pressure suddenly. On a well, a sudden brown surge can signal a dropping water table pulling sediment or a deteriorating casing, both of which deserve a prompt look.

Preventing Rust Before It Starts

The cheapest rusty-water problem is the one you head off. For municipal-water homes in Egger Highlands, three habits make the biggest difference. First, flush your water heater once a year to clear sediment and inspect the anode rod, replacing it roughly every three to five years — faster near the coast, where the marine air shortens its life. Second, if your home still has any of its original galvanized supply lines, plan a phased re-pipe before they fail rather than waiting for a leak and water damage. Third, install a simple point-of-entry sediment filter so the occasional discoloration from city main flushing never reaches your fixtures.

For well owners in the rural county we serve, prevention looks a little different. An annual well inspection catches early iron-bacteria growth before it becomes a full colony, verifies the casing has not begun to corrode, and confirms your treatment media still has capacity. Filter media is consumable — greensand, catalytic carbon, and softener resin all wear out — so replacing it on schedule keeps water clear and protects the rest of your system. Catching a problem during a routine visit costs a fraction of an emergency rehabilitation later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Egger Highlands have private wells?

Almost all Egger Highlands homes are on City of San Diego municipal water, so rusty water there is typically a plumbing or water-heater issue. The private wells we service are concentrated in the rural eastern parts of San Diego County.

Is rusty water dangerous?

Iron is not a recognized health hazard at typical levels, but it tastes metallic and stains. If discoloration is sudden, persistent, or paired with a strange odor, stop drinking it until the cause is identified.

Could the coastal air be rusting my pipes faster?

Yes. The marine environment near the coast can accelerate corrosion of older metal plumbing and water-heater tanks, which is one reason South San Diego homes often see rust earlier than inland homes.

Why is only my hot water discolored?

Hot-only rust comes from a corroding water heater. The anode rod is likely exhausted and the tank may need replacement.

How do I tell iron bacteria from ordinary rust?

Iron bacteria leaves a slimy, stringy reddish film, easiest to spot in the toilet tank. Ordinary rust settles as gritty orange particles. Iron bacteria needs shock chlorination; ordinary rust is handled by filtration or re-piping.

Do you serve homes outside the city limits?

Absolutely. Much of our work is on private wells in the unincorporated parts of San Diego County. We test the water and install treatment matched to your specific source.
