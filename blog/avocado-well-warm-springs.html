Avocado and Irrigation Water Demands in This Corner of Riverside County

Avocados are among the thirstiest and most particular crops a Warm Springs grower can plant. A single mature tree can pull 40 to 70 gallons on a hot summer day, and a producing acre commonly needs four to six acre-feet of water across the year. The wine grapes and citrus that grow alongside avocados in the De Luz and Temecula-Murrieta hills have their own demands, but avocados set the bar: they want steady, frequent moisture and they punish any interruption during fruit set and sizing. A well that delivers a strong, dependable flow during the August peak is the difference between a profitable harvest and a stressed grove dropping fruit. That is why grove owners here cannot treat a well as a household afterthought — it is production infrastructure.

How a Well and Irrigation System Actually Works

Understanding the parts helps you describe a problem accurately when you call. A typical Warm Springs agricultural or rural-residential system has several pieces working together:

  • The well casing lines the borehole and keeps it open. In this region wells often draw from fractured granitic bedrock or alluvial basins, with depths ranging widely from about 250 feet to 600 feet or more.
  • The submersible pump sits deep in the water column and pushes water to the surface. It is the workhorse, and in a grove it may run for many hours a day during summer.
  • The pressure tank stores a buffer of pressurized water so the pump is not cycling on and off constantly, which protects the motor.
  • The pressure switch tells the pump when to start and stop based on system pressure.
  • A storage tank — common on groves — lets a modest well fill a large reservoir overnight so you can irrigate at high flow during the day without overdrawing the aquifer.
  • Drip lines and micro-sprinklers deliver water efficiently to each tree, reducing waste and keeping the root zone evenly moist.
  • Filtration protects those emitters from sediment and mineral plugging.

When any one link weakens — a worn pump, a waterlogged tank, a clogged filter — the whole grove notices.

Chloride, Salinity, and Why Avocados Are So Sensitive

Avocados have a low tolerance for chloride and overall salinity in irrigation water, and this is one of the most important water-quality issues for Warm Springs growers. Elevated chloride accumulates in the leaves and shows up as tip burn, marginal leaf scorch, and reduced yield over time. Because wells here pull from varied geology, chloride and total dissolved solids can differ from one property to the next. If testing shows your water is on the high side, the practical responses include blending well water with a lower-salinity source, applying a deliberate leaching fraction to flush salts below the root zone, choosing salt-tolerant rootstock when replanting, and monitoring with regular soil and leaf-tissue tests. We can test your well water for the avocado-critical parameters and help you build a plan around the numbers rather than guessing.

Common Well Problems We See Around Warm Springs

After three decades in this terrain, certain issues come up again and again on rural Riverside County properties:

  • Declining yield during drought — as the regional water table drops in dry years, a well that was once adequate may no longer keep up with a grove's summer demand. Hydrofracturing can sometimes restore flow in fractured-rock wells.
  • Hard water scaling — mineral-rich groundwater leaves calcium and magnesium deposits that plug emitters, foul pressure tanks, and shorten pump life.
  • Sediment — fine sand and grit from the formation wears pump components and clogs drip systems.
  • Iron and manganese staining — reddish-brown or black staining on fixtures and lines, sometimes with an off taste or odor.
  • Pressure problems — a failing pressure switch or waterlogged tank that makes the pump short-cycle.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick checks can save you a service call or help us arrive prepared:

  1. Confirm the pump has power — check the breaker and any disconnect switch at the wellhead or pressure tank.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge. Is it reading zero, stuck low, or swinging wildly? Note the number.
  3. Tap the pressure tank near the top and bottom. A tank that sounds full of water all the way up is likely waterlogged.
  4. Listen for the pump. Does it run constantly, cycle rapidly on and off, or never start at all?
  5. Check for obvious leaks at the wellhead, tank fittings, and along irrigation lines.
  6. If you have a sediment or media filter, see whether it is plugged or overdue for service.
  7. Note any recent changes — new staining, cloudy water, air spitting from emitters, or a sudden drop in flow.

Write down what you find. Those details help our technician diagnose the problem faster and often narrow it down before we are on site.

When to Call a Professional

Some situations should go straight to a licensed contractor rather than a do-it-yourself attempt. Call us if you have no water at all, if you smell burning at the pressure switch or control box, if the well is producing sand or air, if water quality has changed noticeably, or if your grove's flow is dropping during the irrigation season. Anything involving pulling the submersible pump, working inside the casing, electrical work at the pump, or assessing a declining aquifer calls for the right equipment and a C-57 licensed well contractor. Guessing with a deep agricultural well is expensive and sometimes dangerous.

Warm Springs Well Service FAQ

How deep are wells around Warm Springs?

Depths vary widely with the local geology. Wells in this part of southwestern Riverside County often draw from fractured granitic bedrock or alluvial basins, and completed depths typically range from about 250 feet to 600 feet or more. The right depth depends on where the productive water-bearing fractures or sand layers sit on your specific parcel.

Is my well water safe for avocados, given chloride sensitivity?

It depends on your water chemistry. Avocados are sensitive to chloride and salinity, so we recommend testing before you rely on a well for grove irrigation. If chloride or total dissolved solids are elevated, options include blending, applying a leaching fraction, and selecting salt-tolerant rootstock. We can test your Warm Springs well for the parameters that matter most to avocados.

Why does my grove's water flow drop in late summer?

During hot, dry stretches the regional water table can decline just as your trees demand the most water, so a well that was adequate in spring may struggle by August. We can evaluate whether the issue is the aquifer, the pump, or the system, and in fractured-rock wells hydrofracturing sometimes restores meaningful yield.

Do you handle drip and micro-sprinkler irrigation, not just the well?

Yes. We service the whole system — casing, submersible pump, pressure tank and switch, storage tanks, filtration, and the drip lines and micro-sprinklers that water your trees. Keeping emitters clear of sediment and scale is part of protecting both your yield and your pump.
