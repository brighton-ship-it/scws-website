What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Chino Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Chino's Agricultural Basin

The city of Chino sits on the floor of the Chino Groundwater Basin in southwestern San Bernardino County, a flat, historically agricultural valley that for generations was defined by its dairies and row crops. The basin is one of the largest in Southern California, holding roughly six million acre-feet of storage, and much of the central valley floor is Pleistocene alluvium that has long supported high-yield wells. While Chino's neighborhoods are largely on district water today, the dairy preserve, the agricultural parcels on the city's southern and eastern edges, and properties along the Santa Ana River corridor still depend on private and irrigation wells. When one of those wells loses output, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) can be an effective way to recover yield, particularly for wells that draw from the firmer, fractured material at the basin margins rather than the loosest alluvium.

It is worth being candid about geology here. Wells screened in clean, loose sand and gravel on the open valley floor are not the textbook hydrofracturing candidates that fractured-rock wells are. But many Chino-area wells touch firmer, partially cemented or fractured zones where the treatment opens real gains, and a proper assessment, including a video inspection and a baseline flow test, is what tells the two situations apart before any money is spent.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation process powered entirely by clean, high-pressure water. No chemicals are used, which matters in the Chino Basin, where decades of dairy and agricultural activity have left a legacy of nitrate concerns in shallow groundwater. The process runs like this:

  • Packers isolate a zone. Inflatable packers are lowered into the casing and inflated to seal off a chosen section of the well so the pressure concentrates on the target.
  • High-pressure injection. Clean water is pumped into that zone at pressures generally between 1,500 and 3,000-plus PSI, flushing accumulated silt and mineral scale out of existing fractures and openings.
  • Fractures open and connect. The pressure widens fractures and can link the borehole to nearby water-bearing zones, enlarging the volume of formation feeding the well.
  • Development and testing. Afterward we surge and pump the well to clear loosened material, then run a flow test to verify the gain.

Why Chino Wells Lose Yield

On the Chino Basin floor, several local causes commonly reduce a well's output:

  • Sediment and fines plugging. In an alluvial basin, fine sand and silt steadily migrate into the screen and the formation immediately around the well, throttling flow.
  • Mineral scaling. Hard groundwater leaves calcium and iron deposits across the intake and screen openings.
  • Iron bacteria and biofouling. A long agricultural history means many older wells develop biological films that slowly clog the intake area.
  • Basin water-level changes. The Chino Basin is intensively managed, and shifting pumping and recharge can lower the water column around a well that once produced freely.
  • Aging well construction. Many wells in this long-farmed area are decades old, and over time their screens corrode and partially collapse, restricting the flow path even when plenty of water remains in the formation.

Signs Your Well May Need Hydrofracturing

These signs, especially several together, point to a yield problem rather than an equipment fault:

  • A gradual, year-over-year decline in how much water the well delivers.
  • The well falls short during summer irrigation or livestock watering, then recovers overnight.
  • The pump cycles on and off more often than it used to.
  • Air spits from the faucets when several fixtures or sprinklers run at once.
  • Increasing sand or grit in the water, suggesting the formation is shedding fines into the well.
  • A well that simply cannot keep up with a growing operation the way it did a few years ago.

First rule out the inexpensive culprits. A failing pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a worn pressure switch can each mimic a low-yield well and cost a fraction as much to repair.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Note the pattern. A shortage that appears only under heavy summer demand and clears in cooler months usually means a recovery-rate problem, which hydrofracturing can address.
  2. Test the pressure tank. If it feels solid with no air cushion, the bladder or air charge has likely failed, a cheap fix unrelated to the well.
  3. Account for demand. More livestock, expanded irrigation, or a new household member can outrun a well that was always near its limit.
  4. Watch the water. Sand or cloudiness can mean fines are moving through the formation and collecting at the intake.
  5. Locate your well log. The original driller's report showing depth, screen intervals, and water-bearing zones helps a technician judge whether hydrofracturing fits your well.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure equipment, properly sized packers, and the experience to read a particular formation. It is not a homeowner task. Reach out to a licensed well contractor when output has clearly fallen, when a flow test confirms production below your needs, or when you are weighing rehabilitation against the larger cost of drilling a new well. A professional starts with a casing inspection, usually by camera, both to confirm the casing is sound and to judge whether your well draws from material that will respond to the treatment. In the Chino Basin, that inspection often distinguishes a true low-yield well from one suffering simple screen plugging or pump trouble.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil and gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water to reopen existing fractures and clear the area around a well. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no deep shale formations. It is a long-established well-rehabilitation method, completely separate from oil and gas extraction.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Chino well?

It depends on what your well is drilled into. Wells that touch firmer, fractured material respond well, while wells screened purely in loose valley-floor sand may benefit more from other rehabilitation methods. We inspect the casing and formation before recommending the treatment, so you do not pay for the wrong fix.

How long does the process take?

Most residential jobs finish in a single day. The high-pressure injection itself runs roughly four to eight hours depending on how many zones we treat, and we flow-test afterward, usually confirming results within 24 to 48 hours.

Could the treatment affect my water quality?

Because only clean water is used, the treatment introduces nothing harmful. Given the Chino Basin's agricultural history and known nitrate issues, we recommend testing your water after any well work simply as good practice.

Is it cheaper than drilling a new well?

Almost always. Hydrofracturing runs $3,000 to $8,000, while a new turnkey well in the Chino area runs $18,000 to $42,000 or more. For most low-yield wells, it is the sensible first step before committing to a new well.

How much improvement can I expect?

For wells that are good candidates, yield gains of two to five times are common. Results vary by well, so we measure your baseline flow before treatment and again afterward so you can see exactly what changed.
