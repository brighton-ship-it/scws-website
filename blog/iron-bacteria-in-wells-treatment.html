The Science Behind Iron Bacteria Contamination

Iron bacteria belong to several genera including Gallionella, Leptothrix, and Crenothrix, each producing characteristic biofilms. These microorganisms occupy a unique ecological niche—they oxidize soluble ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) to insoluble ferric iron (Fe³⁺), harvesting energy from the reaction. The ferric iron precipitates within their slimy polysaccharide matrix, creating the distinctive reddish-brown deposits. Unlike sulfur bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide odors, iron bacteria primarily cause clogging and staining. They proliferate in oxygen-transition zones where iron-rich groundwater meets air—exactly the conditions found at water tables and within well casings. Understanding this biology informs effective treatment strategies.

Recognizing Iron Bacteria Infestation Severity

Iron bacteria problems range from minor aesthetic concerns to severe system failures. Early-stage infestations may show only slight rusty discoloration or faint musty odors. Moderate infestations produce noticeable slimy deposits, persistent staining, and gradual pressure reduction. Severe cases feature completely clogged well screens, dramatically reduced yield, and thick gelatinous masses throughout distribution plumbing. Well yield testing combined with video inspection can reveal internal clogging invisible from surface symptoms. SCWS recommends baseline testing when iron bacteria are first suspected to establish severity and guide treatment intensity. Delaying treatment allows biofilm to thicken, making eventual remediation more difficult and expensive.

Professional Shock Chlorination Procedures

Effective iron bacteria treatment requires systematic shock chlorination achieving 200-500 ppm chlorine concentration throughout the entire system. Professionals calculate required chemical volumes based on well depth, casing diameter, and standing water level. Treatment involves introducing concentrated chlorine solution into the well, agitating to distribute chemical thoroughly, maintaining contact for 12-24 hours, and flushing until chlorine levels return to safe limits. Before treatment, the well casing interior may require mechanical brushing to break up established biofilm. Post-treatment testing verifies bacteria elimination and documents baseline conditions. In severe cases, repeated treatments or alternative oxidizers like chlorine dioxide may be necessary.

Long-Term Management Strategies

Single shock treatments rarely provide permanent solutions in heavily affected wells. Ongoing management typically combines iron removal filtration with periodic maintenance chlorination. Oxidizing filters using air injection, chlorine, or ozone convert dissolved iron to filterable particles while creating conditions hostile to bacteria. Continuous low-level chlorination maintains residual oxidant that prevents recolonization. Some well owners install wellhead sanitation access ports for easier periodic treatment. Ultraviolet disinfection systems provide chemical-free bacterial control downstream but don't address biofilm in the well itself. Your ideal long-term strategy depends on iron concentration, bacteria severity, household water usage, and maintenance preferences.

When to Consider Well Rehabilitation

Severely clogged wells may require rehabilitation beyond chemical treatment. Professional well rehabilitation involves techniques like hydrofracturing, surging, airlift pumping, or acidification to restore flow through clogged screen openings. Video inspection reveals biofilm accumulation extent and guides rehabilitation method selection. In extreme cases, screen replacement or even well replacement becomes more economical than rehabilitation. SCWS provides comprehensive well evaluation services including yield testing, water chemistry analysis, and downhole video inspection. This assessment determines whether treatment, rehabilitation, or replacement represents the best value for restoring your water supply. Early intervention when iron bacteria are first detected prevents escalation requiring these more intensive measures.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is iron bacteria and where does it come from?

Iron bacteria are naturally occurring microorganisms that derive energy by oxidizing dissolved ferrous iron into ferric iron. They produce a slimy biofilm that clogs pipes and well screens. These bacteria exist in soil and surface water throughout Southern California and enter wells during drilling, pump installation, or through surface water infiltration. They thrive in environments containing iron and oxygen—conditions common in many well systems. Once established, iron bacteria colonies can persist for years if untreated.

How can I tell if my well has iron bacteria?

Common signs include slimy, reddish-brown or yellowish deposits inside toilet tanks, rusty or orange staining on fixtures, a swampy or oily taste, and reduced water pressure over time. Iron bacteria produce distinctive stringy, filamentous masses that differ from simple iron staining. If you notice oily-looking sheens on standing water or gelatinous buildup in pipes, iron bacteria are likely present. SCWS can perform specific bacterial testing to confirm the diagnosis and assess colony severity.

Are iron bacteria harmful to my health?

Iron bacteria themselves are not pathogenic and don't cause disease in humans. However, the biofilm they produce can harbor disease-causing organisms and create taste and odor problems. The EPA doesn't regulate iron bacteria as a health concern but acknowledges they create conditions favoring other bacterial growth. The slime can also clog well screens and distribution systems, potentially reducing water availability. Treatment is recommended primarily for aesthetic, operational, and system longevity reasons rather than direct health concerns.

Can I treat iron bacteria myself with household bleach?

While bleach (sodium hypochlorite) can kill iron bacteria, DIY treatment often fails because proper shock chlorination requires specific concentrations, contact times, and procedures. Household bleach degrades quickly and may not achieve adequate strength. You must treat the entire system—well casing, drop pipe, pressure tank, and distribution plumbing. Improper treatment can push bacteria deeper into well screen slots where they survive and recolonize. Professional treatment ensures proper chemical concentrations, adequate contact time, thorough flushing, and follow-up verification testing.

How do I prevent iron bacteria from returning after treatment?

Prevention requires minimizing iron bacteria introduction and controlling growth conditions. Ensure wellhead components are properly sealed and sanitized. Never lower used or unsanitized equipment into the well. Consider continuous chlorination or UV treatment for ongoing control. Reducing iron levels through treatment removes the bacteria's food source. Annual water testing helps detect recolonization early. Some wells in heavily affected areas require periodic maintenance chlorination every few years to prevent biofilm rebuilding. SCWS can recommend preventive protocols based on your specific situation.
