How a Pressure Tank Actually Works

A modern pressure tank is not a storage tank in the way most people picture it. Inside the steel shell is a rubber bladder or diaphragm holding water, surrounded by a cushion of compressed air. When your submersible pump pushes water into the tank, that air is squeezed and stores energy. When you open a tap, the compressed air presses back down and drives water through your pipes without the pump running. Only when tank pressure drops to the pump’s cut-in point — typically 40 PSI on a 40/60 system — does the pressure switch tell the pump to refill. That stored, ready-to-use volume between cut-in and cut-out is called drawdown, and it is the whole reason the tank exists: it lets you draw water without the pump cycling every few seconds.

The air side of the tank carries an air pre-charge, set roughly 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure. On a 40/60 switch, that means about 38 PSI of air in an empty tank. When that pre-charge leaks out over the years, or when the bladder tears and lets water flood the air chamber, the tank loses its cushion. That single failure is behind the large majority of pressure-tank service calls we run in Hemet.

Waterlogged and Ruptured-Bladder Tanks

A waterlogged tank is one where water has filled the space that should hold air. With no air cushion left, the tank can only hold a cup or two of usable water, so the pump switches on and off constantly — sometimes several times a minute. This short-cycling is the number-one killer of well pumps, because every start draws a heavy inrush of current and heats the motor. We have pulled perfectly good three-year-old pumps in the Hemet area that burned out purely because a failed tank ran them into the ground.

You can check for a waterlogged tank yourself. Rap on the tank with your knuckles: the bottom should sound solid (water) and the top hollow (air). If it sounds full all the way up, the air is gone. Next, with the pump breaker off and a faucet open to relieve pressure, press the Schrader air valve on top of the tank with a screwdriver. If water sprays out instead of air, the bladder has ruptured and the tank must be replaced — a torn bladder cannot be patched.

Signs Your Hemet Pressure Tank Needs Attention

  • Rapid short-cycling: the pump clicks on and off every few seconds while water runs
  • Pressure that surges and sags: strong flow that suddenly weakens, then jumps back
  • Water hammer: pipes bang when a faucet or toilet valve shuts
  • A tank that feels heavy or full when you rock it: a classic waterlogged symptom
  • Rust weeping at the seams or base: especially on tanks over ten years old
  • Sputtering, air-spitting faucets: a sign the bladder has failed and air is mixing into the water line

Sizing and Drawdown for San Jacinto Valley Homes

Tank sizing is about matching drawdown to your pump’s flow rate, not just counting bathrooms. A pump should run for at least a full minute per cycle to avoid overheating. Undersize the tank and you get short-cycling even with a healthy bladder; oversize it and you simply gain a longer, gentler cushion. For typical Hemet properties:

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: a 20-32 gallon tank (roughly 5-9 gallons of drawdown) suits most 5-10 GPM pumps
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: a 44-86 gallon tank handles higher simultaneous demand and 10-20 GPM pumps
  • Ranch and horse properties toward Sage and Winchester: 86-120 gallon tanks or paired tanks for irrigation and livestock loads

Because Hemet wells run deep — the California Department of Water Resources shows 927 wells on record with an average depth around 516 feet (some past 1,700 feet) — the pumps here work hard and cost real money to replace. A correctly sized tank is cheap insurance for an expensive pump.

Setting the Air Pre-Charge

Every pressure tank needs its air pre-charge checked at least once a year. Turn off the pump breaker, open the lowest faucet to drain all pressure from the tank, then read the Schrader valve with a low-pressure tire gauge. It should read 2 PSI below cut-in. If it is low, add air with a bicycle or compressor pump in short bursts and re-check. If you add air and it bleeds right back out, the tank is leaking through a bad valve core or a failed bladder. This ten-minute check is the single best thing you can do to extend pump and tank life — and if the reading keeps dropping, it is time to call us.

Diagnosis: What We Check and When to Call a Pro

When we arrive for a pressure problem in Hemet, we do not just swap parts. We test the pressure switch, verify cut-in and cut-out against the gauge, check the pre-charge, inspect the bladder, and confirm the pump is actually delivering rated flow. Short-cycling can come from a waterlogged tank, but it can also come from a clogged switch port, a failing check valve, or a pump losing capacity. A homeowner can safely check the air pre-charge and tap-test the tank. Call a licensed contractor when you see water at the air valve, when the pump runs but pressure never builds, when you smell a hot-motor odor at the pressure switch, or when the tank shows rust and weeping. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Hemet pressure tank is waterlogged?

Tap the tank from top to bottom — a healthy tank sounds hollow up top and solid near the base. If it sounds full all the way up, or the pump cycles on and off every few seconds, the air cushion is gone. Pressing the Schrader air valve is the final test: water instead of air means a ruptured bladder and a tank that needs replacing.

What air pressure should my pressure tank have?

The air pre-charge should sit about 2 PSI below your pump’s cut-in setting. On a common 40/60 system that is roughly 38 PSI, checked with the pump off and all pressure drained. If it keeps dropping after you refill it, the tank is leaking and should be serviced.

Why does my well pump keep turning on and off so fast?

Rapid short-cycling almost always means the pressure tank has lost its air charge or the bladder has torn. It can also come from a bad pressure switch or check valve. Short-cycling destroys pumps quickly, so it is worth diagnosing right away.

How long do pressure tanks last in the San Jacinto Valley?

A quality bladder tank lasts 10-15 years on average, though Hemet’s hard, mineral-heavy groundwater can shorten that. Checking the air pre-charge yearly and keeping sediment out of the tank are the best ways to get full life from it.

Can I replace a pressure tank myself?

Swapping a tank involves shutting down the well, relieving pressure safely, matching the pre-charge and switch settings, and re-priming the system. Sizing errors cause short-cycling and premature pump failure, so most Hemet homeowners have us handle it — and we size the tank to your actual pump output, not a guess.
