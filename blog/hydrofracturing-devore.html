What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Devore Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

In Devore, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Devore wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Wells Around Devore and Cajon Pass

Devore sits at the mouth of Cajon Pass in San Bernardino County, around 2,000 feet, where the San Gabriel and San Bernardino mountain ranges come together and the Cajon Wash spills out onto the valley below. It is a semi-rural area of large lots, ranch properties, and homes near Glen Helen, many of them on private wells. The geology here is transitional, which makes an honest assessment essential before any hydrofracturing work. The foothill benches and the rising ground toward the pass are hard, fractured bedrock, while the broad alluvial fan along the wash is sand and gravel. Hydrofracturing helps wells finished in fractured rock and does little for wells set in loose alluvium, so the first thing we establish on any Devore property is which kind of well you actually have.

Why the Formation Determines Everything

A well drilled into the alluvial fan draws water from the spaces between sand and gravel grains; that material already moves water freely, so there is little for high-pressure injection to fracture and the loose material can slough back. Those wells are generally not candidates. A well drilled into the granitic and metamorphic bedrock of the foothills is the opposite case. Water there travels only through fractures, so yield depends on how many water-bearing cracks the borehole intersects, and widening and connecting those cracks with high-pressure water can sharply increase flow. If your Devore well is a few hundred feet into rock on the higher ground, it may be a strong candidate; if it is a shallower well in the wash, it probably is not.

Cajon Pass also shapes recharge. The fracture system in the surrounding rock fills during winter storms and is drawn down through the dry season, so many local bedrock wells are strongest in spring and weakest by late summer. A well that runs short every autumn is often not out of water; it simply cannot move water through its limited fracture network fast enough to keep up. That seasonal pattern is a strong hint that fracturing could help.

Signs a Devore Well Needs Attention

  • Year-over-year decline in production from a well you know is in rock
  • Slow recovery after heavy household, livestock, or irrigation use
  • Seasonal shortages that bite in late summer and ease after winter
  • A newer bedrock well that never delivered the expected flow
  • A pump that short-cycles or pulls the water level down to the intake

Before any treatment we confirm the formation is the limiting factor and rule out equipment. A pump undersized for a ranch, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch can all imitate low yield, and we check those inexpensive culprits first.

The Treatment Process

  1. Baseline flow test to record your current yield as a documented starting point.
  2. Camera survey to check casing condition and locate the best fracture zones.
  3. Packer isolation to seal off a section of open borehole for focused treatment.
  4. High-pressure injection of clean water, typically 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, to clean and extend fractures.
  5. Development and re-testing to flush loosened material and measure the improvement.

Most Devore jobs finish in a single day. We use only clean water, so there is no chemical risk, and we schedule around livestock and irrigation needs where we can.

What to Check Before You Call

A little history speeds the diagnosis. Find out whether your well is in rock or in the wash alluvium, since that largely determines whether fracturing applies. Note whether your shortage is seasonal, which points to a yield limit. Watch the pressure gauge for rapid cycling, a sign of a waterlogged tank, and listen for a pump that runs long and loses prime, a classic low-yield symptom. If you can share your well's depth, any log, and your storage capacity, we can give you a faster and more accurate answer.

Realistic Results and Cost

Where a Devore well is genuinely in fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, typically two to five times the prior flow. A new well in this area runs about $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, while fracturing a suitable existing well usually costs $3,000 to $8,000, with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the job. For a bedrock well that has lost yield, fracturing is the sensible first step before committing to new drilling. We document every result with before-and-after flow testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Devore well in rock or in the Cajon Wash alluvium?

Your well log usually says, and the well's depth, location, and a camera survey tell us the rest. Wells on the foothill benches and higher ground toward the pass are typically in fractured rock, while wells on the alluvial fan tend to be in sand and gravel. We confirm before recommending treatment.

Why doesn't fracturing help an alluvial well?

High-pressure water opens and connects cracks in solid rock. Loose sand and gravel has no fracture network to widen, and the material can collapse back, so the benefit is minimal. We assess the formation first and decline jobs where fracturing can't reasonably succeed.

Does the process use chemicals?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals or additives, so it does not contaminate your groundwater. It reopens the natural fractures already feeding your well.

How much improvement is realistic?

On a qualifying Devore bedrock well, successful treatments commonly yield two to five times the prior flow, with a success rate around 70 to 80 percent. We flow-test before and after so you see the documented gain rather than a promise.

Can an older well be treated?

Age alone rarely disqualifies a well. What matters is sound casing and whether the well is finished in rock. We inspect with a camera first; if both conditions are met, an older Devore well can respond as well as a newer one.

What if treatment doesn't deliver enough water?

About one in five wells gains little, which is why we assess candidacy honestly and test before and after. If a treated well still falls short, we discuss options such as added storage, deepening, or a new well, and help you weigh the costs.
