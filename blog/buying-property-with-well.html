Buying Property with a Well: Complete Checklist & Due Diligence

Updated February 2026 | By Southern California Well Service

Quick Answer: Before buying a home with a well, get a professional well inspection ($300-$500) and water quality test. Check the flow rate (5+ GPM for residential), review well documentation, verify pump age, and check distance from septic. Don't skip this—well problems after purchase can cost thousands.

Get a Professional Well Inspection

Why It's Essential

  • A general home inspector may not adequately assess wells
  • Well problems aren't always visible
  • Repairs can cost $2,000-$15,000+
  • $300-$500 inspection vs. $10,000+ surprise

What a Well Inspection Includes

  • Visual inspection of wellhead and equipment
  • Pump performance test
  • Flow rate measurement (GPM)
  • Pressure tank assessment
  • Electrical connections check
  • Well cap and seal inspection

Water Quality Testing

  • Basic test: Bacteria, nitrates ($50-$100)
  • Comprehensive: Plus minerals, pH, hardness ($150-$300)
  • Full panel: Includes heavy metals, chemicals ($300-$500)

Due Diligence Checklist

Well Basics

  • ☐ Well location identified and accessible
  • ☐ Well depth documented
  • ☐ Well age known
  • ☐ Static water level checked
  • ☐ Well cap in good condition

Flow and Quantity

  • ☐ Flow rate tested (minimum 5 GPM for residential)
  • ☐ Recovery rate acceptable
  • ☐ No history of running dry

Equipment

  • ☐ Pump age and condition (typical life: 10-15 years)
  • ☐ Pressure tank age and condition
  • ☐ Pressure switch working properly
  • ☐ Any treatment systems documented

Water Quality

  • ☐ Bacteria test negative
  • ☐ Nitrates within limits
  • ☐ No concerning contaminants
  • ☐ Hardness level known

Legal/Permits

  • ☐ Well permit on file
  • ☐ Well meets setback requirements
  • ☐ Any water rights documented
  • ☐ No unpermitted modifications

Documents to Request from Seller

Essential

  • Well completion report: Filed with county when drilled
  • Well permit: Original drilling permit
  • Recent water test: Within last year
  • Pump installation date: Receipt or documentation

Helpful

  • Service history and invoices
  • Equipment manuals
  • Treatment system documentation
  • Previous inspection reports

If Documents Unavailable

Contact county Department of Environmental Health—they maintain well records. Well logs can also be searched through state databases.

Red Flags to Watch For

Serious Concerns

  • ⚠️ No documentation exists (unknown well)
  • ⚠️ Low flow rate (under 3 GPM)
  • ⚠️ History of running dry
  • ⚠️ Failed bacteria test
  • ⚠️ Well too close to septic (less than 100 feet)
  • ⚠️ Very old pump (15+ years)

Moderate Concerns

  • Old pressure tank needing replacement
  • Hard water (treatable)
  • Minor equipment issues
  • Well cap needs replacement

Negotiating Points

These findings can support negotiating:

  • Price reduction
  • Seller repairs before closing
  • Credit at closing for repairs
  • Escrow holdback for fixes

Potential Repair Costs

Pump replacement $1,500-$4,000

Deepen existing well $5,000-$15,000
New well (if needed) $15,000-$50,000+

Questions to Ask the Seller

Well History

  • When was the well drilled?
  • How deep is it?
  • Has it ever run dry?
  • Any problems or repairs?

Equipment

  • When was the pump installed?
  • Has it been serviced recently?
  • When was the pressure tank replaced?
  • Any treatment systems? How old?

Water Quality

  • Do you drink the water untreated?
  • Any taste/smell issues?
  • When was water last tested?
  • Any filtration needed?

Usage

  • Does it supply irrigation?
  • Any pressure issues?
  • How many people use it daily?

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check before buying a house with a well?

Flow rate, water quality test, well/pump age and condition, documentation/permits, and distance from septic. Get a professional well inspection—it's worth the $300-$500.

How much does a well inspection cost?

Basic inspection: $300-$500. With comprehensive water testing: $500-$800. Money well spent before a major purchase.

Is it risky to buy a house with a well?

Not if you do proper due diligence. Millions of homes use wells successfully. The key is knowing what you're getting and planning for maintenance.

Who pays for well inspection when buying a house?

Typically the buyer, as part of their inspection contingency. Some sellers may pay as a negotiating point.

What if the well fails inspection?

Negotiate: ask for repairs, price reduction, or credit. In severe cases, you can walk away if you have inspection contingency.
