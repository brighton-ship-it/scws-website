Pressure Tank Service for Fontana Wells

Fontana is located in San Bernardino County at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains, where urban development meets the Inland Empire's alluvial basin. Most private wells here tap into the Rialto-Colton Basin aquifer system — a mix of alluvial deposits and deeper confined aquifers that produce excellent yields but challenge pressure tank systems with hard water and sediment.

Fontana's Well Geology and Pressure Tank Challenges

Fontana properties with private wells typically experience:

  • Alluvial aquifer wells (150-400 feet deep) — unconsolidated sand, gravel, and clay layers from San Gabriel Mountain runoff
  • High total dissolved solids (TDS) — 300-600 mg/L typical, with hardness often exceeding 15 grains per gallon
  • Iron and manganese — common in deeper confined aquifer zones, causing orange staining and sediment buildup in tanks
  • Temperature extremes — summer highs over 100°F stress pressure tank bladders and seals
  • Suburban/light industrial proximity — some wells near older commercial areas show elevated nitrates or VOCs requiring treatment systems that add pressure demand

5 Common Pressure Tank Problems in Fontana

1. Waterlogged Tank (Most Frequent Issue)

Symptoms: Pump cycles on and off every 15-30 seconds, water pressure surges and drops, rapid pump wear, possible electrical odor from frequent motor starts.

Cause: The internal bladder has ruptured or developed a leak, allowing water to compress the air cushion. Without separation between water and air, the tank loses its buffering capacity. In Fontana's hard water, bladders typically fail after 8-12 years.

Fix: Replace the pressure tank. Most Fontana residential wells use 44-80 gallon bladder tanks. Professional replacement costs $500-$900 depending on tank size and accessibility. Well-X-Trol, Amtrol Extrol, and Flexcon tanks perform well in the Rialto-Colton Basin's mineral-rich water.

2. Low Pre-Charge Air Pressure

Symptoms: Pump runs longer than normal, uneven pressure between first and second floor fixtures, tank feels heavy (full of water) even when pump is off.

Cause: Air has leaked from the bladder through the pre-charge valve or a micro-tear. Tanks should be pre-charged to 2 PSI below the pressure switch cut-in setting — typically 28 PSI for a 30/50 switch or 38 PSI for a 40/60 switch.

Fix: Shut off power to the pump, drain all water from the system (open a faucet at the lowest point), then recharge the tank to correct PSI using a standard tire valve and air compressor. If the tank won't hold pressure for more than a week, the bladder needs replacement.

3. Sediment and Iron Buildup Inside Tank

Symptoms: Rust-colored or brown water when first using faucets in the morning, visible sediment in toilet tanks, reduced effective tank capacity (pump cycles more frequently despite tank being correct size).

Cause: Fontana's aquifer contains dissolved iron and manganese that precipitate out inside the pressure tank. Over 5-10 years, this sediment accumulates at the tank bottom, reducing usable volume and potentially clogging the bladder neck.

Fix: Drain and flush the tank annually through the drain valve. For severe sediment issues, install a whole-house sediment filter (20-50 micron) before the pressure tank. Brands like Pentek Big Blue or Rusco Spin-Down filters cost $200-$400 installed and dramatically extend tank life in Fontana's water conditions.

4. Pressure Switch Failure or Corrosion

Symptoms: Pump won't turn on at all, pump won't shut off, erratic cycling, visible corrosion or burn marks on switch terminals, smell of burning plastic.

Cause: Moisture from humid well pit environments or condensation enters the switch housing, corroding electrical contacts. High mineral content in the air near the tank accelerates this. Pressure switches typically last 8-15 years in Fontana before contacts wear out.

Fix: Replace the pressure switch. Standard Square D 30/50 or 40/60 switches cost $35-$65 plus $100-$150 labor. Upgrade to a low-pressure cut-in switch (20/40) if you have elevation changes or second-story fixtures. For ultimate reliability, install a Cycle Stop Valve (CSV) system that eliminates pressure switch cycling entirely.

5. External Tank Leaks or Corrosion

Symptoms: Water pooling under or around the tank, rust stains on tank exterior, wet insulation if tank is wrapped, visible pinhole leaks or seepage.

Cause: Older galvanized steel tanks corrode from external humidity or condensation. Bladder-style composite tanks rarely leak externally unless physically damaged. In Fontana's dry climate, external corrosion is less common than in humid coastal areas, but can occur in damp well pits or garages with poor ventilation.

Fix: Replace the tank immediately. A leaking pressure vessel is a failure waiting to happen and can cause significant water damage. Upgrade to a modern composite bladder tank with epoxy lining — these resist corrosion far better than galvanized steel.

How to Size a Pressure Tank for Fontana Wells

Proper sizing is critical to prevent short cycling, which destroys well pumps. Fontana's alluvial aquifer wells typically produce 10-25 GPM at depths of 150-400 feet. Use this table as a guide:

Pump Size (HP / GPM) Minimum Tank Size Recommended for Fontana
½ HP (10 GPM) 30 gallons 44 gallons
¾ HP (12 GPM) 44 gallons 60 gallons
1 HP (15 GPM) 60 gallons 80 gallons
1½ HP (20 GPM) 80 gallons 120 gallons
2 HP (25+ GPM) 120 gallons 120-200 gallons

Why larger tanks for Fontana: Hard water accelerates pump wear. A larger tank reduces cycling frequency from 20-30 starts per hour to 5-8 starts per hour, extending pump motor life by years. This is especially important for deeper Fontana wells (300-400 ft) where pump replacement costs $2,500-$4,500.

Preventive Maintenance for Fontana Well Systems

Fontana's hard water and high TDS demand proactive maintenance:

  • Monthly: Check pressure gauge with no water running. Should read 40-60 PSI for a 30/50 switch. If it's stuck at 30 or fluctuates wildly, investigate immediately.
  • Quarterly: Run water for 5 minutes and listen for short cycling (pump kicking on every 10-30 seconds). This indicates a waterlogged tank.
  • Twice yearly: Drain 5-10 gallons from the tank's bottom drain valve to flush sediment. In Fontana's iron-rich water, this prevents buildup.
  • Annually: Check pre-charge air pressure with pump OFF and all water drained from the system. Should be 28 PSI (30/50 switch) or 38 PSI (40/60 switch). Recharge as needed.
  • Every 3 years: Professional inspection of tank, pressure switch, check valve, and pump amperage draw. Catch problems before they become emergencies.

Cycle Stop Valves: The Smart Upgrade for Fontana Wells

Many Fontana well owners benefit from upgrading to a Cycle Stop Valve (CSV) system. A CSV maintains constant pressure (50-55 PSI) by slowing the pump when demand is low rather than cycling it on/off.

Benefits for Fontana wells:

  • Eliminates short cycling — pump runs smoothly at variable speeds instead of brutal on/off cycles
  • Extends pump life 3-5x — critical for Fontana's 200-400 ft wells where replacement costs $2,500-$5,000
  • Constant pressure — no more pressure drops when multiple fixtures run
  • Smaller tank — can use a 20-30 gallon buffer tank instead of 60-80 gallons
  • Ideal for irrigation — Fontana properties with landscaping or small vineyards see dramatic performance improvement

Cost: $450-$700 installed with a 20 gal buffer tank. ROI typically achieved within 5-7 years through avoided pump replacements. For properties using 500+ gallons per day, payback can be under 3 years.

When to Replace vs. Repair Your Pressure Tank

Repair if:

  • Tank is less than 5 years old and only needs air pressure adjustment
  • Leak is from a fitting, gauge, or drain valve (not tank body or bladder)
  • Pressure switch is the only failed component
  • Repair cost is under $250 and buys at least 2 more years of service

Replace if:

  • Tank is waterlogged (bladder has failed)
  • Tank is over 10 years old
  • Visible rust, corrosion, or external leaking from tank body
  • Repeated short cycling despite pressure adjustments and pump diagnostics
  • Upgrading pump size or adding water treatment system that exceeds current capacity
  • Sediment buildup is so severe that flushing doesn't restore function

Frequently Asked Questions: Pressure Tanks in Fontana

How long do pressure tanks last in Fontana's water?

Quality bladder tanks (Well-X-Trol, Flexcon, Goulds) last 10-15 years with proper maintenance. Fontana's hard water (15-20 grains hardness typical) and high TDS can reduce this to 8-12 years if the tank isn't flushed periodically. Old galvanized steel tanks may last 20+ years but accumulate sediment that degrades performance.

Why is my pump cycling on/off every 15 seconds?

Classic short cycling caused by a waterlogged tank. The internal bladder has failed, eliminating the air cushion that buffers water demand. This will destroy your pump within 3-6 months if not fixed immediately. Call for service before pump failure adds $2,500-$4,500 to your repair bill.

Can I install a pressure tank myself?

California law allows homeowners to work on their own wells. However, proper installation requires:

  • Shutting down and fully draining the well system
  • Correctly sizing the tank for pump GPM and household demand
  • Setting pre-charge pressure (requires complete water drainage first)
  • Configuring pressure switch cut-in/cut-out settings
  • Pressure testing all fittings and checking for leaks

Professional installation takes 1.5-2 hours and includes a warranty. DIY installations often result in incorrect pre-charge (causing premature pump failure) or undersizing (causing short cycling).

What pressure setting should I use for my Fontana well?

Most residential wells use a 30/50 PSI switch (pump starts at 30, stops at 50). For homes with second-story fixtures, pool fill systems, or properties with elevation changes, upgrade to a 40/60 switch. Pre-charge the tank to 2 PSI below cut-in: 28 PSI for 30/50, 38 PSI for 40/60.

Should I add a water softener if I install a new pressure tank?

Yes, strongly recommended for Fontana wells. Water here typically runs 15-20 grains per gallon hardness (very hard). A softener will:

  • Extend pressure tank bladder life by 30-50%
  • Reduce sediment buildup inside the tank
  • Protect pump seals and impellers from scale
  • Eliminate hard water stains on fixtures

Typical cost: $1,200-$2,200 installed for a whole-house system. ROI through extended equipment life and lower maintenance costs.

Why is there orange/brown water coming from my tank?

Iron and manganese from Fontana's aquifer are oxidizing inside your pressure tank. This is extremely common in the Rialto-Colton Basin. Solutions:

  • Short-term: Drain and flush the tank every 3-6 months
  • Long-term: Install an iron filter or oxidation system ($800-$2,500 depending on iron concentration and flow rate required)

Do I need a larger tank if I add irrigation?

Probably. Fontana properties with landscaping or small vineyards often add drip or sprinkler systems drawing 10-20 GPM. If this causes short cycling (pump kicking on every 1-2 minutes during irrigation), either:

  • Upgrade to the next tank size (e.g., 60 → 80 gal or 80 → 120 gal)
  • Install a Cycle Stop Valve system that maintains constant pressure during high-demand periods

Can I convert my old galvanized tank to a bladder tank?

Retrofit bladder kits exist, but we don't recommend them. Old steel tanks have unknown remaining lifespan, accumulated sediment, and lack modern corrosion protection. A new bladder tank costs only $200-$300 more than a retrofit kit and comes with a 5-7 year warranty.

What size pressure tank do I have?

Look for a label, stamped metal tag, or molded markings on the tank body. Common residential sizes: 20, 30, 44, 60, 80, 120 gallons. If no label exists, measure diameter and height:

  • 44 gallon: ~22" diameter × 38" tall
  • 60 gallon: ~24" diameter × 44" tall
  • 80 gallon: ~24" diameter × 52" tall

Why does my pressure tank sweat or have condensation?

Normal in Fontana's temperature extremes (cold groundwater + hot ambient air = condensation). Not harmful unless it pools and causes rust on steel fittings. Insulating the tank can reduce condensation but makes service access harder and can trap moisture. We typically don't recommend insulation unless the tank is in an unconditioned space subject to freezing (rare in Fontana).

Should I replace my tank and pump at the same time?

Only if both are near end-of-life. If your pump is 15+ years old and the tank just failed, it may make sense to replace both together and save a second service call. However, if the pump is less than 10 years old and running well, just replace the tank. Don't fix what isn't broken.
