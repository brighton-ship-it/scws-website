How a Well System Supplies an Avocado Grove

An avocado grove is one of the most demanding irrigation jobs a private well can take on. A mature tree can drink 40 to 70 gallons on a hot summer day, and a planted acre may need four to six acre-feet of water per year. Delivering that volume, at steady pressure, day after day, requires every part of the system to work together.

The Well and Pump

The well itself is the foundation. Depth, casing condition, and the yield of the water-bearing zone determine how much water you can pull and how fast. Most agricultural wells in San Diego County use a submersible pump set well below the water table. The pump is sized to the well's safe yield and the grove's peak demand, because an oversized pump that outpaces the well will pull air, cycle hard, and burn out early. Matching pump to well is one of the most important decisions in the whole system.

Pressure and Storage

Avocado irrigation rarely runs straight off the pump. A pressure tank smooths out cycling and protects the pump, while a storage tank, often several thousand gallons, lets a modest well refill slowly between irrigation sets and then deliver a large volume on demand. The pressure switch tells the pump when to start and stop. On larger or hillside groves, a constant-pressure or booster system holds even pressure across changing elevations so that every block of trees gets the same flow.

Irrigation Delivery

From storage, water moves to the grove through micro-sprinklers or drip lines. Avocados are shallow-rooted and salt-sensitive, so growers favor systems that apply water frequently and evenly, wet the root zone thoroughly, and allow controlled extra water to push salts down and away from the roots. Filtration protects those small emitters from sediment, and pressure regulation keeps the pattern uniform. A weak link anywhere, undersized pump, failing tank, clogged filter, leaking line, shows up fast as stressed, scorched trees.

Water Quality for Avocados: Chloride, Salinity, Hardness, and Iron

For avocados, water quality matters as much as water quantity. The avocado is one of the most salt-sensitive tree crops grown in California, and the chemistry of your well water can quietly limit yield long before a tree dies.

Chloride and Salinity, the Coastal Concern

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride and overall salinity. This is well established: even modest chloride levels in irrigation water accumulate in avocado leaves and cause the classic tip burn, where leaf edges turn brown and crisp, followed by leaf drop, reduced canopy, and smaller crops. Near the coast, in communities like Pacific Beach and across low-lying parts of San Diego County, seawater intrusion can push chloride and sodium into groundwater, especially where aquifers have been drawn down. A well that tested fine years ago can creep upward in salinity over time. For avocados, chloride in the irrigation water is the single number we watch most closely, and managing it is often the difference between a productive grove and a declining one.

Hardness

Hard water, high in calcium and magnesium, is common throughout the county. Hardness is not toxic to trees, but it scales up emitters, sprinkler heads, and pump components, gradually choking flow and forcing the pump to work harder. In a micro-irrigation system built around tiny orifices, scale is a real maintenance problem.

Iron and Manganese

Iron and manganese show up as rust-colored staining and, when iron bacteria are present, as a slimy buildup that plugs screens, filters, and drip emitters. Beyond the nuisance staining, the biggest practical issue for a grove is clogged irrigation hardware and lost uniformity. These minerals are best handled with targeted filtration or treatment rather than ignored until emitters fail.

Common Local Scenarios and Their Causes

Over decades of service across San Diego County, certain problems come up again and again:

  • Leaf tip burn and salt stress traced back to rising chloride or sodium in the well, often worse in coastal-influenced or heavily pumped aquifers.
  • Pump short-cycling or premature failure from a waterlogged pressure tank, a worn pressure switch, or a pump that is simply too large for the well's yield.
  • Falling pressure and uneven irrigation as scale, sediment, or iron buildup restrict pipes and emitters.
  • Declining yield during drought when the static water level drops and a well that was always marginal can no longer keep up with summer demand.
  • Stained fixtures, odors, and clogged filters from iron, manganese, or sulfur in the water.
  • Electrical faults and tripped breakers from power fluctuations, worn wiring, or control problems at the well head.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick checks can tell you a lot, and may save a service call:

  1. Power. Confirm the well breaker has not tripped and any disconnect at the pump is on. Reset once; if it trips again, stop and call a professional.
  2. Pressure gauge. Watch the gauge. Pressure that swings rapidly or never builds points to a tank or pump problem.
  3. Pressure tank. Tap the tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow on top and solid near the bottom; fully solid usually means a failed bladder.
  4. Filters and screens. Inspect sediment filters and emitter screens for clogging, staining, or slime.
  5. The trees themselves. Widespread leaf tip burn is a strong signal to test the water for chloride and salinity before the next irrigation season.
  6. Recent water test. If it has been more than a year, or if you are near the coast, get a fresh test for chloride, total salinity, hardness, iron, and manganese.

When to Call a Professional

Some issues are genuinely do-it-yourself, like swapping a clogged sediment cartridge. Many are not. Call a licensed well contractor when the breaker trips repeatedly, when you have no water or rapidly dropping pressure, when pumps need to be pulled, when water tests come back high in chloride or salinity, or when you are planning a new grove and need a system designed around your well's true capacity. Pulling a submersible pump and diagnosing aquifer or electrical problems is work for an experienced, properly licensed crew. Frequently Asked Questions

Are avocados really that sensitive to chloride in well water?

Yes. Avocados are among the most chloride- and salt-sensitive tree crops grown in California. Chloride accumulates in the leaves and causes tip burn, leaf drop, and reduced yield. If your trees show browning leaf edges, testing your irrigation water for chloride and salinity should be a first step.

Why does coastal location matter for avocado well water near Pacific Beach?

Coastal and low-lying aquifers can be affected by seawater intrusion, which raises chloride and sodium in groundwater, particularly where the water table has been drawn down by pumping. Because avocados are so salt-sensitive, coastal-influenced wells deserve regular testing and, where needed, blending or extra leaching irrigation to keep salts away from the roots.

How much water does an avocado grove need from a well?

A mature avocado tree can use 40 to 70 gallons per day in summer heat, and a planted acre commonly needs four to six acre-feet of water per year. Your well must be able to meet peak summer demand, often with the help of a storage tank that refills between irrigation sets.

What can I do if my well water is high in chloride or salinity?

Options include blending with a lower-salinity source, applying controlled extra leaching irrigation to flush salts below the root zone, selecting salt-tolerant rootstocks for new plantings, and monitoring with regular soil, leaf, and water testing. We help growers test and build a practical management plan.

How do I know if I need a new pump or just a repair?

Short-cycling, no water, or rapidly dropping pressure can stem from a failed pressure tank or switch, problems that are far cheaper than a pump. Our diagnostic visit pinpoints the cause so you do not replace a pump that does not need replacing. The $125 diagnostic fee is credited toward any repair.

Do you serve coastal San Diego, or only the inland avocado areas?

Both. From our Ramona and Anza offices we serve Pacific Beach and nearby coastal communities along with the inland avocado-growing areas of San Diego County. Same-day emergency service is available throughout our service area.
