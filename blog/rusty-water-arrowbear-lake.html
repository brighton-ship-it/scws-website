Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Arrowbear Lake well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Arrowbear Lake well.

Rusty Well Water in Arrowbear Lake: A Mountain Homeowner's Guide

At roughly 6,000 feet on the Rim of the World Highway, Arrowbear Lake sits in a pocket of the San Bernardino Mountains between Running Springs and Snow Valley, ringed by the pines of the San Bernardino National Forest. Wells up here don't draw from a deep, sandy valley aquifer the way desert-floor wells do. Instead, they pull water from fractured granitic and metamorphic bedrock — narrow seams and cracks in the mountain that fill slowly with snowmelt and groundwater. That geology is exactly why so many Arrowbear Lake homeowners eventually open a tap and see orange, brown, or rust-tinted water.

If your water has turned the color of weak tea or is leaving orange rings in the toilet tank, you're not alone, and the problem almost always has a clear, fixable cause. This guide explains why mountain wells like yours produce rusty water, what you can check yourself, and when it's worth calling a licensed well contractor.

Why Arrowbear Lake Wells Run Rusty

Rusty water is almost always an iron problem, and at this elevation iron has several ways to reach your glass:

  • Iron leached from decomposing granite. The crystalline rock the San Bernardino range is built from contains iron-bearing minerals like biotite and pyrite. As that rock weathers, slightly acidic snowmelt dissolves iron and carries it into the fractures your well taps. Dissolved (ferrous) iron is invisible at the wellhead, then turns orange the moment it hits air or your water heater.
  • Iron bacteria. These naturally occurring organisms thrive in cold, oxygen-poor mountain groundwater. They feed on dissolved iron and leave behind a slimy, rust-colored gel that clogs screens, stains fixtures, and produces a swampy or musty smell. Iron bacteria are common in low-yield fractured-rock wells that sit unused for stretches — exactly the pattern in a community with many second homes and weekend cabins.
  • Corroding steel casing. Many older Arrowbear Lake wells were drilled decades ago with steel casing. Freeze-thaw cycles and naturally corrosive, low-mineral mountain water slowly eat at that steel, shedding rust into the water column.
  • Aging galvanized plumbing. Cabins built in the mid-20th century often still have galvanized steel supply lines. These rust from the inside out and can be the real source of orange water even when the well itself is clean.
  • A failing water heater. A rusting anode rod or tank produces rusty hot water only. If only your hot side is discolored, start here before suspecting the well.

Is Rusty Water Dangerous?

Iron itself is not considered a health hazard at the concentrations typical of mountain wells — it's classified as a secondary (aesthetic) contaminant. That said, rusty water tastes metallic, stains laundry and porcelain, scorches the flavor of coffee and tea, and can slowly choke pressure tanks, water heaters, and fixtures with sediment. More importantly, a sudden change in color can signal something worth investigating: a cracked casing, a new bacterial colony, or infiltration after one of the heavy winter storms that hit this elevation. When iron bacteria are involved, the same conditions can sometimes harbor other organisms, which is why testing matters.

What You Can Check Before Calling

A few minutes of observation will help you — and your technician — pinpoint the source:

  1. Hot, cold, or both? Rusty hot water only points to the water heater. Rust on both sides points to the well or main plumbing.
  2. One fixture or the whole house? A single rusty faucet usually means a corroded fixture or branch line, not a well problem.
  3. Constant or intermittent? Rust that appears after the well has sat idle (returning to your cabin after weeks away) strongly suggests iron bacteria or settled sediment in the casing.
  4. Run an outside spigot. Fill a clear glass from a hose bib upstream of any treatment and let it sit. Iron clouds and settles as orange particles; iron bacteria leave stringy, reddish slime.
  5. Check after storms or snowmelt. Spring runoff can change a shallow fractured-rock well's chemistry quickly. Note whether discoloration tracks the weather.

Proven Solutions for Mountain Iron Problems

The right fix depends entirely on the cause, which is why a diagnosis beats guessing at a softener from a big-box store:

  • Oxidizing/iron filters. For straightforward dissolved iron, an air-injection or catalytic oxidizing filter converts iron to a filterable form and backwashes it away — no salt required, which suits remote cabins well.
  • Water softeners. Where iron comes alongside hardness, a properly sized softener handles modest iron loads and protects appliances.
  • Shock chlorination and well rehabilitation. When iron bacteria are the culprit, disinfecting the well, pump, and lines breaks the colony. Low-yield mountain wells sometimes need this on a periodic schedule.
  • Casing repair or lining. A camera inspection can confirm whether corroded steel casing is shedding rust; lining or replacement stops it at the source.
  • Plumbing upgrades. Swapping galvanized supply lines for PEX or copper ends household-generated rust for good — and PEX tolerates the freeze risk of mountain crawlspaces better than steel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Arrowbear Lake well water suddenly look rusty?

A sudden change usually means one of three things: a fresh iron-bacteria bloom after the well sat idle, sediment stirred up by seasonal snowmelt entering the fractured rock, or corrosion that has progressed in steel casing or galvanized plumbing. A water test plus a look at when the color appears (after travel, after storms, hot vs. cold) narrows it down quickly.

Is the orange water from my mountain well safe to drink?

Iron is an aesthetic issue, not a recognized health hazard at typical levels, so it won't generally harm you — but it tastes metallic, stains everything, and can mask other problems. Because iron bacteria sometimes accompany other contaminants, we recommend a lab test before assuming it's harmless, especially after a change in color or odor.

Will a water softener fix rusty well water?

Sometimes. A softener handles modest dissolved iron along with hardness, but it can be overwhelmed by heavy iron or fouled by iron bacteria. For higher iron levels, a dedicated oxidizing iron filter is more effective and lower-maintenance. Matching the equipment to your test results is the key.

My cabin sits empty for weeks — does that cause rusty water?

Yes. When a low-yield fractured-rock well goes unused, iron bacteria multiply and sediment settles in the casing, then flushes out when you return and run the taps hard. Periodic well disinfection and running the system before heavy use both help.

Could it just be my water heater?

If only the hot water is rusty, the most likely cause is a corroding anode rod or tank, not your well. Flushing the heater or replacing the anode rod often clears it. If both hot and cold run orange, the source is upstream in the well or plumbing.
