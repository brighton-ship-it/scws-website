What Are Tannins?

Tannins are natural organic compounds released when vegetation decays in soil and enters groundwater. They're the same compounds that give tea its color and astringent taste. Wells in areas with swamps, marshes, or heavy forestation often have tannin issues. Shallow wells and those with damaged casings allowing surface water intrusion are particularly susceptible.

Signs of Tannin Contamination

Water appears yellow, amber, or light brown—like weak tea—straight from the tap. Unlike iron discoloration, tannin color doesn't change or intensify with air exposure. Water may have a musty, earthy taste and faintly swamp-like odor. White laundry develops yellow tinge. Tannins also interfere with water softeners and iron filters, reducing their effectiveness.

Testing for Tannins

Standard water tests don't always include tannins, so request this specifically. Results are reported in mg/L, with levels above 0.5 mg/L typically noticeable. Testing should also check for iron and manganese since these often occur together and require different treatment approaches. Color measurement can indicate tannin presence but testing confirms concentration.

Tannin Removal Systems

Anion exchange systems using organic-scavenging resin are most effective, removing 90%+ of tannins. These systems regenerate with salt brine similar to water softeners. For wells with both tannins and iron, install iron treatment upstream—tannins can interfere with iron removal otherwise. Oxidation methods using chlorine or ozone can break down tannins, followed by carbon filtration. Whole-house treatment ensures clear water at every tap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are tannins in well water harmful?

Tannins are organic compounds from decaying vegetation and are not harmful to health. However, they cause unpleasant taste and odor, stain laundry and fixtures, and interfere with other water treatment processes like iron removal.

How do I know if I have tannins vs iron in my water?

Both cause discoloration, but tannin-colored water stays consistently yellow-brown even after sitting, while iron-tinted water often starts clear and turns orange when exposed to air. A water test can definitively identify the culprit.

Will a carbon filter remove tannins?

Standard activated carbon filters are not effective for tannins. Tannin removal requires specialized anion exchange resin systems designed specifically for these organic compounds, or oxidation followed by filtration.
