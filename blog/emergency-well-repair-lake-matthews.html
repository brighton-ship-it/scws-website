The Most Common Well Emergencies in Lake Mathews

After decades of service calls across rural Riverside County, we have learned that well failures tend to repeat themselves. Recognizing the symptoms helps you describe the issue clearly, and it helps us bring the right parts on the first trip out to the canyon.

  • Sudden loss of water with no warning. Taps that worked this morning run dry by afternoon. This is the single most common emergency we respond to, and it has several possible causes.
  • Pump failure or motor burnout. The submersible pump set deep in your well is the workhorse of the system, and motors wear out or burn up after a surge, especially on properties that run irrigation and livestock water hard.
  • Pressure tank failure. When a bladder tank waterlogs or ruptures, the pump cycles on and off rapidly, water pressure collapses, and the pump itself takes a beating.
  • A well that has gone dry or low. In the dry foothill soils around Lake Mathews, a drop in the static water level after a dry winter can leave the pump drawing air.
  • Electrical or control box failure. Heat, dust, rodents, and age all take a toll on the control box and pressure switch that govern the system.
  • Burst pipe or major leak. A cracked line or failed fitting bleeds away pressure and can flood a pump house or well enclosure before you notice.
  • No water after a power outage. When the grid blinks in the canyon, pumps sometimes refuse to restart cleanly, tripping breakers or overload protection.

What to Check First

Before you assume you need a full pump replacement, there are several safe checks any homeowner can do. Many emergencies turn out to be a simpler, less expensive fix, and reporting what you find speeds up the whole visit.

  1. Check your breaker. In your main electrical panel, find the breaker for the well or pump. If it has tripped, flip it fully off and back on a single time. If it trips again right away, leave it off and call us instead of repeatedly resetting it.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. The gauge on your pressure tank or pump should normally read between 40 and 60 psi. A reading near zero suggests a supply or pump problem; a stuck high reading suggests a switch problem.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. Rap your knuckles on the tank near the top and near the bottom. The top should sound hollow and the bottom solid. If the whole tank sounds full and heavy, it is probably waterlogged.
  4. Inspect the pressure switch. The small switch box has two electrical contacts that can burn or corrode. Do not touch them while power is on, but make a note of anything that looks scorched.
  5. Confirm the grid. Make sure the well circuit actually has power and that there is no wider outage affecting the area.

What to Do While You Wait

After you have reached us and a technician is en route, a few simple steps make the repair go faster. If the breaker keeps tripping, leave it off so the pump motor is not fighting a fault. Ration the water already in your pipes and water heater for drinking and essential needs, and hold off on irrigation, laundry, and dishwashing. If you keep horses or other livestock, set aside whatever stored water you have for the animals. Clear easy access to your well head, pressure tank, and pump house so our technician can get to work the moment they arrive, and keep your phone nearby in case we call with questions about the route into your property.
