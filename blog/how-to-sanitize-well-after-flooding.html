Assessing Flood Damage to Your Well

After floodwaters recede, thoroughly inspect your well before attempting to use it. Check the wellhead for damage: is the cap secure, are there cracks in the casing above ground, does surface water pool around the well? Look for debris, mud, or sediment near the wellhead. If the electrical panel or pump controls were submerged, do not restore power until an electrician inspects them—water-damaged electrical components are fire and shock hazards. Document any visible damage with photos for insurance purposes. Even if everything looks fine externally, floodwaters can push contamination down through the soil and into shallow aquifers. Assume contamination and proceed with disinfection regardless of visible damage.

Step-by-Step Shock Chlorination Process

First, calculate your well's water volume (depth x diameter charts available online). Mix the appropriate amount of unscented bleach—typically 2-4 cups per 100 gallons of water. Turn off the pump and remove the well cap. Pour bleach solution directly into the well. Attach a hose to an outdoor faucet and run the other end into the well, recirculating water for 15-30 minutes to mix chlorine throughout. Next, run water at each faucet inside and outside until you smell chlorine—this ensures the entire distribution system is disinfected. Close all faucets and let chlorinated water sit for 12-24 hours. Avoid running water during this contact time.

Flushing and Testing After Disinfection

After the contact period, flush chlorine from your system. Connect hoses to outdoor faucets and run them away from septic systems, plants, and streams—chlorine harms septic bacteria and aquatic life. Flush until chlorine odor disappears, which may take several hours for deep wells. Then run each indoor faucet until chlorine smell clears. Wait 48 hours after flushing before collecting water samples for testing. Collect samples in sterile containers from a treated indoor faucet after running water for several minutes. Submit to a certified laboratory for coliform bacteria testing. Many local health departments offer low-cost testing. Don't resume normal water use until results confirm no bacteria present.

Safety Precautions During Well Disinfection

Concentrated chlorine is hazardous—wear rubber gloves and eye protection when handling bleach. Never mix bleach with other chemicals, especially ammonia-based cleaners, which create toxic gases. Ensure adequate ventilation when working around the wellhead during chlorination. If your pump was submerged, have it professionally inspected before energizing—water damage to motors can cause failures or fires. Keep children and pets away from flushing water and the work area. If you smell chlorine gas strongly at any point, move away immediately and ventilate the area. Never enter a well pit or confined space without proper training and equipment—chlorine accumulates in low areas. Call SCWS if any step feels unsafe or uncertain.

When to Call Professional Well Services

While basic shock chlorination is a reasonable DIY task, certain situations require professional help. Contact Southern California Well Service if: your well pump or electrical components were submerged, repeated chlorination fails to eliminate bacteria, you notice structural damage to the wellhead or casing, sediment or discoloration persists after multiple flushings, you're uncomfortable with any part of the process, or your well is very deep (over 200 feet) requiring specialized equipment. SCWS can perform video inspection to assess casing condition, identify contamination entry points, and ensure proper sealing. After major flooding, professional evaluation provides peace of mind that your water supply is truly safe for your family.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well was contaminated by flooding?

Assume contamination if floodwaters reached within 50 feet of your wellhead, if water rose above the well cap, if your well is shallow (under 50 feet) in a flooded area, or if you notice any changes in water appearance, taste, or smell after flooding. Even without visible flooding at the wellhead, bacteria and contaminants can infiltrate through the saturated ground. Don't rely on clear-looking water—contamination isn't always visible. Test first, sanitize second, and retest before resuming normal use.

What type of chlorine should I use for well disinfection?

Use unscented household liquid bleach (sodium hypochlorite) containing 5-8% chlorine. Avoid bleaches with additives, fragrances, or surfactants—these can introduce unwanted chemicals. Calculate the amount based on your well's depth and diameter: roughly 3 cups of 5% bleach per 100 gallons of well water. For a typical 6-inch diameter well at 100 feet depth, you'd need approximately 1-2 gallons of bleach. Pool chlorine tablets are not recommended as they dissolve unpredictably and may contain other chemicals.

How long should I wait after chlorinating before using the water?

After circulating chlorinated water through your entire system, let it sit for 12-24 hours minimum—overnight is common practice. This contact time ensures chlorine kills bacteria throughout the system. Then flush by running outdoor hoses until chlorine smell disappears (avoid septic systems during flushing). Wait 48 hours after flushing before testing. Don't use the water for drinking or cooking until tests confirm bacteria levels are safe. The entire process—chlorinate, wait, flush, wait, test—typically takes 3-4 days.

Can I drink the water while waiting for test results?

No—use bottled water for drinking and cooking until test results confirm safety. Even if water appears clear and smells normal after disinfection, invisible bacterial contamination may persist. Use well water only for toilet flushing until confirmed safe. If you must use it for bathing, avoid swallowing any water and keep it away from open wounds. This precaution is especially important for children, elderly individuals, and anyone with compromised immune systems. Wait for the all-clear from your lab.

What if my well tests positive for bacteria after disinfection?

If bacteria persist after proper shock chlorination, the contamination source may still be active or your well has structural problems allowing ongoing infiltration. Repeat disinfection once—sometimes a second treatment succeeds. If bacteria return again, professional inspection is essential. SCWS can evaluate your wellhead seal, casing integrity, and surrounding drainage to identify contamination pathways. Chronic bacterial problems often require well repairs, improved surface sealing, or in severe cases, drilling a new well in a safer location.
