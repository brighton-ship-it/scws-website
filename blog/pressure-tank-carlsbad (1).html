Salt Air: Carlsbad's Special Challenge for Well Equipment

Living within reach of the Pacific has a cost for well hardware. The salt-laden marine air that keeps Carlsbad mild is corrosive to steel tank shells, pressure-switch contacts, and exposed fittings. We regularly see Carlsbad pressure tanks with rust blooming at the base seam and switch contacts pitted years before an inland counterpart would show wear. That coastal exposure makes two things especially important here: choosing a quality, well-coated bladder tank in the first place, and inspecting exposed equipment regularly so surface corrosion is caught before it eats through the shell.

Understanding Your Bladder Pressure Tank

A bladder pressure tank works on a simple principle. A rubber diaphragm separates a pre-charged air pocket from the water your pump delivers. Water compresses the air as the tank fills; when you open a tap, the air pushes water back out without the pump running. The pump only restarts when pressure drops to the switch's cut-in point, then shuts off at cut-out. The usable volume in that range is the drawdown, and it is what stands between your pump and a life of constant, damaging cycling. On a coastal well, protecting that drawdown protects a submersible pump that runs $2,500-$5,500 to replace.

How to Spot a Failing Tank in Your Carlsbad Home

  • Short-cycling: the pump turning on and off every few seconds while one fixture runs — the clearest sign of a waterlogged tank.
  • Fluctuating pressure: a strong stream that fades then recovers as the failing tank loses its cushion.
  • Water hammer: pipes knocking when a valve closes.
  • A solid, heavy tank: tap up the side; a solid thud high on the tank plus unusual weight means it's waterlogged.
  • Water at the air valve: press the Schrader valve on top — a spray of water rather than air confirms a ruptured bladder.
  • Coastal corrosion: rust weeping at the base is more common here than inland and signals the shell is failing.

Pre-Charge and Sizing for Carlsbad Properties

The air pre-charge is the number that most often trips up well owners. It should sit 2 PSI below the pump's cut-in pressure — roughly 38 PSI on a standard 40/60 switch, measured with the tank drained and the pump off. A slow leak-down of that pre-charge over the years can perfectly imitate a dead tank; recharging it fixes the problem as long as the bladder hasn't torn.

For sizing, we look at your pump's flow rate and your peak simultaneous demand rather than counting bathrooms. Many Carlsbad homes are comfortable on a 32- to 44-gallon tank, but a larger La Costa property irrigating landscape while the household draws water may need an 86-gallon tank, twin tanks, or a constant-pressure booster system ($2,000-$4,500). Because coastal conditions already stress the tank, sizing it correctly the first time is the best way to avoid premature failures and repeat visits.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  1. Run a single fixture and time the pump; restarts more than once a minute point to the tank.
  2. Watch the pressure gauge for a needle that swings hard between high and low in seconds.
  3. Tap and lift-test the tank for the solid, heavy feel of waterlogging.
  4. With power off and the tank drained, briefly press the air valve — water means a failed bladder.
  5. Inspect the base and fittings for salt-driven corrosion.

Do not attempt electrical repairs. The pressure switch carries live 240-volt power and any wellhead wiring should be left to a licensed professional.

Making a Tank Last Near the Coast

Coastal tanks need a bit more vigilance than inland ones. Once a year, with the pump off and the tank drained, check and top up the air pre-charge to 2 PSI below cut-in. Inspect the base seam and fittings for the first signs of salt corrosion, and address surface rust before it becomes a leak. Keep the pressure switch cover intact so marine air doesn't reach the contacts. If your well water carries sediment or scale, filtration ($300-$900) protects the tank and everything downstream, and a periodic well inspection ($150-$400) catches a fading tank before it fails on you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Carlsbad's salt air really damage pressure tanks?

Yes. Marine air is corrosive, and we see Carlsbad tanks develop rust at the base and pitted switch contacts sooner than inland systems. A quality coated tank and regular inspection of exposed equipment go a long way toward offsetting that coastal wear.

Why does my Carlsbad pump keep cycling on and off?

Rapid short-cycling almost always means the pressure tank has lost its air cushion, either from a ruptured bladder or a leaked-down pre-charge. With no drawdown left, the pump restarts constantly — and that cycling can quickly ruin a pump costing $2,500-$5,500 to replace.

What air pressure should my tank hold?

Set the pre-charge 2 PSI below your pump's cut-in — about 38 PSI on a 40/60 switch, measured with the tank drained. If pressing the air valve releases water, the bladder has failed and the tank must be replaced.

How long do pressure tanks last in a coastal area like Carlsbad?

Generally 10-15 years, though salt-air corrosion can shorten that. Keeping the pre-charge correct, shading exposed hardware, and adding sediment filtration ($300-$900) where warranted help a tank reach the upper end of that range.
