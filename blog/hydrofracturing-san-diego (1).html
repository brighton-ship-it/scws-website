What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Diego Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in San Diego: Where It Fits and Where It Does Not

Within the City of San Diego itself, most homes and businesses are connected to the municipal supply delivered by the San Diego County Water Authority and the city's Public Utilities Department, so a private well is the exception rather than the rule in the urban core. If you live in a denser neighborhood near the coast, Mission Valley, or the inland mesas served by city mains, hydrofracturing is usually not the right starting point. The honest answer for those properties is that low flow or no water is far more often a pump, pressure tank, or plumbing problem than a depleted aquifer. Where private wells truly matter in this region is the county's rural backcountry — the granitic foothills and mountain communities east and north of the city — and that is exactly where hydrofracturing earns its reputation.

We want San Diego property owners to spend money on the right repair. That is why our first visit always begins with a diagnostic, not a sales pitch. If your well sits in fractured bedrock out in the backcountry and the yield has genuinely declined, hydrofracturing may be the most cost-effective fix available. If you are inside the urban service area, the better path is often a pump or component repair, a measured deepening of an older shallow well, or a complete rehabilitation of an aging system.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation method, not a drilling method. A technician lowers an inflatable packer into the borehole and seals off a target zone, then injects clean water under controlled pressure — generally in the 1,500 to 3,000 PSI range, though the exact figure depends on depth and rock type. That pressure flushes the silt, clay, and mineral scale that have plugged the natural seams in the rock, and it can nudge those hairline fractures open wider so groundwater has a clearer path into the casing. In hard crystalline rock like the decomposed granite common to inland San Diego County, a single well can be treated at multiple depths in one day. No chemicals are used; this is the opposite of the oil-and-gas process people picture when they hear the word "fracking."

The result is measured, not assumed. We run a baseline flow test before treatment and a verification test afterward so you can see the before-and-after numbers in writing rather than taking our word for it.

Common San Diego Scenarios and What Causes Them

  • Urban or suburban home on city water with weak flow: This is almost never an aquifer issue. Look first at the pressure switch, the pressure tank, a failing booster pump, or a clogged regulator. Hydrofracturing does not apply.
  • Older shallow well inside the urban fringe: Some legacy properties still rely on a private well drilled decades ago. If the casing is sound but the water table has dropped, deepening the existing well or drilling a new one is frequently the better answer than fracturing a formation that no longer holds water at that depth.
  • Backcountry bedrock well producing less than it once did: This is the classic hydrofracturing candidate. Mineral buildup and fine sediment seal off the fractures that feed the well over time, and high-pressure treatment can reopen them.
  • Brand-new backcountry well with disappointing yield: A freshly drilled hole in granite that only makes a half-gallon per minute is often a good candidate for development by hydrofracturing before the drill rig even leaves the site.

What to Check Before You Call

A little observation saves everyone time. Note these things first:

  • Are you on a well at all? Confirm whether your address is served by city or district water or by a private well — many San Diego homeowners are unsure.
  • Listen to the pump: rapid on-off cycling usually points to a waterlogged pressure tank or bad switch, not the formation.
  • Track the timing: does the flow fade only during peak summer demand, or has it declined steadily year over year? Seasonal drops hint at the aquifer; sudden changes hint at equipment.
  • Check pressure at the tank gauge and compare it to the switch settings (commonly 30/50 or 40/60 PSI).
  • Look for air spitting at the faucets, which can mean the water level is dropping near the pump intake.

Why Backcountry Geology Makes the Difference

The reason hydrofracturing succeeds so often east of the city comes down to rock. The Peninsular Ranges that rise from the coastal plain toward the mountains are built largely of granitic and metamorphic crystalline rock. Wells in this material do not draw from a sandy aquifer that behaves like a sponge; they draw from a network of cracks, joints, and fracture planes in otherwise solid stone. Over years of pumping, those fractures gradually choke with fine clay, weathered grit, and precipitated minerals. The water is still in the formation — it simply cannot move through the clogged seams fast enough to keep up with the pump. High-pressure water reopens that plumbing. That is also why the same treatment does little in a coastal sand-and-gravel setting: there are no discrete fractures to reopen, so the pressure simply dissipates into the loose material.

Understanding this distinction is the single most useful thing a San Diego property owner can take away. Before recommending hydrofracturing, we look at the original well log when one exists, the depth of the casing, and the lithology the driller recorded. If the record shows hard rock and the historical yield once exceeded what you get today, the odds are strongly in your favor.

Frequently Asked Questions

I live in the City of San Diego — should I hydrofracture my well?

Most likely not. The vast majority of city addresses are on municipal water, and even those with private wells usually have flow problems caused by pumps or pressure equipment rather than the aquifer. Hydrofracturing makes sense mainly for bedrock wells in the rural backcountry.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure. It is a rehabilitation technique that reopens natural fractures in rock and does not involve the chemical additives associated with energy-industry fracking.

How long does the process take?

A typical treatment runs four to eight hours on site, with post-treatment flow verification usually completed within 24 to 48 hours.

What yield improvement is realistic?

In San Diego County's decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, suitable wells often see a two- to fivefold increase. Wells in loose sand or gravel are poor candidates and we will say so before any work begins.

What if I am not even sure I have a well?

That is common in the urban area. We can confirm your water source during a diagnostic visit and steer you toward the correct repair — which is frequently a straightforward pump or tank fix, not hydrofracturing.

Is a new well ever the smarter choice?

Yes. If your formation no longer holds water at the current depth, or the casing is failing, a deepening or a new well can outperform fracturing. We give you the numbers both ways so you can decide.
