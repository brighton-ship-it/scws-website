What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Rialto Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Rialto: An Honest Local Assessment

Rialto sits in the heart of the Inland Empire, on the floor of the Rialto-Colton groundwater basin in western San Bernardino County. This roughly 40-square-mile basin is bounded by the Rialto-Colton Fault on the southwest and the San Jacinto Fault to the northeast, and it is filled with hundreds of feet of heterogeneous alluvium — interbedded gravel, sand, silt, and clay washed down from the San Gabriel Mountains. That geology matters, because it shapes whether hydrofracturing is the right tool for your property or whether your money is better spent somewhere else.

Here is the straight answer most Rialto homeowners deserve: hydrofracturing is usually a poor fit for wells completed in the alluvial Rialto-Colton basin. Hydrofracturing works by using high-pressure water to open and clean natural fractures in hard, consolidated bedrock — granite, granodiorite, and other crystalline rock. Loose sand-and-gravel aquifers like the ones beneath most of Rialto do not hold open fractures; the unconsolidated material simply collapses back. If a well driller tells you hydrofracturing will dramatically boost an alluvial-basin well, be skeptical. We would rather tell you the truth up front than charge you $3,000-$8,000 for a treatment unlikely to help.

That said, a small number of Rialto-area properties — particularly older parcels near the basin margins or up against the bedrock of the surrounding hills — do have wells bottomed in or near fractured rock. For those specific wells, hydrofracturing can be worth evaluating. The only way to know which category your well falls into is a proper diagnostic, and that is where we start every job.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

When hydrofracturing is appropriate, the process is straightforward. A crew lowers an inflatable device called a packer into the well casing and inflates it to seal off a target zone. Clean water is then injected behind the packer at pressures that typically range from 500 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure forces open and flushes clean the hairline fractures already present in the surrounding rock, clearing out the fine silt, mineral scale, and drilling debris that slowly choke a well's intake over the years. New micro-channels can also connect the borehole to water-bearing fractures it never reached before.

The whole job usually takes four to eight hours. No chemicals are used — only water — which is why municipal-style water-well hydrofracturing should never be confused with the chemical-laden oil-and-gas fracking you read about in the news. In the right rock, yield improvements of two to ten times are common. In loose alluvium, the technique simply has nothing to grip.

Common Well Scenarios in Rialto — and Better Solutions

Because Rialto is largely served by city water through the City of Rialto and West Valley Water District, true private domestic wells here are relatively uncommon compared with the rural backcountry we serve elsewhere. When Rialto-area customers do call us about a struggling well, the underlying cause is rarely something hydrofracturing would fix. Far more often we find one of the following:

  • A failing or worn pump. Declining flow and short-cycling are usually a pump or pressure-system problem, not an aquifer problem. A submersible pump in heavy use may simply be at the end of its 10-15 year service life.
  • A dropping water table. Decades of pumping and drought cycles in the Inland Empire have lowered groundwater levels in parts of the region. If your pump now hangs above the productive zone, the fix is lowering the pump or deepening the well — not fracturing.
  • Sediment and sand intrusion. Alluvial wells frequently pull fine sand that wears pumps and clouds water. The answer is proper screening, a sediment filter, or well rehabilitation, not high-pressure fracturing.
  • Water quality issues. The Rialto-Colton basin has documented perchlorate and nitrate concerns in some areas, plus hard water rich in calcium and magnesium. These are treatment problems, addressed with filtration or softening, not yield problems.

For the typical Rialto situation, the honest recommendation is one of: a pump replacement, lowering or deepening the existing well, drilling a properly designed new well into the alluvial aquifer, or installing the right water treatment. We will tell you which, and we will not upsell you a fracturing job that won't move the needle.

What to Check Before You Call

A few observations from your end help us diagnose faster and can save you a service visit:

  1. Listen to the pump. Is it cycling on and off rapidly, running constantly, or not starting at all? Rapid cycling often points to a waterlogged pressure tank or a failing pressure switch.
  2. Check your pressure tank. Tap it — the top should sound hollow and the bottom solid. A tank that is heavy and full all the way up has lost its air charge and needs service or replacement.
  3. Note when the problem appears. Low water only during summer irrigation suggests a supply or pump-sizing limit; low pressure all the time points to equipment.
  4. Look for sediment. Sandy or cloudy water at the tap is a strong clue you are dealing with an alluvial-aquifer issue, not a fracture-flow issue.
  5. Find your well records. The original driller's log (filed with the California Department of Water Resources) tells us the well's depth, casing, and whether it is bottomed in alluvium or rock — the single most important fact for deciding if hydrofracturing is even on the table.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing help my Rialto well?

Probably not, if your well is completed in the alluvial Rialto-Colton basin. Hydrofracturing only works in hard fractured bedrock, and most of Rialto sits on sand and gravel. We will review your well log and assess your geology before recommending it — and we will tell you honestly if it isn't the right approach.

My water pressure dropped. Is my well going dry?

Usually not. A sudden pressure drop is far more often a failing pressure switch, a waterlogged tank, or a worn pump than an exhausted aquifer. A diagnostic visit pinpoints the real cause before you spend money on the wrong fix.

What is the better option if hydrofracturing won't work?

Depending on the diagnosis, the right answer is typically lowering or deepening the existing well, replacing the pump, drilling a new properly screened well into the alluvial aquifer, or adding water treatment for hardness, sediment, or contaminants.

Is the water in the Rialto-Colton basin safe to drink?

Parts of the basin have documented perchlorate and nitrate concerns, and the water is generally hard. If you rely on a private well, we recommend periodic testing and can install appropriate filtration or softening to address what the testing finds.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure. It shares a name with petroleum fracking but none of the chemicals.

How much does it cost just to find out what's wrong?

Our diagnostic visit is $125, and that amount is credited toward any repair or service work you authorize, so the assessment effectively pays for itself.
