Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Yermo well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How a Mojave Desert Well System Works

Your well is a chain of dependent parts. Groundwater enters through a screened intake, a submersible pump lifts it up the casing, and a pressure tank governed by a switch holds it under pressure for the home and yard. In the Yermo area, wells often run deep and pumps work hard, because the water table can sit well down the casing and summer demand pushes equipment to its limits in extreme heat.

Mojave groundwater frequently carries elevated total dissolved solids, hardness, and sometimes iron — minerals that scale up pump components, stain fixtures, and clog irrigation. A correctly matched pump, a healthy pressure tank, and the right filtration stage keep delivery steady and water usable. On many Yermo homesteads a storage tank is essential, banking water from a modest well so the household has steady pressure through the heat of the day.

Core Components

  • Submersible pump set deep and sized to the well's real yield
  • Pressure tank and switch to stabilize delivery and prevent short-cycling
  • Storage tank — a near-necessity on remote desert parcels
  • Filtration for hardness, salts, and iron matched to local chemistry
  • Weather-rated electrical and controls built to survive sun, heat, and long service runs

Common Well Scenarios in Yermo

Decades of desert service have shown us the patterns specific to the Mojave around Yermo.

  • Pump overheating and short-cycling in extreme summer heat, often from a waterlogged tank or failing switch
  • Scale and salt buildup that slowly chokes pumps, valves, and emitters
  • Falling water level in dry years that can pull air into an undersized pump
  • High TDS and hardness that stress fixtures and appliances
  • Electrical faults from sun-degraded conduit, rodents, or grid voltage swings in outlying areas

Each leaves a recognizable signature that a desert-savvy technician can read quickly.

Water quality is often the bigger story in Yermo than water quantity. Mojave groundwater can carry enough dissolved solids to taste noticeably salty and to leave a chalky scale on every fixture and appliance it touches. Over months and years, that mineral load shortens the life of water heaters, narrows pipes, and crusts up irrigation emitters. The right answer depends entirely on what a water test reveals — a softener handles hardness, an oxidizing filter handles iron, and a reverse-osmosis stage can polish drinking water — which is why we always recommend testing before specifying treatment rather than installing equipment on a guess.

What to Check Before You Call

A few safe observations speed up the diagnosis and can save you a service visit.

  1. Listen at the pressure tank. Rapid switch clicking usually means the tank has lost its air charge.
  2. Read the gauge. A healthy system cycles between about 40 and 60 psi; a stuck or erratic gauge is a clue.
  3. Check the breaker. A well breaker that trips after a hot afternoon often means the pump drew too much current.
  4. Watch for air or sand spitting from faucets, which points to pump-setting, screen, or casing trouble.
  5. Note whether storage drains on heavy-use days, a sign the well can't keep pace with demand.

Never open the wellhead or touch the pump and wiring yourself — lifting a pump from a deep desert well calls for proper rigging and lockout procedures.

When to Call a Professional

Call us right away if you lose water entirely, if the breaker keeps tripping, if your storage tank empties faster than the well can refill, or if water comes out muddy or full of air. These conditions strain a pump and can destroy a costly motor if the system keeps running. In a Mojave summer, a day without water is a genuine hazard, which is why we run same-day emergency service across the high desert.

How deep are wells in the Yermo area?

Mojave wells vary widely, commonly running from about 150 to 500 feet depending on your position relative to the Mojave River basin and the local water table. A site assessment is the only way to know your exact depth.

Can I grow avocados on well water in Yermo?

Why does my desert water taste salty or leave scale?

That points to high total dissolved solids and hardness, common in Mojave groundwater. Depending on your test results, a softener, filtration, or reverse-osmosis stage can bring the water into a more usable range.

My storage tank runs dry on hot days — what helps?

That usually means a modest well is being outpaced by summer demand. A larger storage tank and a booster pump let the well fill overnight and deliver strong pressure all day; hydrofracturing can also raise a low well's yield.

Protecting a Mojave Water Investment

On a Yermo property, your well is among the most valuable systems you own, and the desert is hard on valuable systems. Heat shortens equipment life, mineral-laden water scales every surface it touches, and the distance from town turns a small failure into a long, dry wait if you are caught unprepared. The homeowners who spend the least over the years are the ones who plan ahead — scheduling checkups, keeping a healthy storage buffer, and addressing minor symptoms before they grow into pump-killing problems.

Water quality deserves the same ongoing attention as water supply in the Mojave. Sediment settling in a storage tank, scale accumulating on a heater, or salts concentrating over a dry season can all degrade your water so gradually that you adapt without noticing. A periodic service visit flushes tanks, inspects filtration, and confirms your treatment still matches what the well produces, because desert water chemistry genuinely shifts as the basin rises and falls through wet and dry years.
