What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Big Bear Lake Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Getting More Water From a Big Bear Lake Well

At roughly 6,750 feet, Big Bear Lake sits on a high granite plateau in the heart of the San Bernardino Mountains — one of the highest year-round communities in Southern California. The bedrock beneath Bear Valley is hard quartz monzonite and granodiorite, and the private wells scattered through Moonridge, Sugarloaf, Fawnskin, Boulder Bay, and the surrounding forest tap water from fractures within that rock. When a Big Bear well starts to fall behind household demand, the cause is usually not a vanishing aquifer but a fracture network that has narrowed or silted over. Hydrofracturing reopens and extends those fractures, and for most mountain homeowners it is the smartest first move before ever considering an expensive new bore.

Rock Well or Valley Fill? It Matters

Big Bear is unusual in that some properties near the valley floor draw from lake and alluvial sediments while properties on the slopes sit squarely in fractured granite. Hydrofracturing is built for the hard-rock wells — the ones drilled into competent granite where water arrives through discrete cracks. If your well draws from loose sand and gravel, fracturing won't help and we'll tell you so up front. A quick look at your well completion report, or a downhole camera survey, tells us immediately which kind of well you own and whether treatment makes sense.

How Hydrofracturing Restores a Mountain Well

The procedure is straightforward but demands specialized gear. We seat inflatable packers in the open-hole portion of your well to isolate a section of rock, then inject clean water at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure scours mineral scale and fine sediment out of existing joints and pushes them open farther into the formation, frequently linking your borehole to water-bearing fractures just beyond its original reach. No chemicals are ever used — only clean water. Most Big Bear jobs are completed in a single four-to-eight-hour visit, weather permitting.

Why Yields Fade at This Elevation

Snowmelt is the engine that recharges Bear Valley's groundwater each spring, and demand peaks in the dry, busy summer and fall tourist season. A well with a limited fracture network can draw down faster than the rock can refill it, leaving owners short exactly when they need water most. Over the years, mineral deposits common in granitic groundwater also build up in the narrowest, most productive cracks nearest the bore, steadily reducing flow. Both problems respond well to fracturing: clearing the clogged fractures and opening new ones gives the well both better inflow and more effective storage to ride out the dry months.

Signs It's Time to Consider Treatment

  • Your well yielded plenty a few years ago but now runs thin during summer occupancy.
  • The pump short-cycles or runs dry during back-to-back showers or laundry.
  • Recovery is slow — the well takes many hours to refill after heavy use.
  • A recently drilled well hit hard rock but never delivered the flow you expected.
  • You find yourself trucking in water or rationing through the late-season dry spell.

What to Do Before We Arrive

Track your water use for several days and note when pressure fades and how long recovery takes — those numbers help us estimate your baseline yield. Locate your completion report for the total depth, casing depth, and original yield if you can. And rule out the easy stuff: a waterlogged pressure tank, a failing pump, or a stuck check valve can mimic a low-yield well. We check all of those during our assessment so you don't pay to fracture a well that simply needed a new pump.

When a Professional Should Step In

Hydrofracturing is never a do-it-yourself job — the pressures involved are dangerous and the equipment is specialized. Our process begins with a pre-treatment flow test and a video inspection to confirm the casing is intact and to map the fracture zones worth targeting. That inspection protects you twice: it ensures fracturing is the right call, and it catches casing damage or equipment faults that would need addressing first. A licensed contractor's judgment here saves you from costly mistakes.

The Geology of Bear Valley

Bear Valley is a fault-bounded basin sitting atop the Big Bear granitic complex, a body of light-colored quartz monzonite and granodiorite that crystallized deep underground millions of years ago and was later uplifted to form the high plateau. This rock is dense and nearly impermeable except where it is broken — along cooling joints, tectonic fractures, and shear zones related to the faults that ring the valley. Groundwater collects and travels in those breaks. A well's productivity therefore depends almost entirely on how many water-bearing fractures it crosses, not on how deep it goes. Drilling deeper into unbroken granite adds cost without adding water, whereas hydrofracturing works horizontally outward from the existing bore to tap the fracture system more effectively. This is precisely why the technique is so well matched to Big Bear's geology.

A Pattern We See Often Around Moonridge and Sugarloaf

Many Big Bear homes began as vacation cabins and have since become full-time or short-term rental properties, multiplying water demand far beyond what the original well was sized to handle. A well that comfortably served occasional weekend guests can fall badly short when a rental sleeps a dozen people every weekend through the summer. Before assuming such a well is finished, it is worth testing whether hydrofracturing can lift its capacity to match the new use — in many cases it does, sparing the owner a five-figure drilling bill. We have seen marginal wells in the two-to-three gallon-per-minute range climb into a comfortable range that supports a busy household after a single treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Big Bear well in rock or sediment?

Slope and forest properties are almost always in fractured granite, which fracturing can improve; some valley-floor lots draw from loose sediments, which it cannot. Your completion report or a downhole camera survey settles the question quickly.

How much can hydrofracturing improve my yield?

Outcomes depend on the rock, but successful treatments in this granite often raise flow two to five times. We document before-and-after flow so the gain is measurable.

Can the work be done in winter?

Sometimes, but heavy Big Bear snow can delay access. Many owners schedule for late spring through fall when roads are clear and equipment can be staged easily.

Will it affect my neighbor's well?

No. Treatment works the fractures immediately around your borehole and does not deplete the broader aquifer your neighbors share.

How long before I see results?

We run a post-treatment flow test within 24 to 48 hours, so you know the outcome almost immediately rather than waiting weeks.

What if fracturing isn't right for my well?

We'll tell you plainly and recommend a better option. We don't sell treatments that won't help.
