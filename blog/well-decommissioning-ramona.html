When Is Well Decommissioning Legally Required?

Under California Water Code and San Diego County ordinance, a well must be properly destroyed whenever it is no longer being maintained for use. In Ramona, the most common triggers we see are:

  • Selling your property. Title companies, lenders, and buyers routinely require proof that any abandoned well on a Ramona parcel has been destroyed under permit. An open or unrecorded well can stall escrow.
  • Drilling a replacement well. When an old well stops producing and you drill a new one, the county requires the original to be decommissioned. You cannot legally keep two wells where one has been abandoned.
  • An inactive or "temporarily abandoned" well. A well that has sat unused without being properly maintained, sealed, and marked is considered abandoned and must be destroyed.
  • Contamination or safety risk. Wells near septic leach fields, former agricultural operations, or failed casings can channel bacteria, nitrates, and surface runoff straight into the aquifer.
  • New construction. If you are building a home, barn, or addition over or near an old well, it must be destroyed first.

The San Diego County Permit Process

All well destruction in Ramona is permitted and inspected by the San Diego County Department of Environmental Health and Quality (DEHQ), Land and Water Quality Division. A well destruction permit is required before any sealing work begins, and the work must be performed by a licensed C-57 contractor. As your contractor, we handle the entire application: we pull the original well completion report when one exists, prepare the destruction method and cross-section the county requires, submit the application, and pay the permit fee on your behalf.

The county reviews the proposed destruction method to confirm it meets California Department of Water Resources standards, then issues the permit. Once approved, we schedule the field work and coordinate the required county inspection. After the well is sealed, we file the Department of Water Resources Well Completion Report (form DWR 188) documenting the destruction, and the county closes out the permit. You receive copies for your records and for any future real estate disclosure.

How a Well Is Physically Destroyed

Proper destruction is far more than pouring concrete down a pipe. We follow the procedures in California Well Standards, DWR Bulletin 74-81 and 74-90, which San Diego County adopts. The steps are:

  1. Verify the well. We confirm total depth, casing diameter, casing material, and the static water level by sounding the well. This tells us exactly how much sealing material is required and whether any perforated zones need special attention.
  2. Remove the pump and obstructions. The pump, drop pipe, wiring, and any debris or foreign material are pulled so the borehole is clear to the bottom.
  3. Disinfect. The well is chlorinated to avoid sealing contaminants into the aquifer.
  4. Place the seal from the bottom up. Using a tremie pipe, we pump neat cement grout, sand-cement grout, or bentonite grout from the bottom of the hole upward so no voids or bridging occur. In gravel-packed or perforated wells, pressure grouting forces sealant into the surrounding formation.
  5. Complete the surface. The top section of casing is removed or cut off below grade, a final concrete plug is placed, and the surface is restored so nothing remains above ground.

What Happens If You Don't Decommission an Old Well

An abandoned well is not a passive hole in the ground. It is an open vertical pathway that connects the surface directly to the aquifer, bypassing the natural soil layers that normally filter contaminants. In Ramona, leaving a well unsealed creates several distinct problems. Ramona's reliance on the San Vicente and Santa Maria basins means a single neglected well can affect many surrounding properties. Surface runoff, fertilizer, fuel, and bacteria can travel straight down a corroded or open casing and degrade the groundwater that neighboring wells still draw from. Old wells also collapse over time, and a hidden, partially filled shaft is a genuine physical hazard to children, pets, livestock, and equipment. From a financial standpoint, an undocumented well is a recorded liability that can surface during a title search and stop a sale cold. Decommissioning resolves all of these at once: it removes the contamination pathway, eliminates the safety hazard, and produces a recorded document proving the well was properly retired.

A Closer Look at the Sealing Materials

The heart of a proper decommissioning is the sealant, and San Diego County follows the California Department of Water Resources standards for what may be used. The acceptable materials are neat cement grout (cement and water, sometimes with a small bentonite addition), sand-cement grout, and bentonite grout. Each has a place: neat cement is the workhorse for sealing the casing itself, while bentonite is often used where a flexible, low-permeability seal is preferred. The material must be placed by tremie pipe from the bottom of the well upward so that the grout displaces water and fills every foot of the bore without trapping voids or bridging partway down. Simply dumping dry sacks or concrete from the top is not compliant and is the most common reason a do-it-yourself "abandonment" fails inspection. Where a well is gravel-packed or has perforated sections, the grout is placed under pressure so it penetrates the annular space and the surrounding formation, not just the inside of the casing.

The San Diego County Department of Environmental Health Permit, Step by Step

In San Diego County, the San Diego County Department of Environmental Health and Quality (DEHQ), Land and Water Quality Division administers the well program. If a well is to be destroyed, the applicant must provide a description and cross-section detailing the destruction method, which the county reviews before issuing the permit. We prepare that cross-section, pull any archived completion report for your Ramona well from the county's document library, and submit everything on your behalf so the review goes smoothly.

Once the permit is in hand, the field sequence is straightforward but exacting. We mobilize our rig to the wellhead, sound the well one more time to confirm conditions match the permit, pull the pump and any down-hole equipment, disinfect, and then grout from the bottom up. A county inspector may be present to witness the seal or may inspect the completed surface. When the work is done, we complete the Department of Water Resources Well Completion Report documenting the destruction, including the materials used and the volumes placed, and we file it with the county and the state. You receive copies for your permanent records, your insurance, and any future buyer's due diligence.
