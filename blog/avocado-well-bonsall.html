Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Bonsall well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source

Bonsall's Grove Country Runs on Well Water

Tucked along the San Luis Rey River between Fallbrook and Oceanside, Bonsall is classic San Diego County grove country — rolling hills planted in avocados and citrus, horse ranches, and nurseries that ship plants across the state. The valley floor follows Highway 76, while the productive groves climb the slopes on either side. Many of those properties sit beyond reliable district service or simply find metered water too expensive for irrigated agriculture, which is why the private well is the workhorse of Bonsall farming.

The geology here is a mix: alluvial deposits along the San Luis Rey corridor where the river has laid down sand and gravel over time, and decomposed granite on the surrounding hills. That means a well on the valley floor can behave very differently from one a quarter mile upslope. Understanding which formation your well taps is the first step to keeping a Bonsall grove watered, and it's something we evaluate on every service call.

How Avocado Irrigation Wells Work

Avocados are thirsty, season-long irrigation customers. A mature tree can use 40 to 70 gallons on a hot day, and an acre commonly needs four to six acre-feet a year, with the heaviest demand during fruit set and sizing. A grove well therefore has to deliver sustained output for long irrigation sets, not the short bursts a house well sees.

Sizing the System to the Well

Good design starts with measuring what the well can actually sustain: static level, pumping level, and how fast it recovers. From there we size the submersible and break the grove into irrigation blocks the well can serve without being pulled down. On a strong alluvial well near the river, you may run large sets directly. On a granite hillside well, smaller blocks or a storage approach often works better.

Storage and Constant-Pressure Systems

Pairing the well with a storage tank is one of the most effective tools we install in Bonsall. The well trickles into the tank at whatever rate the formation allows, and a constant-pressure booster pulls from storage to irrigate at full, uniform pressure. It's the surest way to protect a moderate-yield well while still watering a full grove on schedule.

Common Bonsall Grove Water Problems

Salinity Along the River Corridor

Water quality matters as much as quantity for avocados, which are highly chloride-sensitive. Wells in the lower San Luis Rey corridor can carry elevated salts, and the symptoms — leaf burn, thin canopy, fruit drop — show up in the trees before most growers think to test the water. We test for chloride, TDS and the other parameters that actually drive avocado health, then recommend blending, leaching, or treatment as needed.

Seasonal Drawdown and Pump Wear

After a dry winter, both valley and hillside wells can draw down through the summer, and an aging pump loses capacity right when you need it most. A simple drawdown test separates a declining formation from a worn pump so you aren't replacing the wrong component.

Sand and Emitter Clogging

Sand from alluvial wells and fine mineral content from granite both clog drip emitters and micro-sprinklers, wrecking irrigation uniformity. Wellhead sediment filtration plus periodic flushing keeps every block getting the same water.

Protecting Young Bonsall Plantings

Newly planted avocado trees are far less forgiving than established ones. Their shallow root systems can't reach down to buffer a missed irrigation, so a young Bonsall block depends completely on a well that delivers consistent water and pressure. We often help growers who are expanding or replanting size their system for the grove they are building toward, not just the one in the ground today — because adding trees later can push a marginal well past its limit during peak summer.

Micro-sprinklers are the usual choice for young trees because they wet a wider area than drip while the root zone is still developing, but they demand uniform pressure across the block. On Bonsall's varied terrain, that means pressure regulation and properly zoned valves so the trees at the top of a slope get the same water as those at the bottom. We also encourage growers to keep a simple irrigation log; a gradual rise in run time needed to wet the same soil is one of the earliest warning signs that a pump or well is losing capacity, long before the trees show stress.

Frost is the other young-tree concern in the valley. Some growers run sprinklers for frost protection on cold nights, which is a sudden, heavy, simultaneous draw on the well. A system sized only for normal irrigation can fall short on a frost night, so we factor that peak in when we design water delivery for grove owners who protect against cold.

What to Check Before You Call

You can safely confirm a few things first: that the pump breaker and disconnect haven't tripped, that the pressure tank holds its air charge, that the pump isn't short-cycling rapidly, and that no valve or line break is bleeding pressure. If pressure only fades during long sets, the issue is more likely well yield or pump wear than controls.

Energy Costs and Pump Efficiency in Bonsall

For most Bonsall groves, the electricity to run the well pump is one of the largest ongoing operating costs, and an inefficient or poorly matched system quietly wastes money every irrigation set. A pump that is oversized, worn, or fighting against a clogged distribution system draws more power to move the same water. When we service a grove well, we look at the whole system — pump condition, motor efficiency, pressure settings, and distribution losses — not just the immediate complaint, because small inefficiencies add up across a long irrigation season.

Several upgrades reliably lower pumping costs here. A correctly sized pump matched to the well's real yield avoids wasted energy and premature wear. A constant-pressure or variable-speed system delivers only the pressure the grove needs at any moment rather than cycling a fixed-speed pump on and off. And keeping filters and emitters clean means the pump isn't working against unnecessary back-pressure. We are happy to assess an existing Bonsall system and identify where energy and water are leaking away.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Bonsall well in alluvium or granite, and why does it matter?

It depends on where on your property the well sits. Wells along the San Luis Rey valley floor often tap sand-and-gravel alluvium, while hillside wells draw from decomposed granite and fractured rock. The two behave differently in yield, recovery, and sand production, which changes how we size your pump and design irrigation. We determine this on site before recommending equipment.

My avocado trees look stressed even though I'm irrigating — what's wrong?

If the trees are getting water but still showing tip burn or thin canopy, the problem is often water quality rather than quantity. Avocados are very chloride-sensitive, and some Bonsall wells carry elevated salts. We test your well water for chloride and TDS and recommend blending, leaching, or treatment to correct it.

How much does a new grove well cost in Bonsall?

A complete turnkey new well typically ranges from $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth, geology, and the pump and storage required. Before going that route, we always check whether your existing well can be rehabilitated or hydrofractured for a fraction of the cost.

Can you improve a low-yield hillside well?

Often, yes. Hydrofracturing uses controlled water pressure to open existing fractures in granite wells and frequently boosts yield, at a cost of roughly $3,000 to $8,000. Well rehabilitation to clear fouled screens or perforations is another option. We assess which approach fits your well.

Do you install storage tanks for groves?

Yes. A storage tank paired with a constant-pressure booster lets a moderate-yield Bonsall well fill around the clock while still irrigating at full pressure on demand. It's one of the most reliable setups for protecting a marginal well and watering a full grove.
