How a Private Well Pressure System Actually Works

Before you can understand why pressure drops, it helps to know the four parts that produce it. A submersible pump sits deep in the well casing and pushes water upward whenever the system calls for it. That water flows into a pressure tank, which holds a cushion of compressed air over a rubber bladder; the air charge is what delivers steady pressure to your fixtures between pump cycles. A pressure switch is the brain of the operation — it senses tank pressure and tells the pump when to switch on (the cut-in point, often 40 PSI) and off (the cut-out point, often 60 PSI). Many newer Descanso installations use a constant-pressure system, where a variable-frequency drive ramps the pump speed up and down to hold a single target pressure no matter how many taps are open. When any one of these components drifts out of spec, you feel it at the showerhead. Because Descanso homes often sit well above their wellheads on sloping lots, every part of this chain works under extra load, so small problems show up faster here than they would on flat ground.

Why Descanso's Geology and Elevation Cause Low Pressure

The backcountry of eastern San Diego County is built on the granitic bedrock of the Peninsular Ranges. Instead of a deep sandy aquifer that stores water freely, Descanso wells draw from fractures and weathered seams in decomposed granite. These fractured-rock aquifers can be productive, but they are unpredictable: yield depends on whether your borehole intersected a good fracture network, and that yield can fall sharply during dry years. Many local wells run 300 to 600 feet deep to reach reliable water, and the deeper the standing water level, the harder your pump must work to lift it to a hilltop home. Add the elevation gain across a typical Descanso parcel and the head pressure your pump fights becomes significant. Granitic groundwater also tends to carry dissolved minerals and fine sediment, which scale up pump components, clog screens, and narrow pipes over the years — all of which quietly chip away at the pressure you feel indoors.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Descanso

From hundreds of service calls across the Descanso, Pine Valley, and Cuyamaca area, these are the culprits we find most often:

  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: When the bladder ruptures or the air charge bleeds off, the pump short-cycles and pressure surges and sags. This is the single most common cause we see.
  • Pressure switch out of adjustment or corroded: Pitted contacts or a switch set too low produces chronically weak flow; mountain temperature swings accelerate this wear.
  • Worn or undersized pump: After years of lifting water hundreds of feet, impellers wear and output falls. A pump sized for the original water level may also be undersized once the table drops in drought.
  • Mineral scale and sediment: Granitic water deposits scale inside pipes, fittings, and the pump itself, steadily restricting flow.
  • Dropping water table: During dry seasons the standing level in a fractured-rock well can fall below the pump intake, causing the pump to draw air and lose pressure.
  • Clogged filters or a partly closed valve: Sediment cartridges and screens load up quickly on backcountry water; sometimes the fix is as simple as a neglected filter or a half-shut valve after service work.
  • Leaks in buried line: Long runs from wellhead to house across rural parcels are prone to pinhole leaks that bleed off pressure before it reaches the home.

What You Can Check Before Calling a Pro

A few safe checks can tell you a lot, and sometimes save you a service call:

  1. Watch your pressure gauge. Most systems have a gauge near the tank. Healthy pressure cycles between roughly 40 and 60 PSI. If it barely moves or swings wildly, note what you see.
  2. Check the air charge in the pressure tank. With the pump off and the system drained, a tire gauge on the tank's air valve should read about 2 PSI below your cut-in setting. Way low or zero points to a tank problem.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking on and off (short-cycling) usually means a tank or switch issue, not the pump itself.
  4. Test one fixture at a time. If only one faucet is weak, the problem is local — an aerator or fixture. If every tap is weak, the issue is at the well, tank, or switch.
  5. Replace or remove sediment filters. A clogged whole-house cartridge is a frequent, easy-to-miss cause of system-wide pressure loss.

Leave the wellhead wiring, pressure switch contacts, and any work inside the well casing to a licensed professional — well systems combine electricity and water, and the components sit deep underground.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Descanso well lose pressure in late summer?

Fractured-rock aquifers recharge slowly and depend on winter rain and snowmelt. By late summer the standing water level can drop close to or below your pump intake, so the pump draws air and pressure falls. Often the fix is lowering the pump, adjusting the system, or improving yield with hydrofracturing.

How deep are wells in Descanso?

Most local wells fall in the 300- to 600-foot range to reach dependable fractures in the granite, though depth varies parcel by parcel. Deeper wells and hilltop homes both increase the lift your pump must overcome.

Could low pressure just be my pressure tank?

Frequently, yes. A waterlogged tank with a failed bladder or lost air charge is the most common cause of weak, surging pressure. Replacing a tank ($600–$1,500) is far cheaper than a pump, which is why we always check it first.

Will a constant-pressure system help my hilltop home?

Often a great fit in Descanso. A constant-pressure (variable-speed) system holds steady pressure regardless of demand or elevation, eliminating the sag you feel when the well sits well below the house. Installed cost runs roughly $2,000–$4,500.

Is hard water hurting my pressure?

It can. Granitic groundwater carries minerals that scale up pipes, fittings, and pump parts over time, gradually choking flow. A softener or descaling system ($1,500–$3,500) protects your equipment and keeps pressure stable.
