Well Pump Won't Start in Banning Pass?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Banning Pass homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

Well Pump Won't Start in the Banning Pass? Read This First

The Banning Pass — better known geographically as the San Gorgonio Pass — is the dramatic gap in Riverside County where the land squeezes between the San Bernardino Mountains to the north and the San Jacinto Mountains to the south. Carved by the San Andreas Fault, it is one of the deepest mountain passes in the country and famously one of the windiest places in the United States, home to the sprawling San Gorgonio wind farm. Communities through the pass, from Banning and Beaumont out toward Cabazon, sit in a transition zone between coastal and desert climate, and many homes on the outskirts depend on private wells. When that well pump won't start, a windy, hot pass afternoon turns uncomfortable fast.

Fortunately, a pump that refuses to start almost always comes down to a single failed component. This guide explains how your well system works, the failures most common in the pass, the checks you can safely make yourself, and the point at which you should bring in a licensed professional.

The Well System, Component by Component

Wells in the San Gorgonio Pass draw from a mix of alluvial deposits and fractured bedrock, and most homes use a submersible pump set in the casing. The system delivers water through a coordinated sequence:

  • Power supply: A 240-volt circuit runs from the panel to the pump.
  • Pressure tank: Stores pressurized water so the pump isn't triggered by every small use.
  • Pressure switch: Reads tank pressure and tells the pump when to start and stop.
  • Control box / capacitor: Delivers the starting jolt a single-phase motor needs.
  • Motor and wiring: The motor drives the pump; long wire runs feed it power.
  • Water level: Enough water must sit above the pump intake for it to draw.

Break any link and the outcome is identical — dry taps and a silent system.

Why Pumps Fail to Start in the Pass

Wind, surges, and power interruptions

The pass is defined by its wind, and that wind takes a toll on the power grid. Gusts bring down lines and cause outages, and the surges when power is restored can trip breakers or stress pump electronics. A pump with no power can't run. Reset the well breaker once — if it trips again immediately, leave it off and call for help.

Pressure switch failure

The pressure switch cycles thousands of times a year and is a frequent failure point. Burned contacts or a sediment-clogged sensing port can stop it from closing, which means the pump never receives its start signal.

Capacitor and control box failure

The pass swings from windy and mild to genuinely hot, and heat shortens capacitor life. A failing start capacitor commonly leaves the motor humming or clicking without turning. Replacing the capacitor or control box often brings the pump back.

Motor burnout

A submersible motor can fail after long service, repeated dry-running, or electrical stress from those grid surges. Replacing it requires pulling the pump from the well — the most labor-intensive of the common repairs.

Wiring and connection faults

Blowing grit and temperature swings are hard on connections. Corroded splices, loose terminals, or rodent-chewed surface wiring can interrupt power anywhere along the run, sometimes intermittently.

Low water level

In dry stretches, water levels in the pass can drop. If the level falls near the pump intake, low-water protection may shut the pump off to keep it from running dry.

Checks You Can Safely Make

  1. Confirm the home has power, then check the dedicated well breaker. Reset it once only.
  2. Open a tap and listen at the pressure tank for the pressure switch clicking.
  3. Read the pressure gauge — stuck at zero or pinned high both point to a fault.
  4. Inspect visible wiring at the wellhead and tank for grit damage, corrosion, or scorching.
  5. Note any link to recent high winds, an outage, or a long dry spell.

Safety note: These systems carry 240 volts and the pump sits deep underground. Stick to looking and listening only. Opening the control box or pulling the pump is strictly a job for a licensed professional.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when the breaker trips repeatedly, you smell burning or see scorch marks, the motor hums but won't turn, or your checks come up empty. These indicate capacitor, motor, or wiring failures that require professional meters and often pulling the pump. How Pass Conditions Wear on a Pump System

It is worth understanding why pump components in the San Gorgonio Pass tend to lead harder lives than they might elsewhere. The constant wind that makes this corridor ideal for turbines also drives fine grit and dust into every exposed fitting, working its way into pressure-switch sensing ports and corroding electrical connections over time. The grid that serves the pass takes a beating from those same winds, so motors and capacitors here endure more voltage sags, surges, and rapid power cycles than equipment in calmer areas — and each of those events shortens component life. Layer on the climate swing from breezy mornings to triple-digit afternoons, and you have heat stress that accelerates capacitor failure. None of this means pass wells are unreliable; it simply means that routine attention pays off more here than almost anywhere. A surge protector or pump-saver device guards the motor against the electrical chaos. An occasional inspection catches a grit-fouled switch or a corroding connection before it strands you without water. And keeping the pressure tank's air charge correct prevents the rapid short-cycling that burns out motors prematurely. A little proactive care goes a long way in a place this hard on equipment.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Banning Pass pump hums but no water comes out. What does that mean?

A humming pump that won't turn usually points to a failed start capacitor or control box, though it can also mean a seized motor. Electrical testing tells us which, and a capacitor replacement often resolves it.

Why does my well breaker keep tripping after windstorms?

Wind-driven outages and the surges that follow can stress pump components. A breaker that trips repeatedly is flagging a real fault like a shorted motor or damaged wiring. Don't keep resetting it — shut it off and call us.

Could low water be stopping my pump?

Yes. In dry periods, water levels in the pass can fall near the pump intake, and low-water protection may shut the pump off to prevent dry running. We can measure your level and pump depth to confirm.

How much does a replacement pump cost?

Submersible pump replacement runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower. A pressure-switch repair is much less at $150 to $350. We diagnose before recommending anything.

Is DIY electrical work on my well safe?

No. With 240 volts and a deep pump, electrical repairs are dangerous. Visual and listening checks are fine; leave the rest to a licensed technician.
