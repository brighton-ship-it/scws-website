Your well produces just 2 gallons per minute—barely enough to keep up with a single shower. Or maybe your well flows fine most of the year, but summertime demand for irrigation leaves your household competing for water. Perhaps fire code requires 2,500 gallons of water storage you simply don't have. Or you're preparing for the next power outage that could leave you without water for days. If any of these scenarios sound familiar, a water storage tank or cistern might be the solution you need. Storage systems let low-yield wells serve normal households, provide emergency backup when pumps fail or power goes out, meet fire protection requirements, and supply high-volume irrigation without depleting your well. But choosing the right system means understanding tank types, sizing calculations, pressure requirements, installation costs, and maintenance needs. This comprehensive guide covers everything California well owners need to know about water storage tanks and cisterns—from when you need one and what size to get, to installation requirements and how to keep stored water safe.

💧 California Well Fact: About 25-30% of rural wells in San Diego County produce under 5 GPM—not enough to meet peak household demand without storage. A properly sized storage tank transforms a marginal well into a reliable water source.

When You Need a Water Storage Tank

Water storage tanks aren't necessary for every well system, but they solve specific problems that are common in rural California properties. Here's when you should consider adding storage:

1. Low-Yield Wells (Under 5 GPM)

A well producing 2-3 GPM can theoretically supply 120-180 gallons per hour, which sounds like plenty—until you realize a typical shower uses 2-3 GPM, a washing machine uses 3-5 GPM, and outdoor irrigation can demand 5-15 GPM. Run two fixtures simultaneously and your well can't keep up. The water level drops, the pump cycles frantically or shuts off on low-water protection, and your household runs dry.

A storage tank solves this by letting your slow well fill the tank gradually over time. Even a 1-2 GPM well can fill 500-1,000 gallons overnight. During peak usage, you draw from the tank at whatever rate you need, then the well refills it when demand is low. This setup lets a marginal well serve a normal household effectively. See our guide on slow well water recovery solutions for more strategies.

2. Fire Protection Requirements

Many California counties—especially in high fire-risk areas—require properties to maintain minimum water storage for fire protection. Common requirements include:

  • 2,500 gallons minimum for single-family residences in rural fire districts
  • 5,000-10,000 gallons for properties over 5 acres or in very high fire zones
  • Fire department connections (FDC) for firefighter access to stored water
  • Dedicated fire hydrants or standpipes in some jurisdictions

Even if not legally required, having 2,500+ gallons on hand can make the difference between saving your home and losing everything during a wildfire. Well pumps typically can't deliver the 250+ GPM fire trucks need—stored water can.

3. High-Demand Irrigation

Landscape irrigation, gardens, orchards, and livestock watering can easily consume 500-2,000 gallons per session. If your well produces 5 GPM, that's only 300 gallons per hour—you'd need 2-6 hours of continuous pumping to meet demand. This can overdraw the well, causing recovery problems and potentially damaging the pump.

A storage tank lets you irrigate at high flow rates (10-20+ GPM) for short periods, then allows the well to refill the tank gradually. This is especially important for properties with drip irrigation systems, agricultural operations, or large landscaped areas.

4. Backup Water Supply

Power outages, pump failures, and water quality emergencies can leave you without water. A storage tank provides:

  • Power outage protection: 500-1,000 gallons lasts a household 1-3 days without electricity
  • Pump failure backup: Water on hand while waiting for repairs
  • Well recovery buffer: Protection if well temporarily runs dry
  • Emergency supply: Clean water during contamination events or natural disasters

5. Intermittent Well Production

Some wells produce well during wet seasons but struggle during summer and drought periods. Others produce better at certain times of day as groundwater levels fluctuate. Storage tanks buffer these variations, storing water when production is strong and providing supply when it's weak.

6. Hauled Water Properties

If your property doesn't have a well—or the well is insufficient—you might haul water by truck. Storage tanks (often 1,000-5,000 gallons) let you store delivered water efficiently. This is common for remote properties, new construction before well drilling, or areas with poor groundwater.

🔥 Fire Safety Note

San Diego County and other high-risk fire areas increasingly require water storage for building permits, especially for new construction or major renovations. Check with your local fire marshal and building department early in your project planning. Retrofitting storage after construction is much more expensive than including it from the start.

Storage Tank vs. Cistern: What's the Difference?

The terms "water storage tank" and "cistern" are often used interchangeably, and in modern usage, there's significant overlap. However, there are some traditional distinctions:

Cistern (Traditional Definition)

  • Purpose: Originally designed to collect and store rainwater or hauled water
  • Installation: Typically underground or partially buried
  • Pressure: Usually unpressurized; relies on gravity feed or manual pumping
  • Material: Historically concrete, stone, or brick; modern cisterns use fiberglass or polyethylene
  • Size: Generally larger—1,000 to 10,000+ gallons
  • Use case: Long-term bulk storage, rainwater harvesting, properties without wells

Water Storage Tank (Modern Usage)

  • Purpose: Any container for storing water—well water, municipal water, or collected rainwater
  • Installation: Can be above-ground, below-ground, or elevated
  • Pressure: Often paired with booster pumps and pressure tanks for household pressure
  • Material: Polyethylene (most common), fiberglass, steel, or concrete
  • Size: Wide range—100 to 10,000+ gallons
  • Use case: Supplemental storage for low-yield wells, fire protection, irrigation, backup

Bottom Line

Today, most people use "storage tank" for above-ground installations paired with well systems, and "cistern" for underground or rainwater collection systems. Functionally, they're both just containers for storing water. The key differences are installation type, size, and how they're integrated into your water system.

Types of Water Storage Tanks

Choosing the right tank material and installation type depends on your budget, site conditions, size requirements, and how you'll use the system. Here are the main options:

1. Polyethylene (Plastic) Tanks

Material: High-density polyethylene (HDPE) or cross-linked polyethylene, food-grade approved

Typical sizes: 100-10,000 gallons; most common residential sizes are 500-2,500 gallons

Installation: Primarily above-ground; some models rated for partial or full burial

Lifespan: 15-30+ years with UV protection and proper maintenance

Pros: Lightweight and easy to install, corrosion-resistant, won't rust or leak minerals into water, relatively inexpensive, available in many shapes and colors, UV-resistant coatings available, can be moved if needed

Cons: Can degrade in direct sunlight without UV coating, lighter tanks can be displaced by wind or flooding, not as durable as concrete for long-term underground installation, temperature fluctuations can affect structural integrity in extreme climates

Cost: $500-$2,500 for 500-2,500 gallons (tank only); $1,000-$4,000 installed

Best for:

Most residential well systems, especially above-ground installations. Great balance of cost, durability, and ease of installation. First choice for most California homeowners.

2. Fiberglass Tanks

Material: Fiberglass-reinforced plastic (FRP); corrosion-resistant and durable

Typical sizes: 500-10,000 gallons; custom sizes available

Installation: Above-ground or underground; excellent for burial applications

Lifespan: 30-50+ years with minimal maintenance

Pros: Extremely corrosion-resistant, excellent for underground installation, strong and durable, maintains water quality (won't leach chemicals), handles temperature extremes well, lighter than concrete but stronger than polyethylene

Cons: More expensive than polyethylene, can crack if dropped or improperly supported, UV exposure can degrade surface over time, requires proper bedding for underground installation, limited availability in some areas

Cost: $1,000-$5,000 for 500-2,500 gallons; $2,000-$8,000 installed

Best for:

Underground installations, high water table areas, properties needing long-term durability, and situations where water quality preservation is critical (e.g., potable water storage).

3. Steel Tanks

Material: Galvanized steel, stainless steel, or epoxy/polyurethane-coated steel

Typical sizes: 500-50,000+ gallons; used for large commercial/agricultural systems

Installation: Above-ground (sometimes elevated); rarely underground due to corrosion

Lifespan: 20-40 years depending on coating and maintenance

Pros: Extremely strong and durable, can hold very large capacities, fire-resistant, suitable for high-pressure systems, pest and rodent-proof, can be insulated to prevent freezing

Cons: Expensive, prone to rust and corrosion without proper coating, heavy (requires crane for installation), requires regular maintenance (recoating every 10-15 years), can affect water taste if coating degrades, condensation issues in humid climates

Cost: $2,000-$10,000+ depending on size and coating; installation adds $1,000-$5,000+

Best for:

Large agricultural operations, commercial properties, elevated storage systems, and situations requiring extreme durability or fire protection. Overkill for most residential well systems.

4. Concrete Cisterns

Material: Reinforced concrete; cast-in-place or precast sections

Typical sizes: 1,000-20,000+ gallons; custom sizing common

Installation: Almost always underground; very labor-intensive

Lifespan: 50-100+ years with proper construction and maintenance

Pros: Longest lifespan of any option, excellent for large underground storage, structurally very strong (can be driven over), maintains cool water temperatures, won't shift or float even in high water table, traditional aesthetic for historic properties

Cons: Most expensive option, requires excavation and professional installation, prone to cracking if not properly reinforced, can leach lime into water (affecting pH), difficult to repair if damaged, not portable, requires waterproofing coating

Cost: $3,000-$15,000+ depending on size; installation typically $5,000-$20,000 total

Best for:

Large permanent installations, properties wanting century-long durability, situations where tanks must be buried under driveways or high-traffic areas, historic properties, and high-end custom homes.

How to Size Your Storage Tank

Proper sizing is critical. Too small and you won't have enough buffer; too large and you're wasting money and increasing stagnation risk. Here's how to calculate the right size:

Step 1: Calculate Daily Water Usage

Average household usage: 80-100 gallons per person per day (includes all uses: drinking, cooking, bathing, laundry, dishes, toilet flushing, outdoor watering)

For a family of four: 320-400 gallons per day average

Add extra for:

  • Irrigation: 100-1,000+ gallons per session (varies widely by landscape size)
  • Livestock: 10-20 gallons per horse per day; 5-10 gallons per cow per day
  • Pool filling or large cleaning projects: 500-2,000 gallons
  • Guests or seasonal use: Add 20-30% buffer

Step 2: Determine Well Recovery Rate

Your well's GPM (gallons per minute) production rate determines how quickly you can refill the tank. If your well produces 3 GPM continuously:

  • 3 GPM × 60 minutes = 180 gallons per hour
  • 180 gallons/hour × 24 hours = 4,320 gallons per day maximum

But wells don't run 24/7—pump cycling, drawdown recovery, and rest periods mean real-world production is typically 60-80% of theoretical maximum. So a 3 GPM well realistically produces about 2,500-3,500 gallons per day.

Step 3: Calculate Required Storage Capacity

Basic formula: Storage capacity = (Daily usage - Daily well production) × Buffer days

Example: Family of four uses 400 gallons/day; well produces 2,800 gallons/day

Well production exceeds usage, so you technically don't need storage. However, peak demand (morning showers, evening laundry, etc.) can exceed instantaneous GPM, so you want buffer storage of at least 200-500 gallons.

Low-yield example: Same family uses 400 gallons/day; well produces only 1,500 gallons/day (1 GPM)

Deficit = 400 - 1,500 = -1,100 gallons/day surplus
This well can't keep up! You need storage equal to at least 1-2 days' usage: 400-800 gallons minimum.

Add 20-30% safety buffer → 500-1,000 gallon tank recommended

Step 4: Add Special Requirements

  • Fire protection: Add 2,500-10,000 gallons (check local code requirements)
  • Irrigation: Add 500-5,000 gallons depending on area and frequency
  • Emergency backup: Add 500-1,000 gallons for 2-3 days' supply without power
  • Hauled water: Larger is better (1,000-5,000 gallons) to reduce delivery frequency

💡 Pro Tip: Don't Oversize

Bigger isn't always better. Oversized tanks can lead to water stagnation, increased bacteria growth, and wasted space and money. Unless you need fire protection or large irrigation capacity, match your tank size to actual needs plus a 20-30% buffer. For most households with low-yield wells, 500-1,500 gallons is the sweet spot.

Pressure Systems: Gravity-Fed vs. Booster Pump

A storage tank by itself doesn't provide water pressure. You need a system to deliver water from the tank to your home at usable pressure (typically 40-60 PSI). Here are your options:

1. Gravity-Fed Systems

How it works: Tank is elevated above the house. Gravity provides pressure—every foot of elevation above the outlet provides about 0.433 PSI. To get 40 PSI, the tank needs to be about 92 feet above your highest fixture.

Pros: No electricity needed, extremely reliable, no moving parts to fail, silent operation, very low operating cost

Cons: Requires significant elevation difference (rare in flat terrain), pressure varies with tank level, typically provides lower pressure than booster systems, structural requirements for elevated platforms

Best for: Hillside properties with natural elevation, emergency backup systems, off-grid applications where electricity is unreliable

2. Booster Pump Systems (Most Common)

How it works: A booster pump draws water from the storage tank and sends it to a pressure tank, which then delivers consistent pressure to the house. The system uses a pressure switch to automatically maintain 40-60 PSI.

Components needed: Booster pump (typically 1/2 to 1 HP), pressure tank (20-80 gallons), pressure switch, check valve, pressure gauge, electrical connection

Pros: Consistent pressure regardless of tank level or elevation, can be installed anywhere, familiar setup (works like well pump system), can provide high flow rates, easy to troubleshoot and repair

Cons: Requires electricity, pump maintenance needed, more complexity and potential failure points, operating noise (usually minimal)

Cost: $500-$1,500 for pump, pressure tank, and controls; $800-$2,500 installed

Best for: Most residential installations, properties without elevation advantage, situations requiring consistent high pressure

Typical Storage Tank System Configuration

  1. Well pump fills storage tank slowly as water level drops
  2. Float switch or level sensor in tank controls well pump (prevents overflow and dry running)
  3. Booster pump draws from storage tank and pushes water to pressure tank
  4. Pressure tank (20-80 gallons) provides surge capacity and reduces pump cycling
  5. Pressure switch turns booster pump on/off to maintain household pressure (40-60 PSI)
  6. Household plumbing receives consistent pressurized water

This two-pump, two-tank system (storage tank + pressure tank) is the standard for low-yield well installations.

Installation Requirements & California Permits

Installing a water storage tank isn't as simple as buying a tank and setting it on the ground. Here's what you need to know:

California Permitting Requirements

Requirements vary significantly by county and city, but here are general guidelines for San Diego County and similar jurisdictions:

  • Tanks under 500 gallons: Often exempt from permits (verify locally)
  • Tanks 500-5,000 gallons: Building permit usually required; may need engineered plans for underground installation
  • Tanks over 5,000 gallons: Building permit required, engineered plans required, structural review for elevated installations
  • Electrical work: Electrical permit for pump installation and controls
  • Plumbing connections: Plumbing permit in some jurisdictions
  • Fire protection use: Fire marshal approval and inspection required
  • Underground tanks: May require soils report, septic system clearance, setback variances

Site Requirements

  • Setbacks: Typically 10+ feet from property lines, 50-100 feet from septic systems, 10 feet from buildings
  • Foundation: Level, compacted base; gravel or crushed rock for drainage; concrete pad for large tanks
  • Access: Equipment access for delivery and maintenance; some tanks require crane placement
  • Drainage: Site must drain away from tank to prevent flooding and undermining
  • Sun exposure: Above-ground tanks should be in shade or have UV-resistant coating to prevent algae
  • Underground depth: Below frost line (typically 12-18" in San Diego County); minimum 12" of cover

Safety and Code Compliance

  • Backflow prevention: Required to prevent contamination of potable water supply
  • Overflow management: Overflow pipe must discharge away from tank foundation and buildings
  • Ventilation: Tanks must have screened vents to prevent vacuum and allow air exchange
  • Access covers: Secure, lockable access to prevent children and animals from entering
  • Seismic anchoring: Required in California for tanks over certain sizes (varies by jurisdiction)
  • Fire department connections: If used for fire protection, must meet specific fitting and access requirements

⚠️ Permitting Is Not Optional

Installing a water storage system without permits can result in fines, mandatory removal, insurance issues, and problems selling your property. Unpermitted tanks can also void homeowner's insurance if they cause damage. Always check with your county building department before installation—the permitting process is worth the peace of mind and legal protection.

Maintenance: Cleaning, Inspection & Winterizing

Proper maintenance keeps your water safe, your system reliable, and your tank lasting decades. Here's what you need to do:

Annual Maintenance Tasks

1. Tank Cleaning (Annual)

  • Drain the tank completely
  • Inspect interior for sediment, algae, cracks, or damage
  • Scrub walls and floor with stiff brush and dilute chlorine solution (1/4 cup bleach per 5 gallons water)
  • Rinse thoroughly with clean water until no chlorine smell remains
  • Inspect and clean screens, filters, and vents
  • Refill and disinfect with fresh well water

Best time: Spring or fall when temperatures are moderate. Schedule when you can use tank water for irrigation before draining.

Quarterly Inspection Tasks

  • Check water level: Verify float switches and level sensors are working correctly
  • Inspect for leaks: Look around fittings, valves, and tank seams
  • Test water quality: Check for odor, color, or taste changes; test for bacteria if concerned
  • Examine tank exterior: Look for cracks, UV damage, rust (steel tanks), or structural issues
  • Check pump operation: Listen for unusual noises, verify pressure is consistent
  • Inspect vents and screens: Clear debris, check for insect nests or blockages

Monthly Tasks

  • Run the system: If tank isn't used regularly, run water for 15-20 minutes to prevent stagnation
  • Check chlorine levels: If using chlorination, test residual (should be 0.2-0.5 ppm)
  • Listen to booster pump: Verify it's cycling normally and pressure is stable
  • Visual inspection: Quick look for obvious problems (leaks, pests, damage)

Winterizing (If Applicable)

San Diego County rarely freezes, but if you're in a mountain community (Julian, Palomar Mountain, etc.) or have an elevated tank:

  • Insulate exposed pipes and fittings with foam insulation or heat tape
  • Insulate above-ground tanks with insulating blankets or wrap (especially for smaller tanks)
  • Keep tanks full—thermal mass of water prevents freezing better than empty space
  • Maintain circulation during cold snaps to prevent ice formation
  • For seasonal properties: Drain system completely if unoccupied during winter

Signs You Need Professional Service

  • Water quality changes (odor, color, taste)
  • Persistent algae growth despite cleaning
  • Structural cracks or leaks
  • Pump cycling excessively or not maintaining pressure
  • Float switches or sensors malfunctioning
  • Sediment buildup that won't clear with normal cleaning
  • Bacteria test failures

Water Quality Considerations

Storing water introduces risks that flowing well water doesn't have. Stagnation, temperature fluctuations, and exposure to air can all affect water quality. Here's how to keep stored water safe:

Preventing Bacteria and Algae Growth

1. Block Sunlight

Algae needs light to grow. Use opaque tanks (black, dark green, or brown polyethylene) or install above-ground tanks in shaded areas. Underground tanks naturally block light.

2. Seal the Tank

Food-grade, sealed tanks prevent insects, rodents, debris, and airborne contaminants from entering. Ensure all access ports have gaskets and secure lids.

3. Maintain Circulation

Stagnant water breeds bacteria. If water sits unused for weeks, run the system to cycle fresh water through. First-in-first-out design helps—new water enters at the bottom, old water exits from the top.

4. Chlorinate

Maintain 0.2-0.5 ppm residual chlorine in stored water using chlorine injection systems or periodic manual chlorination. This prevents bacteria growth without affecting taste at low concentrations.

5. Keep Water Cool

Bacteria multiply faster in warm water. Underground tanks stay naturally cool. Above-ground tanks should be insulated or shaded in hot climates.

Treatment Options for Stored Water

  • UV sterilization: Install UV system on outlet line to kill bacteria before use ($300-$1,000)
  • Chlorination: Inject chlorine into tank or supply line ($200-$800 for automatic systems)
  • Filtration: Sediment filters (5-20 micron) remove particles ($50-$300)
  • Carbon filtration: Removes chlorine taste and odors if chlorinating ($100-$500)
  • Reverse osmosis: For drinking water only; removes virtually all contaminants ($300-$2,000)

Water Testing for Stored Water

Test stored water at least twice yearly, or quarterly if used for drinking:

  • Bacteria: Total coliform and E. coli (most important test—$30-$60)
  • pH: Should remain stable; pH drift indicates contamination or tank degradation
  • Turbidity: Cloudiness indicates sediment or biological growth
  • Chlorine residual: If chlorinating, verify 0.2-0.5 ppm residual
  • Taste and odor: Changes indicate bacterial growth or tank material issues

Rainwater Harvesting & California Regulations

California actively encourages rainwater harvesting as a drought-resistance strategy. Storage tanks are essential for capturing and using rainwater. Here's what you need to know:

Is Rainwater Harvesting Legal in California?

Yes. California law (AB 1750, AB 2363) explicitly permits rainwater collection for both non-potable and potable uses, subject to health and building code requirements. Unlike some states that restrict rainwater rights, California encourages it.

Permitted Uses

Non-Potable Uses (Minimal Restrictions)

  • Landscape irrigation
  • Toilet flushing
  • Car washing
  • Fire protection
  • Agricultural irrigation
  • Industrial processes

No special permits typically required, but check local ordinances. Some HOAs restrict visible collection systems.

Potable Uses (Drinking Water—Strict Requirements)

  • Requires health department approval and regular testing
  • Must meet drinking water standards (bacteriological, chemical, physical)
  • Treatment required: filtration, UV sterilization, and/or chlorination
  • First-flush diversion mandatory
  • Sealed, food-grade storage
  • Quarterly water testing
  • May require professional certification of system design

How Much Rainwater Can You Collect?

San Diego County averages 10-15 inches of rainfall per year (varies widely by location—coastal areas get less, mountains get more). Calculate your potential:

Formula: Roof area (sq ft) × Annual rainfall (inches) × 0.623 = Gallons per year

Example: 2,000 sq ft roof in area with 12 inches annual rainfall
2,000 × 12 × 0.623 = 14,952 gallons per year theoretical maximum

Realistically, expect to capture about 75-85% due to first-flush diversion, evaporation, and system losses → ~11,000-12,500 gallons/year usable

This is enough to supplement irrigation for a typical residential landscape, but unlikely to meet all household water needs. Most systems use rainwater to supplement well water, not replace it.

Essential Components of Rainwater Systems

  • Gutters and downspouts: Collect water from roof and direct to storage
  • First-flush diverter: Diverts first 10-20 gallons (containing roof debris and contaminants) away from storage—critical for water quality
  • Debris filter/screen: Removes leaves, insects, and particulates (5-100 micron)
  • Storage tank/cistern: Food-grade, opaque, sealed with overflow management
  • Overflow pipe: Directs excess water away from foundation (must not create erosion issues)
  • Distribution system: Pump and filtration for irrigation or household use
  • Treatment (for potable use): UV sterilizer, chlorination, carbon filter

Rainwater Harvesting System Costs

  • Basic passive system (gutters to tank for irrigation): $1,000-$3,000
  • Mid-range system (first-flush, filtration, pump): $3,000-$7,000
  • Full potable system (treatment, testing, certified installation): $8,000-$20,000+

💡 Smart Integration

The best approach for most California properties is to use rainwater harvesting to supplement well water, not replace it. Collect rainwater for irrigation and outdoor use (reducing well demand), while relying on your well for household water. This maximizes water independence without the complexity and cost of potable rainwater treatment systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

When do I need a water storage tank for my well?

You need a water storage tank when your well's recovery rate (GPM) can't keep up with peak demand—common with low-yield wells producing under 5 GPM. Storage tanks are also essential for fire protection (requiring 2,500+ gallons on hand), irrigation systems that need high flow rates, backup water supply during power outages or pump failures, and properties with intermittent well production. In California, many rural properties install storage tanks to meet fire code requirements or to provide adequate water for large households, livestock, or agriculture despite having slow-recovering wells.

What size water storage tank do I need for my well?

Storage tank sizing depends on your peak daily water usage and well recovery rate. Calculate: (Daily usage in gallons) ÷ (Well recovery rate in GPM × 60 × 24). For example, if you use 400 gallons/day and your well produces 3 GPM continuously, you need about 1-2 days of storage (500-1,000 gallons) for safety. Add more capacity for fire protection (2,500+ gallons), irrigation (1,000-5,000+ gallons), or extended backup needs. Most residential systems range from 500-5,000 gallons. Household size rule of thumb: 100-150 gallons per person per day for peak demand plus 20-30% buffer.

How much does a water storage tank cost?

Water storage tank costs vary by material, size, and installation: Polyethylene tanks (most common) range from $500-$2,000 for 500-2,500 gallons. Fiberglass tanks cost $1,000-$4,000 for similar sizes. Steel tanks run $2,000-$8,000 depending on size and coating. Concrete cisterns cost $3,000-$10,000+ installed, especially for underground installation. Add $500-$3,000 for professional installation, plumbing connections, pumps, and pressure systems. Underground installation costs more than above-ground. Total project costs typically range from $1,500 for a basic 500-gallon polyethylene setup to $15,000+ for large underground concrete cisterns with full pressure systems.

What's the difference between a cistern and a water storage tank?

A cistern is a specific type of water storage tank, traditionally designed for collecting and storing rainwater or hauled water, and typically installed underground or partially buried. Modern usage often uses the terms interchangeably, but cisterns historically refer to unpressurized bulk storage for gravity-fed systems or manual collection. Water storage tanks can be any container holding water—above-ground or below-ground, pressurized or unpressurized. In well systems, storage tanks are usually paired with booster pumps for pressure, while cisterns might feed gravity-fed systems. Functionally, both store water; the main difference is historical context and typical installation method.

Can I use a storage tank with a low-yield well?

Yes—storage tanks are the ideal solution for low-yield wells. A well producing just 1-3 GPM can't supply a typical household's peak demand (showers, dishwasher, and laundry running simultaneously can demand 8-15 GPM), but it can fill a storage tank slowly over time. The tank provides high-flow capacity when needed, while the well refills it gradually. Size the tank to hold 1-2 days of your household's water usage. Install a float switch or level sensor to prevent the well from running dry while refilling. Pair with a booster pump and pressure tank for consistent household pressure. This setup lets a slow well serve a normal household effectively.

Do I need a permit to install a water storage tank in California?

Yes, most California counties require permits for water storage tank installation, especially for tanks over 500 gallons or underground installations. Requirements vary by county—San Diego County typically requires building permits for tanks over 5,000 gallons, structural permits for elevated tanks, electrical permits for pump installations, and plumbing permits for connections to household systems. You'll need engineered plans for large or elevated tanks, setback compliance (usually 10+ feet from property lines), and fire department approval if used for fire protection. Rainwater catchment systems have additional regulations. Underground tanks often require septic clearance certifications. Always check with your local building department before installation to avoid costly compliance issues.

How do I prevent bacteria growth in my water storage tank?

Prevent bacteria and algae growth in water storage tanks by: (1) Using opaque tanks that block sunlight—UV light promotes algae. (2) Installing sealed, food-grade tanks with screened vents to prevent debris and insects. (3) Maintaining water circulation—stagnant water breeds bacteria. (4) Keeping tanks full or at least half-full to minimize air exposure. (5) Chlorinating water—maintain 0.2-0.5 ppm residual chlorine. (6) Cleaning tanks annually: drain, scrub with dilute chlorine solution, rinse thoroughly. (7) Installing first-flush diverters on rainwater systems. (8) Using UV sterilization or chlorination systems at the point of use. (9) Testing water quarterly for bacteria. (10) Keeping the tank cool—bacteria multiply faster in warm water. Properly maintained tanks can store safe water for months.

Can I use rainwater to fill my storage tank in California?

Yes, rainwater harvesting is legal and encouraged in California under AB 1750 and AB 2363, but regulations apply. Rainwater can be collected from roofs and stored in cisterns for landscape irrigation, toilet flushing, and non-potable outdoor uses without special permits in most areas. For potable (drinking water) use, you need proper filtration, treatment (UV sterilization and/or chlorination), testing, and local health department approval—requirements are strict. Systems must include first-flush diverters, debris filters, sealed storage, and overflow management. Some HOAs and local ordinances restrict rainwater collection, so check local rules. Size your system based on annual rainfall: San Diego County averages 10-15 inches/year, so a 1,000 sq ft roof yields about 6,000-9,000 gallons annually. Best used to supplement well water, not replace it.
