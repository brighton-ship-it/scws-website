Day 1: The Basics

Locate Your Wellhead

Before anything else, find your well. It might be:

  • Under a well cap — A sealed cover at ground level, usually 6-8 inches in diameter
  • In a well house — A small shed protecting the pressure tank and equipment
  • Near the pressure tank — Follow pipes from your pressure tank; they lead to the well

Mark the location. You'll need to find it quickly if problems arise.

Check the Pressure Tank

The pressure tank is usually in your garage, basement, or a well house. Look for:

  • Size — Note the gallons (usually 20-80 gallons for residential)
  • Pressure gauge — Should read 40-60 PSI when pump is off
  • Age — Tanks last 10-15 years; look for a date or condition issues
  • Waterlogging — Tap the tank; if it's mostly water (heavy/solid), the bladder may be failed

Watch the Pump Cycle

Turn on a faucet and watch your pressure gauge. The pump should:

  • Turn ON around 30-40 PSI (cut-in)
  • Turn OFF around 50-60 PSI (cut-out)
  • Run for at least 1-2 minutes before shutting off

Red flags: If the pump cycles on and off every few seconds (short cycling), or runs continuously without building pressure, you have a problem that needs immediate attention.

Week 1: Gather Information

Find Your Well Records

Well records tell you critical information: depth, flow rate, casing size, and when it was drilled. Here's where to look:

Previous Owner

Ask for any documentation: well driller reports, water test results, maintenance records, pump installation paperwork. Sellers often have a folder of this information.

County Records

California requires well drillers to file completion reports. Contact:

  • San Diego County: Department of Environmental Health, Well Permitting — (858) 565-5145
  • Riverside County: Environmental Health, Well Program — (888) 722-4234
  • San Bernardino County: Environmental Health Services — (800) 442-2283

Request your well log by providing the property address. Records typically cost $10-25 and take 1-2 weeks.

Check Your Property Documents

Your escrow file or title documents may include:

  • Well disclosure statements (required in California)
  • Recent water quality test results
  • Home inspection notes about the well
  • Well sharing agreements (if applicable)

Week 2: Test Your Water

Why Testing Matters

Even if the seller provided test results, you need your own baseline. Water quality can change with seasons, drought conditions, and nearby land use. Plus, you want documentation dated when you took ownership.

Essential Tests for New Owners

Test Why Cost
Coliform Bacteria Safety — presence indicates contamination $30-50
Nitrates Safety — especially critical with infants $25-40
pH & Hardness Affects plumbing, appliances, water quality $20-30
Iron & Manganese Causes staining, metallic taste $25-40

Optional Tests Based on Location

  • Agricultural areas (Ramona, Valley Center, Fallbrook): Pesticides, arsenic
  • Granite areas (Julian, Palomar Mountain): Radon, uranium
  • Near septic systems: E. coli, nitrates (higher concern)
  • Near old orchards: Lead, arsenic (historical pesticide use)

Understanding Your Well System

The Components

Your well system has several key parts:

The Well Itself: A drilled hole with casing (pipe) that extends into the water table. Depth varies — in San Diego County, residential wells typically range from 200-600 feet.

Submersible Pump: Sits at the bottom of the well, pushing water up. Typical lifespan is 15-25 years. Most residential pumps are ½ to 1 ½ horsepower.

Pressure Tank: Stores water under pressure so the pump doesn't run every time you open a faucet. Contains a rubber bladder that separates air and water.

Pressure Switch: Turns the pump on when pressure drops (around 30-40 PSI) and off when it reaches the set point (50-60 PSI).

Control Box: For ½ HP+ pumps, this box (usually near the pressure tank) contains the starting capacitor and relay.

Normal vs. Warning Signs

Normal Warning Sign
Pump runs 2-5 minutes then shuts off Pump cycles on/off every few seconds
Slight pressure drop during heavy use Pressure drops drastically or won't build
Clear water, no odor Brown, cloudy, or smelly water
Occasional air spurts when pump first cycles Constant sputtering at faucets
Electric bill stays consistent Sudden spike in electric usage

Ongoing Maintenance Schedule

Monthly

  • Check pressure gauge readings
  • Listen for unusual pump sounds
  • Note any changes in water quality

Annually

  • Water quality test (bacteria, nitrates minimum)
  • Check pressure tank air charge
  • Inspect wellhead seal and cap
  • Review electric bills for pump run time changes

Every 3-5 Years

  • Professional well inspection
  • Flow rate test (compare to baseline)
  • Comprehensive water quality panel
  • Check pressure switch contacts

Common Questions from New Well Owners

Is my well water safe to drink?

It's likely safe, but test first. Most San Diego County wells produce excellent water. Testing confirms there are no bacteria, nitrates, or contaminants. If issues are found, treatment options exist for almost any water quality problem.

How much water can I use?

Depends on your well's flow rate. A well producing 5 GPM (gallons per minute) yields 300 gallons per hour — plenty for normal household use. Lower flow rates may require a storage tank system. Your well inspection should include flow rate testing.

What if the previous owner didn't maintain the well?

Wells are fairly resilient. Even neglected systems often just need basic maintenance to restore proper function. The worst case is usually a pump that needs replacement sooner than expected. Get an inspection to know what you're working with.

Will my well run dry?

Properly drilled wells in good aquifers rarely run dry. However, extended droughts can lower water tables. If your flow rate was marginal when drilled, you may experience reduced flow during dry years. This is where knowing your well's original specs helps.

Do I need a water softener?

Depends on your water hardness. Much of San Diego County has hard water (10-25 grains per gallon). Softeners prevent scale buildup in pipes and appliances. Your water test will tell you if one is needed.

Red Flags: When to Call Immediately

Contact a well professional right away if you notice:

  • No water at all — Could be pump failure, power issue, or well problem
  • Sudden water discoloration — May indicate well damage or contamination
  • Electrical issues — Tripped breakers, burning smell, or sparking
  • Sewage or chemical odors — Possible contamination requiring immediate testing
  • Flooding near wellhead — Risk of surface water contaminating your well

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check first when I inherit a well?

Start with water quality testing (bacteria, nitrates, minerals), then locate all well records, check the pressure tank and pressure switch settings, inspect the wellhead for damage or contamination risks, and verify the pump is cycling normally. A professional inspection provides a complete baseline for your new well system.

How do I find records for my inherited well?

Check with the previous owner first. Then contact your county's Environmental Health or Water Resources department. In San Diego County, well records are maintained by the Department of Environmental Health. Riverside County keeps records at the Environmental Health department. Records show well depth, casing size, driller information, and original flow rate.

Should I test the water if the previous owner said it was fine?

Yes, always test water when you take ownership. Conditions change, and you need your own baseline. At minimum, test for coliform bacteria, nitrates, pH, and hardness. In agricultural areas, also test for pesticides. Testing costs $75-200 and takes 1-2 weeks for results.

What does a well inspection cost?

A comprehensive well inspection typically costs $200-400 in San Diego County. This includes visual inspection of all components, pump performance testing, flow rate measurement, electrical system check, and water quality sampling. It's money well spent to understand your system before problems arise.
