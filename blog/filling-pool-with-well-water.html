You've just installed a beautiful new pool, and now you face a critical question: should you fill it with well water or pay for delivery? For San Diego County homeowners with private wells, using well water to fill a 15,000-20,000 gallon swimming pool is tempting—it's essentially free water, right? But the reality is more complex. Yes, you can fill a pool with well water, but success depends on your well's flow rate, recovery capacity, and critically, your water quality. Fill without proper testing and pre-treatment, and you could wake up to rust-colored stains on your pristine new plaster, stubborn mineral deposits, or chemistry so out of balance you'll spend hundreds fixing it. This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know about filling pools with well water—from calculating how long it takes at various GPM rates to preventing iron staining, understanding the impact on your well system, comparing costs with delivery, and mastering the initial chemical balancing that sets up your pool for success.

🏊 Quick Answer: Most residential wells (5-15 GPM) can fill a pool, but it takes 12-50+ hours depending on flow rate. The bigger concern is water quality—iron, manganese, and high hardness can permanently stain and damage your pool. Always test first.

Can You Fill a Pool with Well Water?

Yes, you can fill a swimming pool with well water—millions of pool owners with private wells do it successfully every year. However, whether you should depends on three critical factors:

1. Well Capacity and Flow Rate

Your well must produce sufficient water volume over an extended period. A typical residential pool holds 10,000-25,000 gallons. Even with a robust 15 GPM well, filling a 15,000-gallon pool takes about 16-17 hours of continuous pumping. Most residential wells produce 5-15 GPM, which means fill times of 17-50 hours.

The question isn't just "how much water" but "how long can my well sustain that flow?" If your well's recovery rate (how fast the aquifer refills it) is slower than your pump's flow rate, you'll eventually run dry and need to fill in stages.

2. Water Quality

This is where most well owners encounter problems. Municipal water used by delivery companies is treated and balanced. Your well water? It could contain:

  • Iron: Even 0.3 mg/L can cause permanent rust stains when oxidized by chlorine
  • Manganese: Creates purple or black stains on pool surfaces
  • Copper: Produces blue-green staining, especially on plaster
  • High hardness: Calcium and magnesium cause scaling and make water balancing difficult
  • Low or high pH: Requires significant chemical adjustment
  • High TDS (total dissolved solids): Can make achieving proper chemistry nearly impossible

⚠️ Critical Warning: Test Before You Fill

Iron staining from well water is one of the most common—and most regrettable—pool problems. Once rust stains set into plaster or pebble-tec, they're extremely difficult and expensive to remove. A $50 water test before filling can save you $1,000+ in stain removal or resurfacing.

3. Your Timeframe

Pool delivery trucks can fill a pool in 2-3 hours. Your well? Plan on 1-3 days for most residential systems. If you need the pool ready for a party this weekend, delivery is your only realistic option. But if you have a week or two, well water is perfectly viable.

Bottom line: Well water works great if you have adequate flow, decent water quality (or pre-treatment), and time. It doesn't work if you have low yield, high iron/manganese, or need immediate filling.

GPM Requirements and Fill Times

Understanding your well's flow rate—measured in gallons per minute (GPM)—is essential for planning your pool fill. Here's how to calculate fill time and what to expect at various flow rates.

Calculating Your Pool's Volume

Rectangular pools:
Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Average Depth (ft) × 7.5 = Gallons

Round pools:
Diameter (ft) × Diameter (ft) × Average Depth (ft) × 5.9 = Gallons

Oval pools:
Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Average Depth (ft) × 6.7 = Gallons

Example: A rectangular 20' × 40' pool with 5' average depth = 20 × 40 × 5 × 7.5 = 30,000 gallons

Don't Know Your Well's GPM?

Most well owners don't know their exact flow rate. Here's how to find out:

  • Check well records: Your well drilling report should list the tested yield in GPM
  • Bucket test: Fill a 5-gallon bucket and time it. If it takes 30 seconds, that's 10 GPM; if 60 seconds, that's 5 GPM
  • Well pump tag: Your pump's nameplate might list flow rate (though actual delivery can be lower)
  • Call a pro: Well service companies can test flow rate accurately

Water Quality Issues with Well Water

The biggest difference between well water and municipal delivery isn't volume—it's chemistry. Here are the most common well water problems that affect pools:

Iron Staining (The #1 Problem)

Iron in well water is the most common cause of pool staining. Even small amounts—as little as 0.3 mg/L (0.3 parts per million)—can cause visible rust staining when chlorine oxidizes the dissolved iron.

How it happens: Your well water contains dissolved ferrous iron (clear, invisible). When you add chlorine to sanitize the pool, it oxidizes the iron into ferric iron (rust), which appears as orange/brown stains on plaster, tile, and pool walls.

What it looks like: Rust-colored streaks, spots, or overall orange discoloration. Stains typically appear within hours or days of adding chlorine.

How to prevent: Test for iron before filling. If iron is present, use a pre-filter during filling and add a metal sequestrant to keep iron in suspension. Never add chlorine until you've addressed iron.

Safe levels: Iron should be below 0.3 mg/L (ideally 0). Above 0.3 mg/L, pre-treatment is highly recommended.

For detailed solutions, see our guide: Iron in Well Water: Causes & Treatment

Manganese Staining

Manganese behaves similarly to iron but creates purple, brown, or black stains instead of orange. It's less common than iron but equally problematic.

  • Safe level: Below 0.05 mg/L
  • Stain color: Purple, brown, or black
  • Treatment: Same as iron—pre-filtration or sequestrants

Copper Staining

Copper in well water can come from natural sources or corrosion of copper pipes. It causes blue-green staining, particularly on white plaster.

  • Safe level: Below 0.1 mg/L (ideally 0)
  • Stain color: Blue-green or teal
  • Common in: Newer copper plumbing with acidic water

High Mineral Content (Hardness)

San Diego County well water is notoriously hard—often 15-30+ grains per gallon. High hardness (calcium and magnesium) causes:

  • Scaling: White, crusty deposits on tile, waterline, and equipment
  • Cloudy water: Calcium carbonate precipitation when pH rises
  • Chemistry imbalance: Makes achieving proper pH and alkalinity difficult
  • Reduced chlorine efficiency: Hard water interferes with sanitizer effectiveness

Ideal pool calcium hardness: 200-400 ppm
San Diego well water: Often 200-500+ ppm before any pool chemicals

Starting with very hard water limits your ability to adjust chemistry. If your well water hardness exceeds 400 ppm, you may need to partially dilute with delivered water or use a pre-softener.

pH Imbalance

Well water pH varies widely based on geology. San Diego County wells typically range from 6.5 to 8.5, though some are more extreme.

  • Ideal pool pH: 7.2-7.8
  • Low pH (acidic) well water: Corrodes pool equipment, etches plaster, causes eye/skin irritation
  • High pH (alkaline) well water: Causes scaling, reduces chlorine effectiveness, creates cloudy water

pH is relatively easy to adjust with chemicals, but starting far from ideal means higher chemical costs initially.

High Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)

TDS measures all dissolved minerals and salts in water. High TDS makes balancing pool chemistry extremely difficult because you can't remove dissolved solids—you can only dilute them.

  • Ideal for pools: Below 1,500 ppm
  • Maximum acceptable: 2,000-2,500 ppm
  • Problems above 2,500 ppm: Cloudy water, difficulty maintaining chlorine, corrosion or scaling

If your well water TDS exceeds 2,000 ppm, filling exclusively with well water may not be advisable. Consider mixing with delivered water.

💡 Pro Tip: Test First, Fill Second

Spend $50-$100 on comprehensive water testing before filling your pool. Test for: iron, manganese, copper, pH, total alkalinity, calcium hardness, and TDS. This small investment can save you thousands in stain removal, resurfacing, or water replacement.

Pre-Treatment Before Filling Your Pool

If your well water test reveals iron, manganese, or other problem minerals, don't panic—you have several pre-treatment options to fill safely:

1. Sediment and Iron Pre-Filters

The most effective approach for well water with iron or manganese is using a dedicated pre-filter during the fill process.

How it works: Attach a garden hose filter or dedicated cartridge filter to your hose before it enters the pool. The filter media oxidizes and captures iron, manganese, and sediment before it enters your pool.

Types available:

  • Garden hose sediment filters: $20-$50, good for sediment and light iron
  • Iron removal cartridge filters: $100-$300, handles higher iron levels
  • Inline oxidizing filters: $150-$400, best for heavy iron/manganese

Effectiveness: Can remove 90-95% of iron and manganese when properly sized. You may need multiple filter cartridges for a full pool fill.

2. Metal Sequestrants (Chelating Agents)

If pre-filtration isn't practical or your iron levels are moderate (under 1 mg/L), metal sequestrants can keep metals in solution so they don't stain.

How it works: Sequestrants bind to dissolved metals (iron, copper, manganese) and keep them suspended in water, preventing oxidation and staining. Think of it as putting the metals in protective "cages."

Popular products: Jack's Magic The Blue Stuff, ProTeam Metal Magic, CuLator Metal Eliminator, Natural Chemistry Stain Free

Application: Add sequestrant as you begin filling (before chlorine). Follow product dosing—typically 1 quart per 10,000 gallons for initial treatment.

Important: Sequestrants don't remove metals—they just prevent staining. You'll need to maintain sequestrant levels throughout the season. Not a permanent solution for high metal content.

3. Whole-House Water Treatment Systems

If you have ongoing well water quality issues (not just for pool filling), consider installing a permanent treatment system:

  • Iron filters: $800-$2,500 installed—removes iron and manganese from all household water
  • Water softeners: $1,000-$3,000—reduces hardness (but adds sodium)
  • Combined systems: $2,000-$5,000—address multiple issues

These make sense if you'll refill the pool annually, top it off regularly, or have quality issues affecting your home plumbing.

4. Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid) Treatment

If you accidentally fill with iron-laden water and develop stains, vitamin C can remove them:

For spot stains: Rub a vitamin C tablet directly on stains underwater—they'll often disappear immediately. This confirms iron staining.

For whole-pool treatment: Add 1-3 lbs of ascorbic acid per 10,000 gallons. Circulate with pump, brush stains, and they should fade within 24-48 hours.

Note: This treats existing stains but doesn't remove iron from the water. You must follow up with sequestrant or filtration to prevent re-staining.

Impact on Your Well System

Filling a pool puts significant demand on your well—you're asking it to produce 10,000-25,000 gallons (or more) continuously. Understanding the impact helps you protect your well system.

Risk of Running the Well Dry

The biggest concern is depleting your well faster than it can recharge. This happens when your pump's flow rate exceeds the aquifer's recovery rate.

⚠️ What Happens When a Well Runs Dry:

  • Pump runs without water, potentially causing damage or burnout
  • Sediment gets stirred up and drawn into your system
  • Air enters the plumbing, causing sputtering and pressure tank issues
  • In extreme cases, pump failure requiring replacement ($800-$3,000+)

How to protect your well:

  • Know your recovery rate: This should be on your well drilling report. If your well recovers at 5 GPM but your pump delivers 10 GPM, you can pump at 10 GPM for a limited time before you outpace recovery.
  • Fill gradually: Instead of continuous filling, run for 6-8 hours, pause for 4-6 hours to let the well recover, then resume.
  • Monitor water level: If you have access to your well, check the water level periodically. If it's dropping significantly, slow down or pause.
  • Install low-level cutoff: Some well systems have automatic shutoffs that protect the pump if water level drops too low.
  • Avoid filling during droughts: If groundwater levels are already low, filling a pool could push your well over the edge.

Pump Stress and Overheating

Submersible well pumps are designed for intermittent use with periodic breaks. Running continuously for 24-48 hours can stress the motor:

  • Heat buildup: Pumps are cooled by the surrounding water. Extended runtime can cause overheating.
  • Wear and tear: Continuous operation accelerates wear on bearings and seals.
  • Electrical stress: Prolonged current flow can stress wiring and motor windings.

Protection strategy: Fill in stages with 2-4 hour breaks every 8-12 hours to let the pump cool and the well recover. This extends pump life and ensures safe operation.

Household Water Availability

While filling your pool, your household still needs water for drinking, cooking, showers, toilets, and laundry. A pool fill can temporarily impact household water availability.

  • Reduced pressure: With the pool hose running, you may notice lower pressure at indoor fixtures
  • Delayed recovery: If someone flushes a toilet or runs a shower while filling, the pressure tank may take longer to recharge
  • Cloudy water: Continuous pumping can stir up fine sediment, causing temporary cloudiness

Solution: Fill during low-use times (overnight, while you're at work) and avoid simultaneous heavy water use like laundry or long showers.

Well Water vs. Water Delivery Costs

Let's compare the real costs of filling a pool with well water versus having it delivered by truck.

Well Water Costs

Electricity: Running a 1 HP submersible pump for 25 hours (filling a 15,000-gallon pool at 10 GPM) uses approximately 18-20 kWh of electricity. At $0.30/kWh (San Diego rates), that's about $5-$6 in electricity. For larger pools or lower flow rates, expect $10-$30 in electricity costs.

Pre-treatment (if needed):

  • Garden hose filter cartridges: $20-$50
  • Iron removal filters: $100-$300
  • Metal sequestrant: $25-$75 per pool
  • Water testing: $25-$100

Total well water cost for 15,000-gallon pool: $50-$400 depending on water quality and pre-treatment needs

Water Delivery Costs

Pool water delivery companies charge per 1,000 gallons or per truckload. Standard delivery trucks hold 6,000-9,000 gallons.

Typical pricing:

  • Per-load pricing: $200-$600 per truck (6,000-9,000 gallons)
  • Per-gallon pricing: $0.03-$0.08 per gallon
  • Delivery fees: $50-$150 depending on distance
  • Minimum orders: Many require 6,000-gallon minimum

When Delivery Makes Sense

Despite the higher cost, water delivery is the better choice if:

  • Low well yield: Under 5 GPM or poor recovery rate
  • High iron content: Above 1-2 mg/L (pre-treatment becomes complex and expensive)
  • Very hard water: Above 400-500 ppm calcium hardness
  • Time constraint: Need pool ready immediately (delivery takes 2-3 hours vs. 1-3 days)
  • Drought conditions: Well levels are already low and you need to conserve
  • First fill of new plaster: Some pool builders recommend municipal water for initial curing

💡 Hybrid Approach

Consider a combination: use delivered municipal water for the initial 50-70% fill (ensuring good base chemistry), then top off with well water. This gives you better water quality while still saving $200-$600 compared to 100% delivery.

Chemical Balancing After Filling with Well Water

Once your pool is filled, proper chemical balancing is critical—especially with well water, which typically has very different chemistry than municipal water.

The Critical First 24-48 Hours

The initial chemical treatment sets the foundation for your pool's long-term water quality. Here's the proper sequence:

Step 1: Test Everything (Before Adding Anything)

Test your filled pool water for:

  • pH (target: 7.2-7.8, ideal 7.4-7.6)
  • Total Alkalinity (target: 80-120 ppm)
  • Calcium Hardness (target: 200-400 ppm)
  • Cyanuric Acid/Stabilizer (target: 30-50 ppm for outdoor pools)
  • Free Chlorine (target: 1-3 ppm)
  • Metals (iron, copper if well water)

Use a quality test kit (Taylor K-2006 or similar) or take a sample to a pool store for professional analysis.

Step 2: Adjust Total Alkalinity First

Alkalinity acts as a pH buffer. Balance it before adjusting pH.

  • To raise alkalinity: Add sodium bicarbonate (baking soda). 1.5 lbs per 10,000 gallons raises alkalinity by ~10 ppm.
  • To lower alkalinity: Add muriatic acid. 1 quart per 10,000 gallons lowers alkalinity by ~10 ppm (also lowers pH).

Wait 4-6 hours, circulate, then retest before proceeding.

Step 3: Adjust pH

Well water pH can be anywhere from 6.0 to 9.0. Target 7.4-7.6.

  • To raise pH: Add soda ash (sodium carbonate). 6 oz per 10,000 gallons raises pH by ~0.2.
  • To lower pH: Add muriatic acid. 1 cup per 10,000 gallons lowers pH by ~0.2.

Make adjustments gradually. Large pH swings can cause temporary cloudiness.

Step 4: Adjust Calcium Hardness (If Necessary)

Most San Diego well water already has high calcium (200-500+ ppm). If it's below 200 ppm, add calcium chloride to prevent plaster etching. If above 400 ppm, there's little you can do except dilution with softer water.

  • To raise hardness: Add calcium chloride. 1 lb per 10,000 gallons raises calcium hardness by ~10 ppm.
  • To lower hardness: Dilute with softer water or use a reverse osmosis mobile service (expensive).

Step 5: Add Cyanuric Acid (Stabilizer)

Outdoor pools need stabilizer to prevent chlorine from degrading in sunlight. Well water has zero stabilizer.

  • Add cyanuric acid granules or liquid to reach 30-50 ppm
  • 13 oz (1 lb) per 10,000 gallons raises CYA by ~10 ppm
  • Takes 24-48 hours to fully dissolve—add to skimmer or pre-dissolve in bucket

Step 6: Sanitize with Chlorine (LAST STEP)

IMPORTANT: If you have iron or metals in your well water, DO NOT add chlorine until you've added a metal sequestrant. Chlorine oxidizes iron instantly, causing permanent stains.

  • Add metal sequestrant first if metals are present
  • Wait 24 hours
  • Then add chlorine to reach 1-3 ppm free chlorine
  • Use liquid chlorine, granular dichlor, or trichlor tablets
  • Run filter continuously for first 24-48 hours

⚠️ Critical Mistake to Avoid

Never shock or super-chlorinate a pool filled with well water until you've confirmed there's no iron or added sequestrant. High chlorine + iron = instant rust stains. Start with low chlorine (1-2 ppm) for the first week while monitoring for any discoloration.

Initial Chemical Costs

Expect to spend $100-$300 on initial chemicals for a newly filled pool:

  • pH adjusters (acid or soda ash): $15-$40
  • Alkalinity increaser (baking soda): $10-$25
  • Calcium hardness increaser: $15-$30 (if needed)
  • Cyanuric acid stabilizer: $20-$50
  • Chlorine (initial stock): $30-$80
  • Metal sequestrant: $25-$75 (if needed)
  • Test kit: $25-$70

Well water typically requires more alkalinity and pH adjustment than municipal water, so budget accordingly.

Ongoing Pool Maintenance with Well Water

Once your pool is balanced and operating, ongoing maintenance with well water requires a few special considerations:

Topping Off Water Loss

Pools lose 1-4 inches of water per week to evaporation, splashing, and backwashing. You'll add hundreds to thousands of gallons throughout the season.

  • If using well water for top-offs: You're continuously adding hard water, increasing calcium hardness over time. Monitor hardness monthly and be prepared to partially drain and refill every few years if it climbs too high.
  • If using delivered softened water: Expensive for regular top-offs but maintains better chemistry balance.
  • Best practice: Use well water for routine top-offs but keep sequestrant levels maintained if you have iron/metals.

Managing Rising Hardness

Calcium hardness only goes up—never down—unless you dilute or drain. With San Diego well water starting at 200-500 ppm and evaporation concentrating minerals, hardness can climb to 600-800+ ppm within a few years.

Solutions:

  • Partial drain/refill: Every 2-3 years, drain 1/3 to 1/2 of pool water and refill to dilute TDS and hardness
  • Reverse osmosis mobile service: Filters pool water on-site to remove dissolved minerals ($500-$1,500 depending on pool size)
  • Hardness reducer chemicals: Limited effectiveness; work for moderate hardness only

Metal Stain Prevention

If your well water has iron or metals, maintain sequestrant levels year-round:

  • Add maintenance doses weekly or monthly per product instructions
  • Test for metals quarterly
  • After heavy rain or well maintenance, add extra sequestrant (sediment disturbance can release metals)
  • Keep a vitamin C stain removal kit on hand for spot treatment

Filter Maintenance

Well water with high mineral content or sediment clogs filters faster than municipal water:

  • Sand filters: Backwash more frequently (every 1-2 weeks instead of monthly)
  • Cartridge filters: Clean every 2-4 weeks with muriatic acid soak to remove mineral buildup
  • DE filters: Backwash and recharge as needed; expect more frequent maintenance

Consider a pre-filter or sediment sock on the fill line if your well water is cloudy or has visible sediment.

When NOT to Use Your Well

Despite the cost savings, there are situations where using well water is inadvisable or impossible:

Low Yield Wells (Under 3-5 GPM)

If your well produces less than 3-5 GPM, filling a pool becomes impractical. At 3 GPM, a 15,000-gallon pool takes 83 hours (3.5 days) of continuous pumping—risking well depletion and household water interruption. Use delivery instead.

High Iron or Manganese (Above 2-3 mg/L)

Above 2 mg/L iron or 0.5 mg/L manganese, pre-treatment becomes complex and expensive. You'd need substantial filtration ($500-$1,500+ in equipment) or very aggressive sequestrant dosing. Delivery with municipal water is often more practical and cost-effective.

Extreme Hardness (Above 500 ppm)

Wells with calcium hardness above 500 ppm will make pool chemistry extremely difficult to balance. Scaling will be constant, and you'll need frequent partial drain/refills. Consider blending with delivered soft water or using 100% delivery.

Contamination Issues

If your well has bacterial contamination, nitrates, sulfur smell, or other quality issues, don't fill a pool with it. Address the contamination first, then consider pool filling.

Drought or Low Water Table

During droughts or when your well is already struggling to keep up with household demand, filling a 15,000+ gallon pool could push it over the edge. Wait for water levels to recover or use delivery.

New Plaster or Pebble-Tec Pools

Some pool builders specifically recommend using municipal water for the initial fill of new plaster or pebble-tec surfaces during the 28-day curing period. Well water chemistry variations can interfere with proper curing. Follow your builder's recommendation.

Immediate Need

If you need the pool filled today or tomorrow, delivery is your only option. Well filling takes 1-3 days minimum.

💡 When in Doubt, Test First

A $50-$100 water test can tell you definitively whether your well water is suitable for pool filling. If iron is above 0.5 mg/L, hardness above 400 ppm, or TDS above 2,000 ppm, you'll save money and headaches by using delivery or a blended approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fill my pool with well water?

Yes, you can fill a pool with well water, but several factors determine whether it's practical and advisable. You need adequate flow rate (ideally 10+ GPM), sufficient well recovery to maintain flow for many hours, acceptable water quality (low iron, manganese, and hardness), and proper pre-treatment for problem minerals. If your well produces 5 GPM continuously, it will take about 33 hours to fill a 10,000-gallon pool. Check your well's capacity, test water quality first, and consider pre-filtration if you have iron or high mineral content to avoid staining your new pool surface.

How many gallons per minute do I need to fill a pool?

Most residential wells produce 5-15 GPM, which is sufficient to fill a pool—it just takes time. At 5 GPM, a 15,000-gallon pool takes 50 hours; at 10 GPM, 25 hours; at 15 GPM, about 17 hours. The key isn't just GPM but sustained flow—your well must recover fast enough to maintain flow for the entire fill time. If your well runs dry after a few hours, you'll need to fill in stages (a few hours per day over several days) or supplement with delivered water. Calculate your pool volume: Length × Width × Average Depth × 7.5 = Gallons.

Will well water stain my pool?

Well water can stain pool surfaces if it contains iron, manganese, or copper. Iron causes rust-colored (orange/brown) stains; manganese creates purple/black stains; copper produces blue-green stains. Even small amounts—as little as 0.3 mg/L of iron—can cause staining when chlorine oxidizes these dissolved metals. The stains appear on plaster, tile, and pool walls and are extremely difficult to remove. To prevent staining, test your well water for metals before filling, use a pre-filter or metal sequestrant during filling, and consider a sequestering agent in the initial water chemistry treatment. If your well has iron above 0.3 mg/L, pre-treatment is strongly recommended.

How long does it take to fill a pool with well water?

Fill time depends on your well's flow rate (GPM) and pool size. For a typical 15,000-gallon residential pool: at 5 GPM = 50 hours (2+ days), at 10 GPM = 25 hours (1 day), at 15 GPM = 16.7 hours (under 1 day), at 20 GPM = 12.5 hours (half day). These assume continuous flow. Most homeowners fill over 2-3 days, running the well during off-peak hours to avoid depleting it. If your well recovery rate is slower than your pump's flow rate, you'll need to fill in stages with breaks to let the well recharge. Always monitor water level during filling to avoid running the well dry.

Is it cheaper to fill a pool with well water or have it delivered?

Well water is significantly cheaper than delivery if your well can handle the demand. Well water cost: mostly electricity ($20-$50 for a large pool fill), plus potential pre-treatment ($100-$300 for filters/chemicals if needed). Water delivery cost: $200-$600 per truckload (typically 6,000-9,000 gallons), so filling a 15,000-gallon pool costs $400-$1,200 in delivery fees alone. Well water saves $300-$1,100+ per fill. However, delivery is faster (2-3 hours vs. 1-3 days) and uses pre-treated municipal water with predictable chemistry. Choose delivery if your well has low yield, high iron content, or you need the pool filled immediately. Use well water if you have adequate flow, acceptable water quality, and time to fill gradually.

What should I test in well water before filling my pool?

Before filling a pool with well water, test for: Iron (should be under 0.3 mg/L to avoid rust staining), Manganese (under 0.05 mg/L to prevent purple/black stains), Copper (under 0.1 mg/L to avoid blue-green stains), Total Hardness (calcium/magnesium—high levels make balancing difficult), pH (ideal 7.2-7.8 to minimize adjustments), Total Dissolved Solids/TDS (under 1,500 ppm preferred), and Alkalinity (affects pH stability). A basic well water test kit costs $25-$50; professional lab testing runs $50-$150 but provides more accurate results. Testing before filling lets you pre-treat problem water and avoid permanent staining or chemistry nightmares.

Should I add a metal sequestrant when filling with well water?

Yes, if your well water contains any detectable iron, manganese, or copper, add a metal sequestrant (also called chelating agent) as you fill. Sequestrants bind to dissolved metals and keep them suspended in solution so chlorine can't oxidize them into visible stains. Add sequestrant before adding chlorine. Popular products include Jack's Magic The Blue Stuff, ProTeam Metal Magic, and CuLator Metal Eliminator. Follow product instructions—typically you add the initial dose at the start of filling and maintain residual levels during the first weeks. Sequestrants don't remove metals; they prevent staining. For heavy metal content (iron above 1 mg/L), consider pre-filtration instead of relying solely on sequestrants.

Can filling a pool damage my well?

Filling a pool won't permanently damage a properly functioning well, but it can stress the system if done improperly. Risks include: running the well dry (if you pump faster than it recharges), which can cause pump damage or sediment intrusion; overworking the pump with continuous multi-day operation (pumps need occasional breaks); depleting the aquifer temporarily in drought conditions. To protect your well: know your recovery rate, fill gradually over multiple days if needed, monitor water level and shut off if the well runs dry, give the pump breaks every 8-12 hours, avoid filling during peak household water use times, and never run the pump dry (install low-level cutoff if possible). After filling, let the well fully recover before resuming normal use.
