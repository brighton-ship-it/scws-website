Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Well Pump Won't Start in Barstow? A High-Desert Troubleshooting Guide

Barstow sits at the crossroads of the Mojave Desert, where the Mojave River, Interstate 15, and the historic Route 66 corridor all converge in San Bernardino County. Out here, private wells are a way of life, especially for the ranches and rural homesteads scattered around Lenwood, Hinkley, and Yermo. When a well pump won't start in this kind of country, it isn't just inconvenient, it can leave a property with no water for livestock or household use in 100-degree heat. Understanding why pumps fail in the high desert, and what to do about it, can save you a long, dry afternoon.

This guide is written specifically for Barstow-area well owners. It covers how the system works, the failure patterns unique to deep desert wells, the checks you can make safely, and when to bring in a licensed C-57 well contractor.

The Anatomy of a Desert Well System

A Barstow well system has the same fundamental parts as any other, but the desert environment puts each one under extra strain:

  • Power supply: A 240-volt circuit, often with a separate fused disconnect, runs the pump. In rural Barstow, long service drops and occasional grid instability make voltage quality a real factor.
  • Pressure switch: Senses tank pressure and signals the pump on and off. Fine windblown sediment can clog the sensing port.
  • Control box and capacitor: Three-wire submersibles use a surface control box; the start capacitor inside takes a beating in desert heat.
  • Pressure tank: Buffers demand so the pump isn't cycling constantly.
  • Motor and drop cable: Because Mojave wells are often 200-600 feet deep, motors here work harder against more total dynamic head than shallow coastal pumps.
  • Well and water level: The Mojave River basin water table can drop during drought, and a falling level near the pump intake will trigger protective shutdowns or cause the pump to run dry.

Why Barstow Pumps Fail to Start

The combination of deep wells, hard mineral-rich water, blowing sand, and a strained summer grid creates a distinctive set of failure patterns. The most common reasons a Barstow pump won't start are:

  • Tripped breaker from voltage swings: Summer heat waves push the grid to its limits, and the resulting sags and surges trip pump breakers and stress motors.
  • Heat-killed start capacitor: A swollen or failed capacitor in a sun-baked control box is one of the most common desert no-starts. You'll often hear a hum without flow.
  • Sediment-clogged pressure switch: Fine Mojave dust and mineral scale block the switch's sensing port so it never calls for the pump.
  • Worn-out deep-set motor: Deeper wells mean harder-working motors. After 10-15 years, windings fail, often tripping the breaker on startup.
  • Drop-cable damage: Decades of thermal cycling and the occasional rodent at the wellhead cause shorts and opens in the wiring.
  • Low static water level: Drought-driven drawdown can leave the pump intake near or above the water line, triggering dry-run shutdowns.

Checks You Can Safely Make

Before calling, a few simple checks can narrow things down. Keep clear of any live 240-volt wiring.

  1. Reset the breaker once. Locate the double-pole pump breaker and reset it a single time. If it trips again, leave it off.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Zero pressure with no recovery suggests the pump never started.
  3. Check for grid-wide issues. In rural Barstow, confirm whether you have a broader power problem before assuming the pump is at fault.
  4. Feel the control box (carefully, cover closed). An unusually hot box or a burnt-electrical smell points to a failed capacitor or relay.
  5. Listen at the pressure switch. A click followed by a hum and then silence often means a start-component failure.

Caution: Never open energized equipment, and never repeatedly reset a tripping breaker. In dry desert conditions, an electrical fault near the wellhead is a genuine fire risk.

Why Mojave Water Chemistry Wears Pumps Out Faster

One factor many Barstow homeowners overlook is water chemistry. The groundwater pulled from the Mojave River basin tends to carry high levels of dissolved minerals, including calcium, magnesium, and iron. Over years of service, this hard water deposits scale on impellers, intake screens, and check valves. Scale buildup forces the motor to work harder to move the same volume of water, which raises operating temperature and accelerates winding fatigue. A pump that has been straining against scale for a decade is far more likely to fail to start one morning than a pump drawing clean, soft water.

If your Barstow well has a history of mineral staining or reduced flow, the eventual no-start may be the last symptom of a long, slow decline rather than a sudden electrical event. In those cases we often recommend pairing the pump replacement with a sediment or iron treatment system so the new pump enjoys a longer service life.

Protecting a Replacement Pump in the High Desert

When a Barstow pump finally fails, it is worth doing the replacement right. We size every new pump to the well's tested yield and total dynamic head rather than simply matching the old horsepower, because an oversized pump draws the water level down too quickly and can pull air, while an undersized one runs constantly. For desert installations we frequently add a few protective measures: a properly rated control box mounted in shade or a ventilated enclosure, a surge-protected disconnect to guard against the grid swings that plague the Mojave in summer, and low-water cutoff protection so a dropping water table can never let the motor run dry. These additions cost relatively little up front and routinely add years to a pump's life in this climate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't my well pump start in Barstow?

In the high desert around Barstow, the leading causes are tripped breakers from summer grid strain, sediment-clogged pressure switches, failed start capacitors, and motors worn out from running against a deep water table. A 240-volt pump fault should always be confirmed by a licensed technician.

Are Barstow wells deeper than coastal wells?

Yes. Wells in the Mojave around Barstow and the Mojave River basin are often 200-600 feet deep, and some agricultural wells go deeper. Deeper settings mean higher horsepower and more total dynamic head, which puts extra stress on motors and capacitors.

Can desert heat cause my pump to stop?

Indirectly, yes. Extreme summer heat strains the electrical grid and causes voltage fluctuations that trip breakers and shorten capacitor life. Surface control boxes also run hotter, which accelerates component failure.

Should I keep resetting a breaker that keeps tripping?

No. A breaker that trips repeatedly is warning you of a short or a grounded motor. Resetting it over and over can start an electrical fire. Leave it off and call (760) 440-8520.
