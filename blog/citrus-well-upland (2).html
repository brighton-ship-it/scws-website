Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

How Much Water Citrus Trees Actually Need

Citrus is a thirsty, evergreen crop. Unlike deciduous trees that go fully dormant, orange, lemon, mandarin, and grapefruit trees transpire year-round, and a mature grove in Upland's warm, dry summers carries a heavy water budget. As a planning figure, a mature citrus grove typically uses somewhere in the range of 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the exact number driven by tree age, rootstock, canopy size, and how hot and dry the season runs.

What matters for well sizing is not just the annual total but the peak demand. During a July or August heat wave, evapotranspiration spikes and your irrigation system has to deliver water fast enough to keep the root zone from drying out. A grove that needs a modest flow in spring can require a substantial, sustained pumping rate at the height of summer. We help growers translate acreage and tree count into a realistic gallons-per-minute (GPM) target so the well and pump are matched to the worst case, not the average.

Frost Protection Changes the Math

Upland's foothill location means cold air can settle into the groves on clear winter nights, and citrus is vulnerable to freeze damage. Many growers use water for frost protection, running micro-sprinklers when temperatures approach the danger zone so that the heat released as water freezes helps protect the fruit and wood. Frost protection is one of the most demanding things you can ask of a well, because it can require on the order of 50 or more GPM per acre, all at once, often in the middle of the night for several hours. A well sized only for routine irrigation may not keep up during a hard freeze, which is why frost capability has to be considered up front.

Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage

A productive citrus well is a system, not just a hole in the ground. The three pieces that have to work together are well yield, pump capacity, and storage.

Well Yield

The well itself has to be able to give up water at a sustainable rate without drawing the water level down too far. We evaluate yield through proper testing rather than guesswork, and where an existing well underperforms, options such as hydrofracturing can sometimes improve flow in the right geology.

High-Capacity Pumps

Most commercial citrus operations run a high-capacity submersible pump or, for larger or shallower settings, a vertical turbine pump. These are built to move significant volumes against the pressure an orchard irrigation system requires. Correct pump selection is about matching flow and head to your grove; an oversized pump can cavitate or overdraw the well, while an undersized one leaves trees short during peak demand.

Storage Ponds and Tanks

Because peak irrigation and frost-protection demand can exceed what a well delivers minute-to-minute, many Upland growers add storage. A lined pond or a bank of tanks lets the well fill the reservoir steadily over many hours, and the irrigation system or frost system then draws from that stored volume at a high rate when needed. Storage is often the most cost-effective way to bridge the gap between a steady well and a spiky demand curve.

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is one of the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so water chemistry deserves real attention. The three issues we watch most closely are salinity, chloride, and boron.

  • Salinity (EC): High overall salt content, measured as electrical conductivity, stresses citrus, reduces water uptake, and over time can cut yields. Salt-sensitive crops like citrus need either lower-salinity water or extra leaching to flush salts below the root zone.
  • Chloride: Citrus leaves are prone to chloride accumulation, which shows up as leaf-tip and margin burn. Elevated chloride in irrigation water can damage foliage and weaken trees, especially on sensitive rootstocks.
  • Boron: Boron is a micronutrient at trace levels but toxic to citrus at concentrations only slightly higher, generally a concern above roughly 0.5 to 1 part per million. Boron toxicity also presents as leaf burn and is something we test for when symptoms or local conditions suggest it.

Across the broader Chino and western-county basins, growers sometimes also ask about nitrate, which can be present in groundwater in historically agricultural and former dairy areas. For citrus irrigation, the salinity, chloride, and boron picture is usually the priority. We can sample your well, interpret the lab results in plain language, and recommend blending, treatment, or rootstock and management adjustments where they make sense.

Drought and Groundwater Management

Southern California's recurring droughts put real pressure on private wells. As basins draw down in dry years, static water levels can drop and yields can fall just when citrus needs water most. The western San Bernardino County basins, including the Chino Basin nearby, are actively managed groundwater systems, with oversight through mechanisms such as the Chino Basin Watermaster that govern how much can be pumped and how the basin is replenished. For growers, the practical takeaways are to use water efficiently, monitor your well's performance over time, and address declining yield early rather than during a crisis.

Common Citrus Well Issues We See in Upland

  • Declining yield as a well ages or as drought lowers the water table, leaving the grove short during peak summer demand.
  • Pump wear and failure, often accelerated by sediment, mineral scale, or running a pump that is poorly matched to the well.
  • Clogged drip emitters and micro-sprinklers caused by sediment, mineral precipitation, or biological growth in the lines.
  • Water quality drift, where salinity or chloride creeps up over a season and begins to show on the leaves.
  • Electrical and control problems on older irrigation and pump systems that interrupt watering at the worst possible time.

Maintenance That Protects Your Grove

Preventive maintenance is far cheaper than an emergency during a heat wave. We recommend periodic water testing, regular inspection of the pump and pressure system, cleaning or flushing of filtration and irrigation lines, and tracking your well's flow and drawdown so you can spot a downward trend before it becomes a failure. A well that is monitored is a well that rarely surprises you.

When to Call a Professional

Call us when you notice falling pressure or flow, air spitting from emitters, a pump that short-cycles or runs constantly, rising energy bills, visible leaf burn that points to water chemistry, or any interruption in water during frost season. These are signs that something in the system needs attention, and catching them early usually means a smaller repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many gallons per minute does my Upland citrus grove need?

It depends on acreage, tree maturity, and whether you need frost protection. As a rough guide, mature citrus uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year, and frost protection alone can demand 50 or more GPM per acre at once. We size the well and pump to your specific grove and peak demand.

Can well water be too salty for citrus?

Yes. Citrus is salt-sensitive, and high salinity, chloride, or boron can damage leaves and reduce yield. We test your water and recommend blending, treatment, or management changes when the chemistry is a concern.

Do I need a storage pond or tank for my grove?

Often, yes. Storage lets a steady well meet the spiky demand of peak summer irrigation and frost protection. It is frequently the most economical way to ensure you have enough water exactly when the trees need it.

What can I do if my well yield is dropping?

Have it evaluated promptly. Depending on the cause and geology, solutions can include pump adjustments, well rehabilitation, or hydrofracturing to improve flow. Acting early prevents losing trees during peak season.

Is groundwater pumping regulated in this part of San Bernardino County?

The western county basins, including the nearby Chino Basin, are actively managed groundwater systems. We help growers operate efficiently within that framework and keep their wells productive.

How fast can you respond if my grove loses water?

We offer same-day emergency response. When citrus is going without water in summer heat, time matters, and we move quickly to get your system back online.
