What Causes Iron Bacteria in Wells?

Iron bacteria are naturally occurring microorganisms that combine iron and oxygen to form rust deposits and a slimy biofilm. They enter wells during drilling, pump repairs, or any time the well is opened. Once established, they thrive in the oxygen-rich environment near the water surface and along well casings. Wells with high iron content provide the perfect food source for these bacteria to multiply.

Signs You Have Iron Bacteria

The telltale sign is orange, red, or brown slime in your toilet tank, on faucet aerators, and inside pipes. Water may have a swampy or oily taste and smell. You might notice reduced water pressure from clogged screens and fixtures. Laundry and fixtures develop rust stains that are difficult to remove. An oily sheen on standing water is another common indicator.

Treatment Options for Iron Bacteria

Shock chlorination is the first step—introducing a strong chlorine solution into the well to kill bacteria and break down biofilm. For ongoing protection, continuous chlorine injection systems maintain bacteria-free water. Chemical-free options include ozone injection or UV disinfection systems installed after filtration. Severe cases may require physical cleaning of the well casing and screen.

When to Call a Professional

If shock chlorination doesn't resolve the problem after two attempts, professional intervention is needed. A well contractor can video-inspect your well to assess biofilm buildup and recommend appropriate cleaning methods. They can also install and maintain ongoing treatment systems sized correctly for your water chemistry and usage patterns.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is iron bacteria harmful to drink?

Iron bacteria itself isn't typically harmful to human health, but it creates conditions that can harbor other harmful bacteria. The slimy buildup also causes taste and odor problems, stains fixtures, and can clog pipes and well screens.

What does iron bacteria look like?

Iron bacteria appears as orange, red, or brown slimy deposits in toilet tanks, on well screens, and inside pipes. You may also notice a swampy or oily sheen on standing water and a musty, sewage-like odor.

How do you permanently remove iron bacteria from a well?

Permanent removal requires a multi-step approach: shock chlorination to kill existing bacteria, followed by ongoing treatment like chlorine injection or UV disinfection. Physical removal of biofilm buildup is also necessary for lasting results.
