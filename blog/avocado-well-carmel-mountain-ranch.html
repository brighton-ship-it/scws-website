How a Private Well and Irrigation System Works

A residential well here is a complete system, not just a borehole. A submersible pump set deep in the casing lifts water up to a pressure tank at the surface, and a pressure switch tells the pump when to start and stop so your taps and sprinklers see steady pressure. From the tank, water branches to the house plumbing and to the irrigation manifold that feeds your drip lines, spray heads, and hose bibs. On larger landscaped lots we often add a storage tank so a modest well can recharge slowly overnight and then deliver a strong flow at dawn when an automatic controller fires every zone at once.

The ground beneath this part of northeastern San Diego is dominated by decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock. Wells in this terrain often produce from fractures in the bedrock rather than a deep sand-and-gravel aquifer, which is exactly why local knowledge matters: yield, depth, and water chemistry can change dramatically from one ridge or canyon to the next.

Common Local Scenarios We Get Called For

Because the CMR area is largely suburban, the well work we do tends to cluster around a few recognizable situations:

  • Irrigation wells losing pressure mid-summer, usually a tired pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch that leaves sprinklers sputtering.
  • Hard, mineral-rich water leaving scale on fixtures and clogging drip emitters.
  • Older domestic wells on legacy lots that have not been serviced in years and need a pump pull or flow test.
  • Sediment or cloudy water after a wet winter shifts fines in the fractured rock.
  • Decommissioning of an abandoned well on a parcel being redeveloped, which San Diego County requires be done to code.

What You Can Check Yourself Before Calling

A few quick observations help us help you faster, and occasionally save you a service call entirely:

  1. Listen to the pump. Short, rapid on-off cycling almost always points to a pressure tank that has lost its air charge or a failing pressure switch.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Healthy residential systems usually hold somewhere in the 40 to 60 psi band; a needle that never builds or swings wildly is a red flag.
  3. Check the breaker. A tripped breaker or a corroded electrical connection at the wellhead is a common and inexpensive culprit.
  4. Look at the water. Note any new color, smell, grit, or staining and when it started; that timeline narrows the diagnosis quickly.
  5. Walk your irrigation zones. If only some heads are weak, the problem may be clogging or scale rather than the well itself.

When to Call a Professional

Anything that involves pulling the pump, opening the wellhead, handling 240-volt wiring, or reading a water-quality lab report is work for a licensed contractor. If you have lost water entirely, smell rotten-egg sulfur, see a sudden change in clarity, or the pump runs constantly without building pressure, call us rather than guessing. A wrong move on a submersible can drop equipment down the casing and turn a modest repair into a major one. We offer same-day emergency service for no-water situations.

Water Quality and Treatment in the CMR Area

Inland San Diego County groundwater is typically hard, and depending on the specific well it can also carry iron, manganese, or a faint sulfur smell. None of these make a well unusable, but they do call for the right treatment. Hardness leaves scale on water heaters, fixtures, and irrigation emitters and shortens the life of appliances; a properly sized softener fixes it. Iron and manganese show up as rust-colored staining on sinks and concrete and as plugged drip lines, and a dedicated filtration system clears them. A rotten-egg odor usually points to sulfur or to bacteria in the well and is straightforward to treat once we identify the source. We start with a water test so the treatment matches what is actually in your water rather than guessing, which keeps you from paying for equipment you do not need.

Seasonal Maintenance Keeps Costs Down

The cheapest well repair is the one you prevent. We recommend a simple annual check of the pressure tank air charge and the pressure switch, a look at the wellhead seal and electrical connections, and periodic filter changes for any treatment equipment. On irrigation systems, walking the zones a couple of times each season to catch clogged emitters and broken heads protects both your landscape and your pump, since a system fighting blockages works harder and wears out sooner. For homes that depend entirely on a single well, we can also flow-test the well every few years to confirm it is still yielding what your household and landscape need before a dry summer puts it to the test. A modest maintenance habit almost always costs far less than an emergency pump replacement during a heat wave.

How deep are wells in this part of San Diego County?

Depths vary widely in fractured-granite terrain, often from a couple hundred feet to several hundred, depending on where the producing fractures sit. We evaluate each parcel individually rather than assuming a fixed depth.

Why does my well water leave white scale on everything?

That is hard water, high in dissolved minerals, which is typical of inland San Diego County. A softener or targeted treatment system solves it and protects your fixtures and irrigation emitters.

Can I use a well just for irrigation to lower my water bill?

Yes. An irrigation-only well paired with a storage tank and booster pump is a popular way to keep a large San Diego landscape green without paying municipal rates for every gallon.
