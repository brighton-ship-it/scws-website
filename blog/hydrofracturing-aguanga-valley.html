What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Aguanga Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping

  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Aguanga Valley Wells: Getting More From Fractured Granite

Out in the Aguanga Valley, where State Routes 79 and 371 cross between Temecula and Anza, water does not come from a municipal main. It comes up through casing from the decomposed granite and fractured granitic bedrock that underlies these high-desert ranches and ranchettes. At roughly 1,900 to 2,000 feet of elevation, with hot, dry summers and large private parcels spread across the hills, a household well here is not a convenience — it is the entire water supply for the home, the horses, the corrals, and in a growing number of cases the few rows of vines that edge into this corner of Temecula wine country. When that well starts giving you less than it used to, hydrofracturing (often spelled hydrofracking) is frequently the smartest, least expensive way to get the yield back.

Southern California Well Service has worked these granite formations since 2020, and our Anza office sits just up SR-371 from the valley. That proximity matters: the crew that shows up to assess your well already understands how Aguanga-area wells behave, where the productive fracture zones tend to sit, and why a well that "went dry" is very often not dry at all — just choked.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation method, not a drilling method. The principle is simple: nearly all of the water feeding a granite-country well travels through a network of natural cracks and seams in the rock. Over years of pumping, those fractures silt up, mineralize, and partially close. Hydrofracturing reopens and cleans them using nothing but clean water under pressure.

On site, we lower an inflatable packer — sometimes a pair of packers straddling a target zone — into the well below the casing and inflate it to seal off a section of the borehole. Clean water is then injected into that isolated section at high pressure, commonly in the 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI range depending on the depth and the rock. That pressure pushes into the existing fractures, flushing out the accumulated sediment and mineral scale and prying the seams slightly wider so groundwater can move more freely into the well. We work zone by zone down the productive interval. No chemicals are involved at any stage; the only thing going down the hole is water. A typical treatment runs about four to eight hours from setup to the final flow check.

Why Aguanga Valley Wells Lose Yield

If your well in the valley is producing less than it did a few years ago, the cause is usually one or more of the following, and most of them respond well to hydrofracturing:

  • Fracture silting and closure. The fine particles and minerals carried in groundwater gradually pack the feeder cracks. This is the single most common reason a granite well slowly declines, and it is exactly what hydrofracturing is built to reverse.
  • Dropping static water levels. Through the long dry summers and successive drought years across Riverside County, the regional water table can sit lower than it once did. A well that only intersects a few shallow fractures becomes far more sensitive to that drop. Opening additional fracture pathways deeper in the borehole can reconnect the well to water it could no longer reach.
  • A new well that simply underperformed. Plenty of recently drilled wells in fractured rock come in disappointing — the drill bit happened to miss the best seams. Hydrofracturing can extend the reach of the borehole into nearby fractures it never quite tapped.

The common thread is that the water is usually still down there. The bottleneck is the path it has to travel through the granite, and that path is something we can improve.

A Note for Ranchette, Equestrian, and Small-Vineyard Owners

The Aguanga Valley is full of properties that ask more of a single well than a typical suburban lot ever would. A horse property might run domestic water to the house, fill troughs and an automatic waterer or two, wash down a barn, and keep an arena from turning to dust. A small vineyard layered onto a residential parcel adds a modest but steady irrigation demand right through the hottest part of the year. None of these are huge agricultural draws, but together they push a marginal well past its comfortable sustained yield, and that is when the pump starts short-cycling and the pressure tank starts running you out of water during the afternoon.

Hydrofracturing is well suited to this exact situation. By raising the well's sustained yield rather than just its momentary flow, it gives a ranchette or equestrian property the headroom to cover both domestic use and modest animal or irrigation water from the same well — often without the cost and permitting of a second well. For owners balancing the house, the barn, and a few rows of vines on one fractured-rock well, a successful treatment can be the difference between rationing water in August and not thinking about it at all.

What to Check and What to Expect

Hydrofracturing is not the right answer for every well, so the process starts with finding out whether yours is a good candidate. Here is how a typical job unfolds:

  1. Pre-treatment flow test. We measure your current yield and watch how quickly the water level recovers after pumping. This is the baseline we will measure success against.
  2. Downhole video inspection. A camera survey shows us the casing condition, the depth of the productive zones, and where the fractures sit. It also rules out problems hydrofracturing cannot fix, such as a collapsed casing or a failing pump.
  3. The treatment. Packers isolate the target zones and we inject clean water at pressure, working the productive interval over roughly four to eight hours.
  4. Post-treatment flow test. Once the well settles, we re-test the yield and recovery, usually within a day or two, and give you a documented before-and-after comparison.

Good candidates versus poor ones. Wells completed in hard, fractured granite — which describes the great majority of Aguanga Valley wells — respond best, because the whole technique depends on having natural fractures to reopen. Wells producing from loose sand or gravel formations are poor candidates: there are no rock fractures to widen, and the high pressure can simply move sand around without improving sustained yield. The video inspection and flow test tell us which situation you are in before any pressure goes down the hole, so you are not paying to find out the hard way.

When to Call a Professional

It is worth getting a well evaluated when you notice the warning signs: water production that has been trending down year over year, a well that runs low or sputters during peak afternoon use, slow recovery after the pump shuts off, a pump that cycles on and off more than it used to, or household pressure that is simply weaker than it was a few seasons ago. Any of these can mean the feeder fractures are closing up. Because high-pressure work inside a borehole requires the right packers, pumps, and experience to do safely, hydrofracturing is firmly a licensed-contractor job — not something to attempt with rented equipment.

Realistic Costs and the New-Well Comparison

For most Aguanga Valley properties, hydrofracturing runs about $3,000 to $8,000 depending on well depth and the number of zones treated. Compare that to drilling an entirely new well in this terrain, which typically lands somewhere between $18,000 and $42,000 once you account for depth, casing, and development. If the existing well also needs a new submersible pump, that is generally another $2,500 to $5,500. Even in the cases where hydrofracturing only partly succeeds, it is almost always worth trying first, because the cost is a fraction of starting over — and a great many wells that look like they are "going dry" are fully recoverable.
