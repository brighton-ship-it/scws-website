What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Banning Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

In Banning, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Banning wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in Banning and the San Gorgonio Pass

Banning sits in the San Gorgonio Pass of Riverside County, around 2,350 feet, in the dramatic gap between the San Bernardino Mountains to the north and the San Jacinto Mountains to the south, with Cabazon and the Morongo reservation just to the east. Properties on the pass floor, including parts of the Banning Bench and the rural parcels climbing toward the mountains, often rely on private wells. The pass is a mixed groundwater setting: the broad valley floor holds deep alluvial deposits, while the mountain-front benches and rising ground tap fractured bedrock. That mix matters for hydrofracturing, which helps wells finished in fractured rock and offers little to wells set in loose alluvium. On any Banning property, the honest starting point is to determine which kind of well you have before discussing treatment.

Pass-Floor Wells Versus Mountain-Front Wells

A well drilled into the alluvial fill of the pass floor draws from the pore spaces in sand and gravel; that formation already moves water freely, so there is little to fracture and the loose material can slough back. Such wells are generally not candidates. A well drilled into the granitic bedrock of the mountain front, on the higher benches toward the San Bernardino or San Jacinto ranges, is a strong potential candidate. There, water travels only through fractures, and high-pressure water can widen and connect those cracks to increase yield substantially. If your Banning well is several hundred feet into rock on the bench or hillside, it may benefit greatly; if it is a valley-floor well in deep alluvium, it probably will not, and we will say so.

The pass is also famously windy and dry, and demand peaks in the hot months when the fracture system is at its lowest after winter recharge. A mountain-front well that supports a home and some landscaping in spring can fall behind by late summer. Improving the well's sustainable flow through fracturing gives those properties headroom during the hardest part of the year.

Signs Worth a Closer Look

  • Declining yield over several years from a well you know is in rock
  • Slow recovery after heavy household or irrigation use
  • Seasonal shortages that deepen in late summer and ease after winter
  • A newer bedrock well that never produced the expected flow
  • A pump that short-cycles or draws the water level down to the intake

We always confirm the formation is the real limit and rule out equipment first. A worn pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch can mimic low yield, and these inexpensive fixes are worth eliminating before any major work.

How the Process Works on a Qualifying Banning Well

We begin with a baseline flow test to document current yield, then run a camera survey to check casing condition and locate productive fracture zones. An inflatable packer isolates a section of the open borehole, and we inject clean water at pressures typically between 1,000 and 3,000 PSI to clean and extend the fractures. We often treat several zones, then develop the well to flush loosened sediment and run a post-treatment flow test to confirm the gain. The job usually takes a single day, and we use only clean water with no chemicals, so your drinking water stays safe.

What to Check Before You Call

A bit of history makes your assessment faster and more accurate. Try to learn whether your well is in bedrock or in pass-floor alluvium, since that largely determines whether fracturing applies. Note whether your shortage is seasonal, which points to a yield limit rather than equipment. Watch the pressure gauge for rapid cycling, a sign of a waterlogged tank, and listen for a pump that runs long then loses prime, a classic low-yield symptom. Sharing your well's depth and any log lets us give you a quick, well-grounded answer.

Realistic Results and Cost

Where a Banning well is genuinely set in fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, typically two to five times the prior flow. A new well in the pass area runs about $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, while fracturing a suitable existing well usually costs $3,000 to $8,000, with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the job. For a bedrock well that has lost yield, fracturing is the sensible first step before five-figure drilling. Every result is documented with before-and-after flow testing.

When to Call a Professional

Reach out when a known bedrock well has lost production, when recovery is slow, or before you commit to a replacement well. If your real issue is pressure rather than supply, a booster pump or constant-pressure system may be the better fix. We would rather tell you honestly that your Banning well is a poor fracturing candidate than take on work that can't help, because straight answers are what build trust in the pass communities.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Banning well is in rock or alluvium?

Your well log usually states the formation, and the well's depth, location, and a camera survey tell us the rest. Wells on the mountain-front benches and higher ground are often in fractured rock, while pass-floor wells tend to be in deep alluvium. We confirm before recommending treatment.

Why won't fracturing help a pass-floor alluvial well?

High-pressure water opens and connects cracks in solid rock. Loose sand and gravel has no fracture network to widen, and the material can collapse back, so the benefit is minimal. We assess the formation first and decline jobs where fracturing can't reasonably succeed.

Is the process safe for my water?

Yes. It uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals or additives, so it does not contaminate groundwater. It simply reopens the natural fractures already feeding a bedrock well.

How long will my well be offline?

A qualifying Banning well is usually treated in a single day, four to eight hours depending on the number of zones. We reinstall the pump and run a post-treatment flow test before leaving, and water is typically available again the same evening.

What does the assessment cost?

Our diagnostic is $125, credited toward the work if you proceed. It tells you whether your Banning well is a candidate and rules out cheaper equipment fixes, so you never pay for treatment you don't need.

If my well isn't a candidate, what are my options?

For a pass-floor well at its limit, options include added storage to bridge peak demand, deepening the well, or a new one. We explain the costs and trade-offs of each so you can decide with full information.
