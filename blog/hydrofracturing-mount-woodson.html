What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mount Woodson Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

How Hydrofracturing Rehabilitates a Well

Think of hydrofracturing as a deep cleaning and reopening of the natural plumbing around your well. We seal off a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer, then inject clean water into that isolated zone at roughly 1,500 to 3,000+ PSI. The pressure drives into the existing fractures in the granodiorite, flushing out silt and mineral scale and extending the cracks so your well taps into more water-bearing fractures. The on-site work takes about 4 to 8 hours, uses no chemicals, and on deeper wells may involve treating multiple zones.

The Granodiorite Advantage

If you have hiked Mount Woodson, you have seen the granite up close. Beneath the surface, that same rock is exactly what makes hydrofracturing effective here. Groundwater in the Woodson Mountain granodiorite travels through joints and fractures rather than through a sandy aquifer, so a well's yield depends on how many productive fractures it intersects. Over time, those fractures fill with fine sediment and mineral deposits, and the well slowly chokes down. Hydrofracturing cleans them out and opens new connections, restoring flow without drilling.

This is why fractured-rock wells make such good candidates and why wells in loose sand or gravel do not: hard granite gives the pressurized water rigid fractures to clean and extend. The Mount Woodson area is squarely in granite country, which is a big part of why the technique works so reliably here.

First, Confirm It Is the Well

Before recommending hydrofracturing, we make sure the well itself is the bottleneck. Low pressure and "running out" are sometimes caused by a failing submersible pump, a waterlogged pressure tank, a faulty pressure switch, or a leak on the line, all of which are cheaper to fix than a well treatment. A flow test measures real production and recovery and removes the guesswork. The diagnostic is $125 and credited toward the job if you go ahead, so you are not paying for treatment you do not need.

Decomposed Granite at the Surface, Fractured Rock Below

One thing that trips up Mount Woodson property owners is the difference between the soft, crumbly decomposed granite you see at the surface and the hard, fractured granite your well actually draws from at depth. The shallow decomposed material does not hold or move much usable water; the productive water lives in the joints and fractures of the solid rock farther down. When we evaluate a well here, we are looking at how well the deeper borehole connects to that fracture network, because that is what hydrofracturing improves. It is also why two neighbors on the same ridge can have very different wells: a few feet of difference in where the borehole intersects a major fracture can change everything.

Access on these boulder-strewn properties is usually straightforward for our equipment, but the rugged terrain around Woodson Mountain is precisely why drilling a brand-new well can get expensive and complicated. Rehabilitating the well you already have, on a pad and access road that already exist, avoids most of that cost and disruption. For a property owner weighing options, that is a meaningful advantage of trying hydrofracturing first.

We also see a fair number of wells in this corridor that were drilled decades ago, when the original yield was strong, and have simply lost capacity to gradual fracture plugging. Those long-established granite wells are often the best responders to a hydrofracturing treatment, because the fracture network is real and extensive and just needs to be reopened.

A Word on Water Quality

Granite wells around Mount Woodson sometimes carry elevated minerals or fine sediment, and homeowners occasionally assume that cloudiness or sediment means the well is failing. More often it points to fractures shedding fine material or to a pump set too low in the borehole. A flow test and a look at the water tell us whether the issue is yield, sediment, or simple pump placement, so you address the actual problem. If sediment is the concern, straightforward filtration in the $300 to $900 range usually resolves it, separately from any yield work.
