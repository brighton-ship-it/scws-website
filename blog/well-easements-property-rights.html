Types of Well Easements

Access easements allow travel across property to reach a well. Utility easements permit running pipes or electrical lines. Maintenance easements grant rights to service equipment. Water rights easements provide actual rights to draw water. Some easements are appurtenant (attached to a specific property) while others are in gross (personal rights that may not transfer). Understanding your easement type determines your rights and limitations.

Creating and Recording Easements

Easements should be in writing, clearly describe the affected property, specify permitted uses, and be recorded with the county. Include details about: width and location of access corridors, maintenance responsibilities, cost sharing if applicable, and any restrictions on use. Unrecorded easements can still be valid but create title issues and lending problems.

Protecting Your Interests

If you own the well: limit easement scope to necessary uses, require notice before access, mandate restoration after work, and retain control over water allocation. If you hold an easement: ensure it's recorded, includes maintenance rights, survives property sales, and provides adequate access. Both parties benefit from clear, detailed documentation.

Easement Issues When Buying Property

Before purchasing, thoroughly review title reports for existing easements. Understand who has access to your property and for what purposes. For properties with wells serving other parcels, verify the scope of others' rights. Check that access roads are legally established, not just informally used. Consider how easements might affect future property development or improvements.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a well easement?

A well easement is a legal right allowing someone to access, use, or maintain a well located on another person's property. Easements can grant rights to the well water itself, access roads to reach the well, or utility corridors for pipes and electrical lines. They're typically recorded with the property deed and transfer with ownership.

Can I deny access to a well on my property?

If a valid recorded easement exists, you cannot deny access for legitimate purposes covered by the easement. However, easement holders must use reasonable access and minimize disruption. You can establish rules about notice before entry, approved access routes, and restoration after work. Unreasonable denial can result in court-ordered access.

How do I find out if there are easements on my property?

Order a preliminary title report from a title company—it lists all recorded easements. Also check your deed and any referenced documents. Some older easements may be unrecorded but still valid through prescriptive use (continuous use for 5+ years). A property survey can reveal physical evidence of easement use like roads or utility lines.
