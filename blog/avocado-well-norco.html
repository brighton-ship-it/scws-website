Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in Riverside County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Norco well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well Water Service for Norco Horse Properties and Rural Homes

Norco wears its nickname proudly: "Horsetown USA." Tucked into the northwestern corner of Riverside County between Corona, Eastvale, and the Santa Ana River, this is a community built around large lots, corrals, barns, and more than a hundred miles of riding trails. City ordinances even require new construction to keep that rustic, Western character. What that lifestyle quietly depends on, more than almost anywhere else in the Inland Empire, is dependable water. A horse property without reliable water is not just an inconvenience, it is an animal-welfare problem.

Let us be straight about one thing: Norco is not an avocado-growing district. The famous Southern California avocado groves are down in Fallbrook, Pauma Valley, and the San Diego County backcountry, not on the Riverside County valley floor. Many Norco parcels do keep backyard citrus, gardens, and a few fruit trees, but the water that matters most here flows to livestock troughs, wash racks, arena dust control, pasture, and the household. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years keeping rural and equestrian water systems running across this region, and we approach a Norco property for what it actually is: a working ranch, not a commercial orchard.

How a Norco Well and Pressure System Works

Most private wells in and around Norco draw from the alluvial groundwater associated with the Santa Ana River, Temescal Wash, and the broader Chino and Temescal basins. These are sand-and-gravel aquifers rather than the fractured granite of the high backcountry, so yields are often reasonable, but the water tends to be hard and mineral-rich. A typical setup looks like this: a submersible pump set a few hundred feet down lifts water to the surface, a pressure tank stores it and smooths out demand, and a pressure switch tells the pump when to turn on and off. From the tank, lines branch out to the house, the hose bibs, the barn, the troughs, and any irrigation.

On a horse property, that system gets worked hard. Filling several stock tanks, running a wash rack, knocking down arena dust, and supplying the house can all happen at once. Wells in the Norco area commonly run somewhere between roughly 150 and 400 feet depending on the parcel and how the water table has moved through drought cycles. Sizing the pump and tank to the property's real peak demand is the single biggest factor in whether the system feels effortless or frustrating.

Common Water Problems on Norco Properties

Across our years serving Riverside County, the issues we get called out for in Norco follow a familiar pattern:

  • Hard-water scale building up in pipes, valves, automatic waterers, and pump components
  • Iron and manganese staining that leaves orange and brown marks on troughs, concrete, and stucco
  • Pressure tank failure that makes the pump short-cycle, which burns out motors early
  • Worn pressure switches that cause erratic pressure or no water at all
  • Sediment and sand that clog automatic waterers and irrigation emitters
  • Declining yield during long dry stretches, especially on older or shallower wells
  • Electrical faults from power fluctuations and aging wiring on rural services

What You Can Check Before You Call

A few quick observations help you describe the problem and sometimes solve it outright:

  • Look at the pressure gauge on your tank. A healthy system usually cycles between about 40 and 60 psi. If the pump kicks on and off every few seconds, suspect a waterlogged tank or a failing switch.
  • Rap your knuckles on the pressure tank. If most of it sounds solid and full of water rather than hollow up top, it has likely lost its air charge.
  • Determine whether low pressure hits the whole property or just one barn hydrant or faucet. Whole-property issues point to the well, pump, or tank; a single slow spigot is usually local plumbing.
  • Watch for new staining, a sulfur smell, or grit in the water. Sudden sediment can mean a failing well screen or a dropping water table.
  • Check your breaker and any pump control box for tripped or scorched components before assuming the pump itself failed.

When to Call a Professional

Swapping a pressure switch is sometimes a confident homeowner's job. Almost everything else on a well is not. Pulling a submersible pump means handling heavy equipment and hundreds of feet of pipe and wire, and a dropped pump can ruin a well. Diagnosing falling yield takes an understanding of the local aquifer and drawdown behavior. Electrical work at the wellhead carries real shock and fire risk. And when livestock depend on the water, a guess that leaves animals dry overnight is not worth the gamble. If you have no water, treat it as the emergency it is. We offer same-day emergency response throughout the Norco area.

Why Equestrian Properties Choose a Constant-Pressure Setup

One upgrade we recommend often in Norco is a constant-pressure or booster system. On a traditional on-off pump, pressure sags noticeably the moment several demands run together, which is exactly what happens when you are filling troughs while someone hoses down a horse and the house dishwasher kicks on. A variable-speed constant-pressure system ramps the pump to match demand, holding steady pressure to every hydrant and barn line. For sprawling properties with the well at one corner and barns at the other, it can be the difference between constant complaints and a system you never think about.

We also help owners add storage. A storage tank lets the well refill slowly overnight and then deliver a big burst during morning chores, which is gentler on the pump and ideal for properties whose well recovers modestly. Pairing storage with a booster pump is a proven combination for working ranches.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really avocado groves in Norco?

Not commercially. Norco is an equestrian and rural-residential community in Riverside County, not an avocado district. Some homeowners keep backyard citrus and a few fruit trees, but most well water here serves livestock, barns, arenas, and households. We service the wells that support all of those needs.

How deep are wells in Norco?

Wells in the Norco area are typically drilled into sand-and-gravel aquifers and often range from about 150 to 400 feet, depending on the parcel and the current water table. We can review your well log or test your existing well to advise you.

Why does my well water stain my troughs and concrete orange?

That is usually iron, sometimes with manganese, dissolved in the groundwater. It is cosmetic but persistent. An iron or manganese filtration system removes it and protects waterers, fixtures, and surfaces.

My pump runs constantly when I fill stock tanks. What is wrong?

Often the system simply is not sized for the property's peak demand, or the pressure tank has failed. Adding storage, upgrading to a constant-pressure system, or replacing the tank usually solves it. We will diagnose the actual cause before recommending anything.
