Well Pump Won't Start in Bonita?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Bonita homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

When the Well Goes Quiet in Bonita

Bonita is a leafy, semi-rural pocket of San Diego County wrapped around the Sweetwater River valley, just inland from Chula Vista and the South Bay. It is horse country by reputation, full of older homes on generous lots, and a good number of those properties still draw irrigation and household water from private wells rather than the municipal system. When a Bonita pump won't start, it tends to happen at the worst time — a hot weekend, a full pasture, a yard that needs watering — and the first reaction is usually a mix of frustration and worry about a giant repair bill.

Here is the encouraging truth: the majority of no-start calls we run in Bonita turn out to be controls or electrical issues, not a dead pump down the hole. Local wells generally draw from Sweetwater River valley alluvium and the fractured granite beneath it, with domestic pumps commonly set between 150 and 380 feet. Below, we explain how the pieces fit together, what fails most often here, the checks you can safely make, and when to bring in a licensed contractor.

How the System Is Supposed to Behave

Every time you draw water, an invisible relay race happens beneath the surface. When one runner drops the baton, you get nothing at the tap:

  • 240-volt power. The pump is fed by a dedicated breaker and usually an outdoor disconnect near the wellhead. Loose lugs, corroded contacts, or a tripped breaker stop everything cold.
  • Pressure switch. The brain of the everyday cycle, this switch senses falling pressure and calls the pump on. It is the part that fails most often across every well we service.
  • Capacitor or control box. Submersible motors need a start capacitor to spin up — surface-mounted for three-wire pumps, internal for two-wire. A weak capacitor produces a hum and no water.
  • Pressure tank. A healthy bladder tank keeps the pump from cycling on every small demand. A failed bladder causes rapid short-cycling and premature wear.
  • Motor, drop pipe, and aquifer. The motor lifts water up hundreds of feet of pipe and wire. A wiring fault on that run, or a water level that drops below the pump, will both silence the system.

The Failures We See Most in Bonita

Local conditions shape what breaks. In Bonita, these stand out:

  • Hard Sweetwater-valley water. The groundwater here is mineral-heavy. Scale collects on pressure-switch contacts and inside check valves and gradually wears pump internals.
  • Long irrigation runs. Pasture, gardens, and equestrian use mean Bonita pumps log heavy hours, and a hard-working pump exposes a tired switch or capacitor sooner.
  • Legacy equipment. Plenty of Bonita wells date back decades. Original switches, tanks, and control boxes simply age out.
  • Outdoor electrical wear. Disconnect boxes baking in the sun and visited by rodents develop loose, corroded, or chewed connections.
  • Seasonal water-level dips. In dry years the valley table can fall, and a shallower pump may lose prime and trip on protection.

What You Can Check Without Tools

A short, safe inspection often points right at the culprit:

  1. Breaker. Find the double-pole 240V breaker for the well, reset it once if tripped, and stop if it trips again.
  2. Wellhead disconnect. Confirm any outdoor switch is on and the box is dry and clear of nests.
  3. Pressure gauge. Zero with the breaker on means no delivery; tap a stuck needle gently.
  4. Switch sound. Listen for a click at the pressure switch when you open a tap.
  5. Whole-house power. Make sure the issue isn't a broader outage affecting other 240V loads.

Leave the control box and wiring alone. These run on 240 volts and need a meter, not a screwdriver and a hunch.

A Closer Look at Why "Humming but No Water" Happens

One of the most common — and most misunderstood — symptoms we hear in Bonita is a pump that hums but produces no water. Homeowners often assume the motor has burned out and brace for a full pump replacement. In reality, that hum is frequently the sound of a healthy motor that simply can't start spinning because its capacitor has lost the punch needed to break the motor free. A submersible motor draws a heavy surge at startup, and the capacitor stores and releases that energy. When it weakens with age or heat, the motor stalls and hums. Replacing the capacitor or control box restores water at a fraction of the cost of a new pump.

Other times, the hum points to a locked pump fouled by sediment or scale, or to a wiring fault that is starving the motor of one leg of its 240-volt supply. The only way to tell these apart is to measure voltage, resistance, and insulation with the right instruments. That is why we never condemn a pump on symptoms alone — a few minutes of testing can be the difference between a small invoice and a large one.

The Role of the Pressure Tank in a No-Start Call

It surprises many Bonita homeowners to learn that a failing pressure tank can masquerade as a pump that won't start. The tank's job is to hold a cushion of pressurized water and air so the pump can rest between draws. Inside most modern tanks is a rubber bladder separating the water from a charge of compressed air. When that bladder ruptures or the air charge bleeds off, the tank becomes "waterlogged," and the pump is forced to switch on and off rapidly every time you open a faucet. That relentless short-cycling burns out pressure switches and pump motors years before their time, and the visible symptom is often a pump that finally refuses to start at all.

If you notice the pump kicking on and off every few seconds, hear it cycling far more than it used to, or see the pressure gauge needle bouncing rapidly, the tank is a prime suspect. Catching a waterlogged tank early — and replacing it before it takes the switch and motor down with it — is one of the easiest ways to avoid a much larger Bonita repair bill. During any diagnosis, we check the tank's air precharge and bladder integrity as a matter of routine, because fixing the symptom without addressing the tank just sets up the next failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

My pump hums but no water comes out. Do I need a new pump?

Usually not. A hum with no flow most often means a failed start capacitor or control box, both far cheaper than a pump. We test before we replace.

Why do Bonita wells seem hard on pressure switches?

The hard Sweetwater-valley water leaves scale that fouls switch contacts and the sensing port. Replacing the switch and adding treatment helps the whole system last longer.

Can heavy irrigation shorten my pump's life?

Yes. Long pasture and garden cycles run the pump hard, so capacitors and switches wear sooner on irrigation-heavy Bonita lots. Periodic checkups catch trouble early.

The pump worked all spring and quit in August. Why?

Late-summer water-level drops can leave a shallower pump drawing air. We measure the level and can reset the pump deeper or recommend yield improvement.
