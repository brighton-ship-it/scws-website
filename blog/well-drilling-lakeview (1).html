Lakeview is an agricultural community in the heart of the San Jacinto Valley in Riverside County, spread across the flatlands between Perris, Nuevo, and the Lakeview Mountains, with the San Jacinto River drainage running nearby. It is farming country — dairies, hay and grain fields, poultry operations, and rural homesteads that have depended on groundwater for generations. For property owners here, a private well is not a luxury; it is the backbone of the operation. When irrigation, livestock, and household use all draw from the same supply, having a well that produces reliably and consistently is everything.

That is why drilling a new well in Lakeview deserves a contractor who understands the specific ground beneath the San Jacinto Valley — not a one-size-fits-all crew. The valley's water-bearing sediments behave differently from the granite country up in the ranges, and getting the depth, casing, screen placement, and pump sizing right is the difference between a well that carries a farm through a dry summer and one that runs short when demand peaks.

The San Jacinto Basin Under Lakeview

Lakeview sits over the San Jacinto Groundwater Basin, a sediment-filled structural valley (a graben) formed by movement along the San Jacinto Fault Zone. Rather than solid bedrock near the surface, the ground here is a deep fill of younger and older alluvium — sand, gravel, silt, and clay deposited by the San Jacinto River and washed down from the surrounding mountains. In the coarser, more permeable zones this alluvium yields water well, which is precisely why the valley became such productive farmland.

Because the basin is sediment-filled and deep, well depths in Lakeview vary widely with location. Wells near active drainages sometimes tap shallow alluvial water, while others must reach several hundred feet into older, deeper alluvial layers to find a dependable, higher-quality aquifer. Agricultural wells serving heavy irrigation demand are frequently drilled deeper and cased larger than a simple domestic well. Groundwater in the basin has historically been suitable for domestic and irrigation use, though quality and mineral content shift across the valley — another reason a site-specific assessment matters. We review California DWR well completion records and neighboring logs before recommending a depth.

Step by Step: Drilling a New Well

Every new well we drill in Lakeview follows the same disciplined sequence, whether it serves a single home or a working farm.

Siting and Site Assessment

We evaluate your parcel, the local water table, and setback requirements from septic systems, corrals, chemical storage, and property lines. On agricultural land especially, keeping the wellhead safely separated from potential contamination sources is critical to protecting water quality for the long term.

Permitting

Riverside County well permits are issued by the Department of Environmental Health, with a typical turnaround of two to three weeks. We prepare and submit the application, coordinate required inspections, and file the well completion report — handling the full permit process for Lakeview property owners.

Drilling

Our Gefco rotary rig can reach 1,000+ feet. In Lakeview's alluvial valley fill, the rig advances through layered sand and gravel, using drilling fluid to lift cuttings and hold the borehole open. Because the ground is sediment rather than hard rock, drilling is often efficient, though clay layers and variable formations call for an experienced operator. Most wells reach target depth in one to three days.

Casing, Gravel Pack and Development

We set casing — PVC or steel based on depth and use — and place a gravel pack around the screened interval. The gravel pack is especially important in fine alluvial sediments: it filters silt and sand out of the incoming water so your pump and household appliances aren't chewed up by grit. We then develop the well, surging and pumping it to clear fines and confirm sustained yield, and seal the top with sanitary grout.

Pump Installation

Finally we install a correctly sized submersible pump, pressure tank, pressure switch, and control components, and connect the system to your plumbing or irrigation lines. For farms with high, steady demand, a constant-pressure or booster system keeps flow even during peak irrigation. Start to finish, a turnkey well typically takes three to five business days.

Common Lakeview Scenarios

In an agricultural community, the reasons for a new well tend to be practical. A grower whose old well can no longer supply enough water for expanded acreage needs a deeper, higher-capacity replacement. A family building on a rural San Jacinto Valley lot needs a first domestic well before the home can be occupied. And established well owners often need water treatment for the mineral content common in valley groundwater — sediment filtration ($300–$900) for silty alluvial water, a water softener ($1,500–$3,500) for hardness, UV disinfection ($800–$1,800), or a reverse-osmosis system ($300–$1,200) for drinking water. We size every solution to the actual well and its water chemistry.

What to Watch For & When to Call

Sand or grit in your water, a drop in pressure or flow during irrigation season, a pump cycling on and off rapidly, or a spike in your power bill all point to a well or pump that needs attention. In a valley where a single well can carry an entire operation, these are not problems to ignore. New drilling, below-water pump work, and control wiring should always be done by a licensed C-57 contractor to stay safe and compliant. If your well goes dry mid-season, our same-day emergency service gets Lakeview back in water fast.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep do wells need to be in Lakeview?

Depth varies across the San Jacinto Valley. Some Lakeview wells tap shallow alluvial water, while deeper wells reach several hundred feet into older alluvium for a more dependable supply. Agricultural wells are often drilled deeper. We set the target after a site assessment and review of nearby well logs.

How much does a new well cost in Lakeview?

A turnkey well typically runs $18,000 to $42,000, with higher-capacity agricultural wells toward the upper end. The final figure depends on depth, casing, and pump size. We provide a free written estimate before any work begins.

Do I need a permit for a well in Lakeview?

Yes. Riverside County requires a well permit through the Department of Environmental Health, usually issued in two to three weeks. We manage the entire permit application, inspections, and completion report for you.

Can you drill an agricultural well for irrigation?

Absolutely. Lakeview is farm country, and we regularly drill larger-diameter, higher-capacity wells for irrigation and livestock, often paired with a constant-pressure or booster system to hold flow steady during peak demand.

Why is a gravel pack important in Lakeview?

The San Jacinto basin is filled with fine alluvial sediment, so a properly placed gravel pack around the well screen filters out silt and sand. That protects your pump and appliances and keeps grit out of your water.

How long does drilling take?

Most Lakeview wells are drilled in one to three days. The complete turnkey process — casing, gravel pack, development, pump installation, and plumbing tie-in — generally takes three to five business days.
