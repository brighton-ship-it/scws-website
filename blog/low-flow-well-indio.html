Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many Riverside County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Yield Wells in Indio and the Eastern Coachella Valley

Indio is the largest city in the Coachella Valley, in Riverside County, anchoring the eastern end of the valley over the Indio Subbasin of the Coachella Valley Groundwater Basin. This is deep desert alluvial country, with an aquifer estimated to be several thousand feet deep and capable of storing roughly 30 million acre-feet of water in just the upper 1,000 feet. The water-bearing materials are gravel, sand, silt, and clay carried down from the surrounding mountains over geologic time. With only about six inches of rain a year, natural recharge is minimal, and the valley has a long agricultural heritage built on groundwater pumping. When a private well in the Indio area runs as a low-flow or low-yield well, the cause is usually the depth and zone the well draws from, plus a water table that responds to the long-term balance of pumping and replenishment, rather than any shortage of water in the basin overall.

Low yield in the desert is solvable, but the right fix depends on understanding this specific aquifer. Here is how we evaluate and improve low-flow wells in and around Indio.

How to Test Your Well's Flow and Yield

Two numbers matter: the flow at your fixtures and the sustained yield of the aquifer. For a quick check, run an outdoor hydrant into a five-gallon bucket and time the fill. Twenty seconds is 15 GPM; one minute is 5 GPM; two minutes or more puts you at 2.5 GPM or less.

The decisive measurement is a sustained yield (drawdown) test. Because a pressure tank stores water and can briefly mask a weak well, we pump steadily, watch the water level fall, and time the recovery. An Indio-area home generally needs 3 to 5 GPM of dependable supply, much more for desert landscaping, date gardens, or agricultural irrigation. Wells screened in the basin's saturated sand-and-gravel zones can be strong producers, while wells perforated mainly across clay and silt, or wells that have not kept pace with a falling water table, can fall well short.

Keep in mind that low yield and low pressure are different problems that feel the same at the tap. Pressure is how forcefully water moves through your pipes; yield is how much the well can supply over time. A home can show fine pressure for a few minutes and then run short when the well cannot sustain the draw. Measuring sustained yield, not a quick burst, tells you which one you face.

Why Indio-Area Wells Run Low

  • A declining water table. In an arid basin with minimal natural recharge and a long history of agricultural pumping, sustained withdrawals can lower static levels over time. An older, shallower well may end up with too little saturated screen.
  • Screened in fine material. The basin's Pleistocene-age alluvium holds large amounts of clay and silt. A well perforated across fine layers releases water slowly even with productive sand and gravel nearby.
  • Clogged or encrusted screen. Desert groundwater is often mineral-rich, and calcium, iron, and manganese scale gradually seals screen slots and gravel pack.
  • Biofouling. Iron and sulfur bacteria form slimes that plug the intake, sometimes with staining or odor.
  • Pump set too high or sized wrong. If the water table has fallen below the pump's effective range, the well loses prime; an oversized pump pulls the level down faster than the formation refills, causing short cycling.

Solutions and the Tradeoffs

Well Rehabilitation

For an older well that has slowly lost output, cleaning the screen and casing with brushing, surging, and chemical treatment can restore much of the original yield by clearing scale and bacteria. At roughly $1,500 to $5,000, it is the logical first step when a once-strong well has faded. It restores lost capacity but cannot exceed what the aquifer supplies at the well's depth.

Deepening or Lowering the Pump

In a basin where the water table has dropped, deepening the well, or at minimum lowering and resetting the pump to follow the water level, is often the most direct fix. Reaching deeper into the saturated sand-and-gravel zones can recover strong, dependable yield. Because the Indio Subbasin is thick and productive at depth, this is frequently worthwhile; we confirm with nearby well logs first.

Hydrofracturing

Hydrofracturing is a fractured-bedrock technique and is generally not appropriate on Indio's deep alluvial valley floor. We mention it only for completeness; here, depth, pump placement, and rehabilitation matter far more. Where it does apply on bedrock margins, it runs about $3,000 to $8,000.

Storage Cistern With a Booster or Constant-Pressure Pump

For chronic low yield, this is the most dependable buffer. A slow well fills a large cistern, commonly 2,500 to 5,000 gallons or larger on agricultural parcels, around the clock, and a booster or constant-pressure pump delivers strong, even pressure on demand. A 2 GPM well running continuously produces nearly 2,900 gallons a day, comfortably covering household use, with the tank absorbing the heavy peaks that desert irrigation and landscaping create. Plan on roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed, more for large agricultural tanks. The tradeoff is footprint and a bit more equipment, but it buffers a low-yield desert well against both daily peaks and a slowly falling water table.

Right-Sizing the Pump

Sometimes the cure is matching the pump to the well and the current water level, or adding a flow-control valve so it cannot outrun the aquifer. A properly sized pump and pressure tank cost $2,500 to $5,500.

A drop in flow, a short-cycling pump, air sputtering at the taps, or sand and cloudiness in the water all mean it is time for a professional yield evaluation. Frequently Asked Questions

If the Indio Subbasin holds so much water, why is my well low?

The basin is vast, but your well only draws from the zone and depth it is screened in. A declining water table or a well perforated across clay can yield little even with abundant groundwater nearby. Deepening or resetting the pump often recovers yield.

My Indio well used to be strong and now runs low. What changed?

In an arid, heavily pumped basin, the water table can fall over time, leaving an older, shallower well with too little saturated screen. Lowering the pump or deepening the well to follow the water level is often the fix.

Is hydrofracturing useful for an Indio well?

Usually not. Hydrofracturing works in fractured bedrock, while Indio sits on a deep alluvial basin. Here, deepening, lowering the pump, or rehabilitation typically delivers far better results.

How do I know if it is the well or the pump?

A sustained yield test tells you. If the well draws down fast and recovers slowly, the aquifer or water level is the limit; if the level holds but pressure is weak, the pump or tank is to blame. Our $125 diagnostic measures both.

What does it cost to improve a low-yield desert well?

Rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000, a storage-plus-booster system $3,000 to $7,000 (more for large agricultural tanks), and a right-sized pump and tank $2,500 to $5,500. Deepening is quoted by depth, and the diagnostic guides the best path.
