Well Water Discoloration: What Different Colors Mean

Updated February 2026 | By Southern California Well Service

Quick Answer: Brown = iron or sediment. Yellow = tannins or iron bacteria. Red/orange = iron. Black = manganese. Blue/green = copper (rare). Most discoloration is aesthetic, not harmful—but test to confirm. Sudden changes need investigation.

Brown/Rust Colored Water

Most Common Cause: Iron

  • Dissolved iron oxidizes to rust color
  • May be clear from tap, turns brown in glass
  • Stains fixtures and laundry
  • Common in many well systems

Other Causes

Sediment

  • Disturbed well bottom
  • After pump work
  • Heavy usage period
  • Usually clears up

Rusty Pipes

  • Corroding galvanized pipes
  • Worse after not running
  • Clears after running
  • Solution: Repipe with PEX or copper

Well Casing Corrosion

  • Old steel casing rusting
  • May need casing liner
  • More common in older wells

Treatment Options

  • Iron filter: Removes iron before it oxidizes
  • Water softener: Removes low iron (under 3 ppm)
  • Sediment filter: For particulate
  • Test first to determine level

Yellow Water

Tannins

  • Decaying organic matter
  • Tea-colored water
  • More common in shallow wells
  • Harmless but unpleasant

Iron Bacteria

  • Yellow-orange slime
  • Slimy texture
  • May have swampy odor
  • Needs well disinfection

Low-Level Iron

  • Light iron content
  • Not enough for brown
  • Still stains

Treatment

  • Tannins: Special tannin filter or anion exchange
  • Iron bacteria: Shock chlorination
  • Light iron: Softener or iron filter

Black Water

Manganese

  • Most common cause of black water
  • Often accompanies iron
  • Stains worse than iron
  • Creates black deposits

Sulfur/Sulfide

  • May have rotten egg smell
  • Can darken water
  • Hydrogen sulfide gas

Pipe Corrosion

  • Very old pipes
  • Internal deposits breaking loose

Treatment

  • Manganese filter (oxidizing filter)
  • Chlorination for sulfur
  • Test to confirm source

Other Colors

Blue/Green

  • Copper from pipes (acidic water)
  • Low pH dissolves copper
  • Check pH level
  • Treatment: Neutralizer to raise pH

White/Cloudy

  • Air bubbles (harmless, clears)
  • Hardness/scale
  • Sediment (doesn't clear)
  • Methane gas (rare)

Red/Orange

  • Iron (oxidized)
  • Same as brown, just different concentration

Pink/Purple

  • Potassium permanganate (from treatment system)
  • If no treatment: unusual, test water

Color by Cause Summary

Color Likely Cause Treatment
Brown/Red Iron Iron filter, softener
Yellow Tannins, iron bacteria Tannin filter, chlorination
Black Manganese Oxidizing filter
Blue/Green Copper (low pH) Neutralizer
Cloudy Air, sediment, hardness Varies

Sudden Discoloration

Causes of Sudden Changes

  • Pump stirred up sediment
  • Heavy water use drew from deeper
  • Well work/service
  • Aquifer changes
  • Pipe failure

What to Do

  1. Run water for 15-30 minutes
  2. If clears: likely temporary
  3. If persists: needs investigation
  4. Don't drink until tested

When to Test

  • Any sudden color change
  • New taste or odor
  • After any well work
  • Moving to new property

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well water brown?

Usually iron. Can also be sediment, rusty pipes, or tannins. Test to determine cause and treatment.

Is discolored well water safe to drink?

Most causes (iron, manganese, tannins) are aesthetic, not health hazards. But test to rule out contamination.

Will discolored water clear up on its own?

Sediment may settle. Air bubbles clear quickly. Iron/manganese won't clear—needs treatment.

Why did my water suddenly change color?

Could be pump disturbance, pipe failure, or aquifer change. Run water, see if clears. If not, have it checked.

Does a water softener fix brown water?

For low iron (under 2-3 ppm). Higher iron needs iron filter. Test first.
