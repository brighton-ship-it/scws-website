Connecting Your Well to an Irrigation System

Want to water your landscape without paying for expensive municipal water? Connecting your private well to an irrigation system can save hundreds or even thousands of dollars annually on water bills. But it requires careful planning—the wrong setup can damage your well, kill your plants, or leave you without household water during irrigation. This guide covers everything you need to know to design a well-based irrigation system that works.

🌱 Quick Reference: Well Irrigation Essentials

  • • Minimum well capacity: 5 GPM (10+ GPM ideal for combined use)
  • • Target pressure: 50-60 PSI at source (30-50 PSI at heads)
  • • Zone sizing: Keep each zone 20-30% below well capacity
  • • Essential equipment: Filter, pressure gauge, check valve
  • • Best practice: Irrigate during off-peak hours (night/early morning)

Can Your Well Support Irrigation?

Before connecting irrigation, you need to know if your well can handle the additional demand. Key factors include:

Well Yield (GPM)

Your well's flow rate—measured in gallons per minute (GPM)—determines how much water you can use at once. Here's a general guide:

Well Yield Irrigation Potential Recommended Approach
Under 3 GPM Very Limited Drip only, small zones, storage tank recommended
3-5 GPM Limited Small drip/micro-spray zones, careful scheduling
5-10 GPM Moderate Multiple small zones, mix of drip and rotary sprinklers
10-20 GPM Good Full irrigation capability, moderate zone sizes
20+ GPM Excellent Large zones, spray heads, can run multiple zones

Don't know your well's yield? We can perform a yield test to determine exactly what your well produces.

Recovery Rate

Beyond peak flow, your well's recovery rate matters for irrigation. If you draw 10 GPM but the well only recharges at 5 GPM, you'll eventually draw down the water level. Signs your well isn't keeping up include:

  • Pressure drops during long irrigation cycles
  • Air sputtering from faucets after watering
  • Pump cycling on and off rapidly
  • Sediment appearing after irrigation runs

Current Household Demand

If you're using the same well for house and irrigation, calculate your peak household demand. A family of four typically uses 3-5 GPM during peak times (morning showers, dishwasher, laundry). Your well needs enough capacity for both uses simultaneously—or you need to schedule irrigation during low-demand hours.

System Design Options

There are several ways to configure well-based irrigation, each with pros and cons:

Option 1: Direct Connection to House System

The simplest approach connects irrigation to your existing household water supply after the pressure tank.

Direct Connection Setup

✅ Advantages

  • • Lowest installation cost
  • • Simple plumbing
  • • Uses existing pressure tank
  • • No additional equipment needed

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Affects household pressure during irrigation
  • • Accelerates pump wear
  • • Limited by existing pump/tank capacity
  • • Can't run household and irrigation heavily at once

Best for: Small irrigation systems, wells with 15+ GPM, properties where irrigation runs at night only.

Option 2: Dedicated Irrigation Pump

Install a second submersible pump in the same well, sized specifically for irrigation.

Dedicated Pump Setup

✅ Advantages

  • • Household pressure unaffected
  • • Pump sized for irrigation demand
  • • Independent operation
  • • Can optimize for different pressure needs

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Higher installation cost ($2,500-5,000+)
  • • Well must accommodate two pumps
  • • Both pumps draw from same water supply
  • • Additional electrical requirements

Best for: Larger irrigation systems, properties with high household demand, wells with 10+ GPM.

Option 3: Storage Tank System

Fill a storage tank slowly from your well, then pump from the tank for irrigation at higher flow rates.

Storage Tank Setup

✅ Advantages

  • • Works with low-yield wells
  • • Well never overdrawn
  • • Can deliver high GPM from tank
  • • Allows sediment to settle

❌ Disadvantages

  • • Higher cost (tank + transfer pump + booster)
  • • Requires space for tank (500-2,500+ gallons)
  • • More components to maintain
  • • Tank may need treatment to prevent algae

Best for: Low-yield wells (under 5 GPM), large irrigation systems, properties needing water storage for other purposes.

Calculating Water Requirements

Proper zone design starts with understanding how much water each component needs:

Sprinkler Head Flow Rates

Sprinkler Type GPM per Head Pressure Needed Coverage
Fixed spray heads 1.0-3.0 GPM 30-40 PSI 4-15 ft radius
Rotary nozzles (MP Rotators) 0.5-1.5 GPM 40-50 PSI 8-30 ft radius
Rotor heads (gear-driven) 2.0-6.0 GPM 40-50 PSI 20-50 ft radius
Impact rotors 3.0-8.0 GPM 35-50 PSI 25-45 ft radius
Drip emitters 0.5-2.0 GPH (not GPM) 15-30 PSI Point source

Zone Sizing Example

Let's say your well produces 10 GPM sustainably. Here's how to design zones:

📐 Zone Design Example (10 GPM Well)

Target zone flow: 7-8 GPM (70-80% of well capacity)

  • Zone 1 (Front lawn): 8 rotary nozzles × 1.0 GPM = 8 GPM ✓
  • Zone 2 (Side yard): 4 spray heads × 1.5 GPM = 6 GPM ✓
  • Zone 3 (Back lawn): 3 rotor heads × 2.5 GPM = 7.5 GPM ✓
  • Zone 4 (Garden drip): 100 ft drip line @ 0.5 GPH/ft = 4 GPM ✓

Accounting for Pressure Loss

Water loses pressure as it travels through pipes. Calculate pressure loss to ensure adequate pressure at each head:

  • Pipe friction: ~4 PSI per 100 feet of 1" PVC at 10 GPM
  • Elevation change: 0.43 PSI per vertical foot (subtract for uphill, add for downhill)
  • Valves and fittings: ~2-5 PSI per valve/filter assembly
  • Meter (if applicable): 5-10 PSI

Example: If your well delivers 55 PSI at the pressure tank, and you lose 4 PSI in 100 ft of pipe, 4 PSI in elevation rise, and 3 PSI in the valve box, you'll have ~44 PSI at the sprinkler heads—adequate for most systems.

Essential Equipment

A well-based irrigation system needs several components beyond the basics:

🔵 Filtration System

Essential. Screen filter (100-200 mesh) minimum. Disc or media filter for drip. Prevents clogged heads and emitters. Clean monthly or as needed.

🟢 Pressure Gauge

Install at the irrigation takeoff point. Monitors system health and alerts you to problems. Pressure drop = time to clean filter or check for leaks.

🟡 Check Valve

Prevents backflow from irrigation into your drinking water system. Required by code in most areas. Install at point of connection.

🟣 Pressure Regulator

Required for drip zones (reduces to 15-30 PSI). Optional for spray zones but extends component life. Match to system requirements.

🟠 Flow Sensor (Optional)

Monitors actual flow rates. Smart controllers can detect leaks or broken heads by comparing expected vs. actual flow. Worth the investment.

⚫ Master Valve (Optional)

Shuts off irrigation supply when system isn't running. Reduces leak damage. Some smart controllers require this for leak detection.

Programming for Well Protection

How you schedule irrigation can make or break a well-based system:

Schedule During Off-Peak Hours

Run irrigation when household demand is lowest—typically 10 PM to 6 AM. This ensures adequate pressure for irrigation without affecting showers, laundry, or other uses.

Include Rest Intervals

✅ Smart Scheduling for Well Recovery

Instead of running zones back-to-back, insert rest intervals:

  • • Zone 1: 15 minutes → Rest: 10 minutes
  • • Zone 2: 15 minutes → Rest: 10 minutes
  • • Zone 3: 15 minutes → Rest: 10 minutes
  • • Zone 4 (drip): 30 minutes

Total time: ~95 minutes with recovery built in

Use Cycle and Soak

"Cycle and soak" runs each zone for a shorter period, repeats the cycle 2-3 times with rest between. This helps water absorb into soil (reducing runoff) AND gives your well recovery time. Most modern controllers have this feature built in.

Consider Smart Controllers

Weather-based smart controllers (like Rachio, Hunter Hydrawise, or Rain Bird ESP-TM2) adjust watering based on weather conditions. Benefits for well users:

  • Skip watering when it rains (less well usage)
  • Reduce watering in cool weather (less pump wear)
  • Flow monitoring detects leaks that would overdraw well
  • Alerts for system problems

Choosing the Right Sprinkler Types

For well-based systems, some sprinkler types work better than others:

Best Choices for Wells

🏆 Recommended for Well Irrigation

MP Rotators

Low flow (0.5-1.5 GPM), matched precipitation, excellent for low-yield wells

Drip Irrigation

Lowest flow rates, most efficient, perfect for gardens and shrubs

High-Efficiency Rotors

Modern gear-driven rotors, adjustable flow, good for larger areas

Types to Avoid or Limit

🚫 Standard Spray Heads

High flow rates (1.5-3+ GPM each), high precipitation rate leads to runoff. If used, limit to small zones with cycle/soak.

🚫 Large Impact Sprinklers

Can use 5-8 GPM each. One zone might exceed your well's capacity. Better for agricultural wells with high yield.

Maintenance for Well-Based Irrigation

Monthly Tasks

  • Check and clean filter: Well water clogs filters faster than city water
  • Inspect pressure gauge: Note normal operating pressure; investigate drops
  • Run each zone manually: Look for broken heads, dry spots, or overspray
  • Check for leaks: Puddles, unusually green spots, or eroded areas

Seasonal Tasks

  • Spring: System startup, check all heads, clear obstructions, verify controller programming
  • Summer: Adjust schedules for heat, check for drought stress, monitor well performance
  • Fall: Reduce watering as weather cools, prepare for winterization
  • Winter: Winterize exposed components in freeze-prone areas

Signs of Problems

⚠️ Pressure Drop During Irrigation

Could be clogged filter, leak in system, or well not keeping up. Check filter first, then inspect for leaks.

⚠️ Sputtering from Household Faucets

Irrigation is overdrawing the well. Reduce zone sizes or add rest intervals.

⚠️ Pump Running During Off Hours

May indicate a leak in irrigation or plumbing. Check for stuck valves or broken lines.

⚠️ Clogged Emitters/Heads

Filter may need cleaning or upgrading. Well sediment may have increased—check your well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my well for irrigation and household water?

Yes, many homeowners use the same well for both household and irrigation use. However, you need adequate well capacity—typically 10+ GPM for combined use. Running irrigation during non-peak household hours helps, and some homeowners install a separate irrigation pump or storage tank system.

What GPM do I need for well irrigation?

Irrigation needs vary by system size. A small residential system might need 5-10 GPM, while larger properties need 15-30+ GPM. Most sprinkler heads use 1-4 GPM each, and drip zones typically need 2-4 GPM. Calculate total GPM per zone and ensure your well exceeds that by 20-30%.

What pressure do I need for sprinklers from a well?

Most sprinkler systems need 30-50 PSI at the heads for proper coverage. Factor in pressure loss from pipes (about 4 PSI per 100 feet), elevation changes (0.43 PSI per foot of rise), and valves/fittings. Your well system should deliver 50-60 PSI at the source for most residential irrigation.

Should I use a separate pump for irrigation?

A dedicated irrigation pump offers advantages: it can be sized specifically for irrigation demand, doesn't affect household pressure, and extends the life of your main pump. Options include a second submersible pump, a surface booster pump, or drawing from a storage tank with a dedicated pump.

How do I size zones for well irrigation?

Size zones based on your well's sustainable flow rate. If your well produces 10 GPM, design zones that use 7-8 GPM maximum. Smaller zones are better for wells—you can always run multiple cycles. Include rest time between zones to let the well recover.

Will irrigation damage my well?

Heavy irrigation can stress a well if water is drawn faster than it recharges. Signs of overuse include pressure drops, air in lines, sediment in water, and pump cycling. Protect your well by sizing zones appropriately, watering during recovery hours (night), and using a timer with rest intervals.

Do I need a filter for well irrigation water?

Yes, filtration is highly recommended. Sand and sediment can clog sprinkler heads and drip emitters. At minimum, use a screen filter (100-200 mesh). For drip systems, add a disc or media filter. Clean filters regularly—well water clogs them faster than city water.

Can I use well water for drip irrigation?

Yes, drip irrigation works well with wells because it uses less water (2-4 GPM per zone typically). However, drip emitters are more sensitive to sediment and mineral buildup. Use proper filtration (150+ mesh) and consider a pressure regulator since drip operates at 15-30 PSI.
