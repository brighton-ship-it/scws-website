Rusty Water from Your Fontana Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Fontana well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Fontana well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Water in Fontana: A Local Guide

Fontana is one of the larger cities in San Bernardino County, spread across the alluvial plain at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains in the western Inland Empire, between Rialto, Rancho Cucamonga, and Bloomington. The valley floor here is built from deep alluvial fans — gravel, sand, and silt washed down from the mountains over millennia — that form the productive aquifers of the upper Santa Ana River watershed. Groundwater has long been central to this area, and that geology is exactly why iron and manganese show up in private wells around Fontana's more rural and agricultural edges. When that water runs orange, red, or brown, naturally occurring minerals are usually the reason.

Fontana also has a substantial stock of older homes, many of them built during the city's mid-century growth, and plenty still carry galvanized steel plumbing. So rusty water in Fontana can come from the aquifer through a private well or from corrosion inside a home's own pipes and water heater. Telling the two apart is the first and most important step toward a lasting fix.

What Causes the Discoloration

Dissolved iron and manganese

In the sandy, gravelly aquifers beneath Fontana, iron and manganese dissolve into groundwater in a clear, invisible form. They only become visible rust when they meet oxygen at your faucet, which is why well water can look clean at the tap and turn orange as it sits in a glass. Manganese produces a darker brown-black stain and is common in these alluvial deposits.

Iron bacteria

These harmless organisms feed on iron and leave a slimy, reddish residue that clogs filters and irrigation emitters and coats toilet tanks. A swampy odor or an oily sheen on standing water points to iron bacteria rather than dissolved mineral.

Corroding well casing

Many Fontana-area wells have been in service for decades. An aging steel casing corrodes over time and sheds rust directly into the water, sometimes along with a noticeable change in taste or a gradual loss of yield.

Galvanized pipes and water heaters

On both city water and well water, a home's own plumbing can be the source. Galvanized steel supply lines rust from the inside out, and a water heater with a worn anode rod releases rust on the hot side. The desert-edge climate and hard mineral water of the Inland Empire are especially tough on water heaters, accelerating sediment buildup and corrosion.

Tracking Down the Source

  • Hot side only? The water heater is the prime suspect.
  • Clear at the tap, orange in the glass? Dissolved iron from a well.
  • Whole house vs. one fixture? House-wide rust points to the well or main; a single tap points to a corroded local line.
  • Orange slime in the tank? Iron bacteria, which need disinfection.
  • Worst in the morning, clears after a minute? Sediment settling in galvanized pipe overnight.

Solutions for Fontana Properties

Iron and manganese filtration

Air-injection or catalytic oxidizing filters convert dissolved iron and manganese into a solid the filter can capture — the standard fix for clear-water iron from a well. We size the unit to your tested mineral levels and household demand.

Water softening for hardness plus light iron

The Inland Empire is known for hard water, and where hardness comes with modest iron, a correctly sized softener handles both while protecting fixtures and water heaters from scale. Heavier iron loads get a dedicated filter first.

Well rehabilitation and casing repair

Shock chlorination clears iron bacteria from the well and plumbing. A downhole camera inspection reveals whether a corroded casing should be lined or replaced.

Repiping and water heater service

Replacing galvanized pipe with PEX or copper eliminates household rust permanently, and a new anode rod or water heater resolves hot-side discoloration.

When to Call a Professional

Call a licensed well contractor if the rust appears suddenly and severely, if it comes with falling pressure or air spitting from the taps, if you find iron-bacteria slime, or if a water test shows iron above the 0.3 mg/L secondary standard. On a well, a change in taste, odor, or yield alongside the discoloration can indicate casing or pump trouble that should be inspected promptly. Stains that keep returning after a filter is installed mean the real source was never addressed.

Why Inland Empire Water Needs Local Know-How

Groundwater chemistry across San Bernardino County's alluvial basins is not uniform — iron, manganese, and hardness all vary with well depth and the specific sediments a well draws from. A treatment system that works perfectly for one Fontana property can be undersized or simply wrong for the next. Decades of work in the upper Santa Ana basin let us read the symptoms quickly, test accurately, and specify equipment that matches your actual water rather than a generic profile. That experience is also why we test first and recommend second.

Drought, Demand, and Well Stress

Southern California's recurring droughts pull water tables down, and as a well draws from lower in the aquifer it can pick up more sediment and minerals than it did in wetter years. Heavy summer irrigation demand in Fontana's larger lots adds to that stress. If your water turned rusty during a dry stretch or after a season of heavy use, the change may reflect a falling water table rather than a sudden equipment failure — another reason a quick professional assessment is worthwhile.

The Real Cost of Leaving It Alone

Rusty water in Fontana rarely improves on its own. In a home with galvanized plumbing, the same corrosion that tints the water keeps closing off the pipes, so pressure fades year after year until repiping is the only option. On a well, untreated iron and manganese plate out on pump components, foul pressure tanks, and stain every fixture and load of laundry, while iron-bacteria colonies spread through the casing and irrigation lines. The hard, mineral-heavy water common across the Inland Empire compounds the damage to water heaters. Addressing the problem while it is still a water-quality issue — rather than a plumbing-replacement project — is consistently the most economical choice.

A lab profile removes the guesswork from system selection and gives you a documented baseline for future years. For Fontana well owners especially, that one step prevents buying equipment that is mismatched to the water and saves money over the life of the system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the rusty water from my Fontana well safe to drink?

Iron and manganese are aesthetic contaminants rather than acute health hazards at typical concentrations, so the water is usually not dangerous. We still recommend a full test before drinking from a well that has changed color, since discoloration can mask other issues.

Why is my water so hard and rusty at the same time?

The Inland Empire's aquifers are mineral-rich, so hardness and iron often occur together. A combined approach — an iron filter plus a softener, or a softener alone for light iron — addresses both.

My well water tastes metallic. What does that mean?

A metallic taste usually signals iron, and sometimes a corroding steel casing. A water test and, if needed, a camera inspection will pinpoint whether the iron is from the aquifer or the casing.

Can a softener alone fix my rusty water?

For modest dissolved iron combined with hardness, yes. For heavier iron or any manganese, a dedicated oxidizing filter is more reliable.

What is the orange slime in my toilet tank?

Iron bacteria. It is harmless to health but clogs plumbing and returns if untreated. Shock chlorination of the well and plumbing is the cure.
