What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Garnet Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Garnet

Garnet is an unincorporated community in Riverside County near North Palm Springs, spread across the windswept floor of the San Gorgonio Pass where Interstate 10 meets the famous San Gorgonio Pass wind farm. This is rugged, geologically active country: the Garnet Hill fault, the southernmost segment of the San Andreas Fault Zone in the area, runs along the base of Whitewater Hill, and the terrain is a mix of deep alluvial fans washing out of the surrounding mountains and harder, fractured bedrock along the canyon margins. Many properties out here rely on private wells, and in this desert-pass environment a low-yield well is a serious problem. Hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is frequently the most economical way to increase a Garnet well's output without the cost of drilling a new one.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates an existing well using clean water under high pressure. We lower inflatable packers into the casing to isolate a target zone, then inject water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. That pressure flushes silt, fine sand, and mineral scale out of the formation and reopens or extends the natural fractures that carry groundwater to your well. In Garnet's mix of alluvial fan and fractured rock, this cleaning and re-development can significantly improve a struggling well. The process uses no chemicals and is usually completed in four to eight hours.

Why Garnet Wells Lose Yield

The San Gorgonio Pass environment creates several distinct challenges:

  • Fractured-rock wells along the canyon margins. Properties near Whitewater Canyon and the fault-zone bedrock depend on a limited fracture network. Tight or partially sealed fractures mean low yield from the start.
  • Sediment clogging in alluvial-fan wells. The coarse-to-fine fan deposits washing out of the mountains carry abundant sediment that packs into the formation and well perforations over time.
  • Desert recharge limits. This is a dry pass with strong winds and limited rainfall, so a well that captures water inefficiently feels every bit of that scarcity.
  • Aging and hard-water-scaled wells. Mineral-rich groundwater leaves scale that compounds clogging, especially in older boreholes.

Because these are flow-restriction problems rather than a total absence of groundwater, hydrofracturing is well suited to many Garnet wells, particularly those in fractured rock.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Time your recovery. Note how long the well takes to refill after heavy use; a slow recovery suggests restricted inflow that treatment can improve.
  • Rule out the simple parts. A waterlogged pressure tank ($600-$1,500) or a worn pressure switch ($150-$350) can mimic a weak well and is inexpensive to replace.
  • Observe the pump. Short-cycling or loss of prime usually means the water level is dropping faster than the well recovers.
  • Locate your driller's log. It tells us your depth and whether you are in alluvial fan or fractured rock, guiding the treatment.
  • Note sediment and scaling. Sand, grit, or heavy mineral deposits shape the plan and any follow-up conditioning.

When to Call a Professional

If your Garnet well runs short under normal demand, recovers slowly, or has lost output over the years, schedule a professional evaluation. Frequently Asked Questions

Will the area's faults and seismic activity affect my well or the treatment?

Hydrofracturing is a controlled, low-volume process confined to your well and the immediate formation around it. It is unrelated to seismic activity and does not affect faults. We treat each zone within safe pressure limits after inspecting the casing.

Is my Garnet well a good candidate?

Wells drawing from fractured rock along the canyon margins are excellent candidates, and clogged alluvial-fan wells often respond well too. Wells finished entirely in loose sand are generally not suitable. Our diagnostic determines which applies before any work begins.

How much can my yield improve?

For suitable wells in Riverside County's fractured and alluvial formations, yield commonly improves two to five times, with success in roughly 70 to 80 percent of good candidates. We document your flow before and after.

Does the process use chemicals?

No. It uses only clean water under high pressure, with no additives, which matters in a desert environment.

How long does the treatment take?

The injection usually takes four to eight hours in a single visit. We run a post-treatment flow test, and most owners see improved, stabilized flow within 24 to 48 hours.

What if hydrofracturing does not produce enough improvement?

If a treated well underperforms, the diagnostic data guides the next step, whether deeper rehabilitation or a new well. The investigation is never wasted, and we present every option with honest pricing.
