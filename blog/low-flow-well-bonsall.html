Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Diego County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Why Bonsall Wells Lose Flow — and How We Bring It Back

Bonsall sits in the lower San Luis Rey River valley of northern San Diego County, a landscape of rolling avocado and citrus groves, horse ranches, and rural estates strung between Fallbrook, Vista, and Oceanside. Nearly every property off the water district mains here depends on a private well, and the hydrogeology is a story of two very different aquifers. Along the valley floor you may draw from shallow alluvial gravels only a few dozen feet deep, while homes up on the surrounding hills tap fractured granitic bedrock that can run 200 to 400 feet or deeper. When a Bonsall well starts delivering weak flow at the tap, the cause almost always traces back to one of these two systems — and knowing which one you have is the first step to fixing it.

The Real Causes of Low Yield in Bonsall

"Low flow" is a symptom, not a diagnosis. since 2020 of well work across San Diego County, we see the same handful of root causes again and again in the Bonsall area:

  • A dropping water table. The San Luis Rey valley aquifer rises and falls with winter rain. After a dry stretch, the static water level can fall below where your pump is set, so the pump starts sucking air and cavitating. Flow surges, then sputters.
  • A pump set too high or undersized. Many older Bonsall wells were fitted with a submersible pump hung well above the bottom of the casing. As the aquifer declines seasonally, that once-adequate setting becomes the bottleneck.
  • Clogged screen or perforations. The mineral-rich groundwater here deposits calcium and iron scale on the well screen. Add iron-bacteria biofilm — a slimy, rust-colored growth common in the valley — and the open area that lets water into the casing slowly plugs shut.
  • Silt and partial casing collapse. Decomposed granite sloughs fine sand into the borehole. Over years, sediment builds in the bottom of the well and can bury the pump intake or block a lower producing zone.
  • A worn pump or bad check valve. An aging impeller stack simply moves less water than it did when new, and a failed check valve lets water drain back down the drop pipe between cycles.
  • Drought-reduced recharge. Southern California's multi-year droughts leave the aquifer with less water to give, and hillside bedrock wells feel it first.

Common Bonsall Scenarios We Get Called For

A grove owner off West Lilac Road calls because the drip irrigation that used to run two zones at once now barely holds pressure on one. A family on a hilltop parcel near Gird Road notices the shower goes weak whenever the washing machine and dishwasher run together. A horse property near the river bottom finds the well recovers fine overnight but can't keep up with a full afternoon of stock tanks and arena watering. Each of these is a classic low-yield pattern — plenty of water in storage, but not enough instantaneous flow to meet peak demand. The fix is rarely "drill a new well." More often it's matching your system design to what the aquifer can realistically deliver.

What to Check Before You Call

You can gather useful clues yourself. Note whether the pressure drop is constant or only appears during heavy use. Listen for the pump short-cycling (rapid on-off clicking at the pressure switch), which points to a waterlogged pressure tank rather than the well itself. Check that your pressure tank's air charge is correct and that the pressure switch (a $150–$350 part) isn't sticking. If flow is fine first thing in the morning but collapses by evening, that seasonal or daily recovery pattern strongly suggests a yield limitation rather than an equipment failure.

How We Diagnose a Low-Flow Well

When our technician arrives, we run a real hydraulic workup, not a guess:

  1. We measure the static water level (how high the water stands when the pump is off) and compare it to well records to see how far the table has dropped.
  2. We run the pump and track drawdown — how far the level falls while pumping — and the pumping water level where it stabilizes.
  3. We perform a well yield and recovery test, timing how fast the well refills, to establish your true sustainable GPM.
  4. We pull records or camera the casing to confirm pump depth, pump condition, and any silt or scale on the screen.

From those numbers we can tell you whether the problem is the pump, the aquifer, or a plugged screen — and which fix actually pays off.

Solutions That Work in the San Luis Rey Valley

Depending on what the testing reveals, we recommend one or more of the following:

  • Lower or replace the pump. If the water is there but the pump is set too high or worn out, resetting it deeper or installing a properly sized submersible ($2,500–$5,500 installed) restores flow. A new control box or capacitor runs $400–$900.
  • Rehabilitate the well. Mechanical brushing plus a chemical or acid treatment dissolves scale and kills iron bacteria, reopening a clogged screen. This is often the most cost-effective way to recover lost yield on an older Bonsall bedrock well.
  • Deepen the well or drill new. When the aquifer has genuinely declined, deepening into a lower zone or drilling a replacement (a turnkey new well runs roughly $18,000–$42,000 depending on depth and geology) may be the only durable answer.
  • Add storage and a booster. For a low but steady producer, a cistern or storage tank collects water around the clock, then a booster or constant-pressure system ($2,000–$4,500) delivers strong household pressure on demand. This is the workhorse solution for hillside Bonsall properties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Bonsall well produce less water in late summer?

The San Luis Rey valley aquifer recharges mainly from winter rain. By late summer the static water level has fallen, and if your pump is set high, it can begin drawing air. A yield test tells us whether lowering the pump or adding storage is the right response.

Is low flow the same as low pressure?

Not always. Low pressure can come from a bad pressure switch or waterlogged tank even when the well produces plenty of water. Low flow means the well itself can't deliver enough gallons per minute. We test both so you don't pay to fix the wrong thing.

Can a clogged well screen really be reopened?

Yes. Mineral scale and iron-bacteria biofilm are common in Bonsall groundwater, and mechanical brushing combined with an acid or chemical treatment frequently restores a well close to its original yield — far cheaper than drilling new.

How many gallons per minute do I actually need?

A typical single-family home needs roughly 3–5 GPM, while properties with groves, livestock, or multiple bathrooms need more. Many Bonsall wells produce 1–3 GPM, which is workable with a storage tank and booster pump.

Should I deepen my existing well or drill a new one?

It depends on your casing condition and the geology below. Deepening is often cheaper when the existing well is sound; a new well makes sense when the casing is collapsing or the current zone is exhausted. We base the recommendation on your well's actual test data.
