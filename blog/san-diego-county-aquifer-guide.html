Alluvial Basin Aquifers

San Diego's valleys contain alluvial aquifers—groundwater stored in sand and gravel deposited by rivers. San Pasqual Valley, Santee-El Monte, and the San Diego River Valley are primary examples. These aquifers typically offer good yields (10-100+ GPM) and moderate depths (100-400 feet). Water quality varies; hardness and iron are common concerns requiring treatment.

Fractured Rock Groundwater

Mountain and foothill areas rely on fractured rock systems rather than traditional aquifers. Water moves through cracks in granite and metamorphic rock. Yields are unpredictable—hitting the right fracture zone makes all the difference. Water quality is typically excellent, often naturally soft with low mineral content. These systems recharge primarily through winter precipitation.

Coastal and Urban Fringe Areas

Coastal San Diego has limited fresh groundwater resources. Saltwater intrusion threatens some near-ocean areas. Urban development has paved over recharge zones in many locations. Wells in these areas may encounter higher TDS, require deeper drilling, or face permit restrictions. Always research local conditions before planning coastal well projects.

Water Quality by Region

Regional water chemistry reflects local geology. East County mountains: typically soft, low TDS, possible low pH requiring treatment. Valley areas: moderate hardness, possible iron and manganese staining. North County: varies widely from hard water to agricultural contamination concerns. Borrego: good quality but declining water levels. Always test your specific well—generalizations only go so far.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are San Diego's main aquifers?

San Diego County has multiple groundwater basins including San Pasqual, Santee-El Monte, San Diego River Valley, and Borrego Valley. Coastal areas have smaller alluvial aquifers. Mountains rely on fractured rock groundwater rather than traditional aquifers.

How does San Diego groundwater quality vary?

Quality varies significantly. Mountain areas often have excellent, naturally soft water. Valley aquifers may have hardness and iron issues. Coastal areas can have elevated TDS and saltwater intrusion concerns. Testing reveals your specific water chemistry.

Are San Diego aquifers being depleted?

Some basins are overdrafted, particularly Borrego Valley. Others like San Pasqual are carefully managed. The Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) now regulates critically overdrafted basins. Most San Diego wells draw from areas with adequate long-term supply.
