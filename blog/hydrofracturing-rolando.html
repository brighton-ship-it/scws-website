What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Rolando Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Rolando

In Rolando, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Rolando wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in Rolando: What City-Water Homeowners Should Know

Rolando is a close-knit residential neighborhood on the eastern edge of the City of San Diego, tucked between the College Area and the city of La Mesa. Its tidy streets and Spanish-revival bungalows sit on developed urban land, and virtually every home here is connected to municipal water — supplied through the City of San Diego's system, with neighboring La Mesa served by the Helix Water District. For the overwhelming majority of Rolando residents, that means there is no private well on the property and no need for one. So let's be candid: hydrofracturing is rarely relevant to a typical Rolando home.

We get calls from this part of San Diego anyway, usually for one of two reasons. Either a homeowner is researching well options out of curiosity or for a future rural property, or there is a rare older parcel — the kind of larger estate lot you occasionally find around the College Area, Mount Helix, and the La Mesa hills — that still has a legacy private well. This guide is written honestly for both. If you don't have a well, we'll point you to what actually helps. If you do, we'll tell you whether hydrofracturing is worth considering.

Why Hydrofracturing Usually Doesn't Apply Here

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation technique. It uses high-pressure clean water to open and flush the natural fractures in hard bedrock around a well, increasing how much groundwater can flow in. It only makes sense if three things are true: you have a private well, that well is completed in fractured rock (not loose sediment), and its yield is declining. In an urban neighborhood like Rolando, the first condition is almost never met. You cannot hydrofracture a municipal water connection — there is nothing to fracture.

Coastal and near-coastal San Diego County also has limited natural groundwater to begin with; the region imports most of its water precisely because local aquifers are modest. That is another reason private wells are scarce in built-out neighborhoods here, and why the technique that thrives in the granite backcountry of east San Diego County rarely has a role in Rolando proper.

The Problems Rolando Homeowners Actually Have

When a Rolando household notices a water problem, it is almost always something other than a well-yield issue. The common ones we hear about:

  • Low pressure at the fixtures. On city water this is usually a pressure-regulator problem, a partially closed valve, or corroded galvanized supply lines in an older home — not a supply shortage.
  • Hard water and scale. San Diego's imported water is notably hard. White crust on faucets, spotty dishes, and short water-heater life are treatment problems solved with a softener or filtration, not with anything well-related.
  • Taste, odor, or chlorine. These are water-quality concerns addressed with point-of-use or whole-house filtration.
  • A rare legacy well that has gone unused. Some older estates have an abandoned or inactive well. If it is no longer usable, the responsible step is proper decommissioning, not rehabilitation.

For nearly all of these, the right solution is a treatment system or a plumbing fix — areas where we are glad to advise even when there is no well involved.

The Rare Case: An Active Private Well Near Rolando

If you are among the small number of property owners in the broader College Area, La Mesa, or Mount Helix vicinity with a working private well, then the standard well-care logic applies. The first question is what your well is completed in. East San Diego County transitions toward decomposed granite and crystalline bedrock as you move inland and uphill, and a well bottomed in genuinely fractured rock could be a hydrofracturing candidate if its yield is declining. A well in shallow alluvium or a sediment-filled valley would not be.

Even then, hydrofracturing is only worth it if a diagnostic shows a yield problem rooted in clogged or limited fractures. More often, a struggling private well in a developed area turns out to need a new pump, a deeper setting, or simply to be retired in favor of the municipal connection that already runs to the street. We will assess honestly and recommend the lowest-cost effective path — which, for many urban lots, is to abandon the old well properly and rely on city water.

It is also worth understanding how Rolando came to be all-municipal. The neighborhood was largely developed in the early-to-mid twentieth century as San Diego expanded eastward, and city water mains were extended to serve the new tracts from the start. Unlike the rural parcels of the backcountry that were settled around individual wells, Rolando never depended on private groundwater. That history is the simplest explanation for why a well-rehabilitation service like hydrofracturing, so valuable just twenty or thirty miles inland in the granite hills, has almost no application on these particular streets.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Confirm whether you even have a well. Many homeowners assume an old pipe or pump house means an active well; often it is long dead or capped. Knowing this saves everyone time.
  2. Identify your water source. If your bill comes from the City of San Diego or Helix Water District, you are on municipal water and a well issue is unlikely to be your problem.
  3. Check the pressure regulator. On city water, a failing regulator is the most common cause of pressure complaints — an inexpensive fix.
  4. Test for hardness. If scale is your complaint, a simple hardness test points straight to a softener or filtration solution.
  5. Locate any well records. If a legacy well exists, its completion report tells us depth, casing, and geology — the facts that determine whether any well work makes sense.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do most homes in Rolando have wells?

No. Rolando is a built-out urban neighborhood on municipal water, supplied through the City of San Diego with neighboring La Mesa on the Helix Water District. Private wells are very rare here.

Then why would I consider hydrofracturing?

You generally wouldn't. Hydrofracturing only applies to a private well completed in fractured rock with declining yield. If you are on city water, it has no role. We only recommend it for the rare active bedrock well that a diagnostic shows would benefit.

My water pressure is low — is that a well problem?

On city water, almost certainly not. The usual culprits are a failing pressure regulator, a partly closed valve, or old corroded supply lines. These are inexpensive plumbing fixes.

I have an old, unused well on my property. What should I do?

If it is no longer usable, the correct step is proper decommissioning to protect groundwater and meet county requirements — typically $1,500-$5,000 depending on depth and access. We can evaluate and handle it.

Can you help with hard water even though I don't have a well?

Yes. San Diego's water is hard, and we install softeners and filtration for city-water homes. A quick hardness test tells us what you need.

What does an assessment cost?

Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward any work we perform, so the assessment effectively pays for itself once you move forward.
