Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Yield Wells in Redlands: A Local Perspective

Redlands sits at the eastern edge of the San Bernardino Valley in San Bernardino County, tucked against the foothills of the San Bernardino Mountains and famous for the citrus groves that still ring the city. Groundwater here comes largely from the deep alluvial fill of the upper Santa Ana River watershed — the Bunker Hill and Yucaipa basins — where sand-and-gravel aquifers can be hundreds of feet thick. Properties in the higher terrain toward Mentone, Yucaipa, and the mountain edge often drill through that alluvium into fractured granitic bedrock. That mix of geology means a low-flow complaint in Redlands can have very different roots depending on where in town you sit, and treating it correctly starts with understanding your particular aquifer.

What Actually Causes Weak Flow Here

When a Redlands well stops delivering the gallons per minute it once did, the culprit is usually one of these:

  • A declining regional water table. The Santa Ana basins have seen long-term drawdown from decades of pumping and successive droughts. When the static level drops below a pump that was set for wetter years, output falls off and the pump may draw air.
  • An undersized, worn, or too-shallow pump. A submersible that was correctly placed in the 1990s may now hang above the current water level. Worn impellers also quietly lose capacity over time.
  • Screen and perforation fouling. Redlands groundwater is hard and mineral-rich. Calcium carbonate scale plus iron-bacteria biofilm coats the screen and chokes off the openings that let water into the casing.
  • Silted-in or partially collapsed casing. Fine alluvial sand settles to the bottom of the borehole over the years and can bury a lower producing zone or the pump intake itself.
  • Reduced aquifer recharge. The Santa Ana River and mountain-front runoff recharge these basins. Dry winters mean less recharge, and that shows up as lower well yields the following season.

Scenarios We See Across Redlands

A grove owner in south Redlands finds the irrigation risers that once ran a full block of trees now struggle to pressurize half of them. A homeowner up toward Mentone reports the second-floor shower goes to a trickle whenever the landscape sprinklers kick on. A property near the Crafton hills has a well that pumps strong for twenty minutes, then the flow drops sharply and takes hours to recover — a textbook sign of a well drawing down faster than the aquifer can feed it. In each case the total water in storage may be fine; the limitation is how many gallons per minute the well can sustain at peak demand.

Simple Checks You Can Make First

Before scheduling a visit, note the pattern. Does pressure sag only during heavy simultaneous use, or all the time? Listen at the pressure tank for rapid on-off cycling, which usually means a waterlogged tank or a failing pressure switch ($150–$350) rather than a well problem. Confirm the tank holds its air charge. If your well pumps fine in the morning but fades by evening and recovers overnight, that daily rhythm points squarely at a yield limitation and is worth a professional test.

Our Diagnostic Process

We don't replace parts on a hunch. A proper low-flow evaluation in Redlands looks like this:

  1. Measure the static water level with the pump off and compare it against the well log to gauge how far the table has fallen.
  2. Run the pump and record drawdown and the stabilized pumping water level.
  3. Conduct a well yield and recovery (GPM) test to establish the true sustainable output and how quickly the well recharges.
  4. Verify pump depth and condition and, where needed, run a downhole camera to inspect the screen for scale, biofilm, or silt.

Those measurements tell us whether you're dealing with a tired pump, a plugged screen, or a genuinely depleted zone — and steer you away from spending money on the wrong repair.

Fixes That Restore Yield

  • Reset or replace the pump. Lowering a pump that's hanging too high, or installing a correctly sized submersible ($2,500–$5,500 installed), often recovers flow immediately. Control box or capacitor replacement runs $400–$900.
  • Well rehabilitation. Mechanical brushing plus acid or chemical treatment strips scale and iron bacteria from the screen. On Redlands wells fouled by hard water, this frequently returns much of the lost capacity at a fraction of the cost of drilling.
  • Deepen or drill new. If the producing zone is exhausted or the casing is failing, deepening the well or drilling a replacement (a turnkey new well typically runs $18,000–$42,000) provides a lasting solution.
  • Storage plus a booster. For a low but steady well, a cistern collects water 24/7 and a booster or constant-pressure system ($2,000–$4,500) delivers full household pressure. This is an excellent match for foothill Redlands homes with modest yield.

When It's Time to Call a Pro

Don't wait for the well to go dry. Air sputtering at the tap, sandy or discolored water, a pump that never shuts off, or flow that steadily weakens through the day all warrant a prompt look. Addressing these early almost always means a smaller, cheaper repair than an emergency replacement.

How Redlands Geology Shapes Your Options

The difference between a valley-floor well and a foothill well in Redlands is not a technicality — it drives which repair makes sense. Down in the heart of the Bunker Hill and San Bernardino basins, wells draw from thick, permeable sand-and-gravel alluvium that historically produced generously. When one of these wells goes weak, the trouble is more often a fouled screen, a silted bottom, or a pump that no longer reaches the current water level than a truly exhausted aquifer, so rehabilitation and pump work tend to pay off. Toward the Crafton hills, Mentone, and the mountain front, wells frequently punch through the alluvium into fractured crystalline bedrock, where flow depends on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole happens to intersect. Those bedrock wells are more likely to be genuinely low-yield from the start, which is why a storage-and-booster approach so often fits foothill Redlands properties better than chasing a bigger pump.

Recharge patterns matter too. These basins refill from Santa Ana River flows, imported water spreading, and runoff off the San Bernardino Mountains. After a strong winter you may notice your well perks up; after a dry one it sags. That seasonality is normal, but if each year's low point is worse than the last, it signals a long-term decline worth measuring with a proper yield test rather than simply living with. Understanding where your property sits in this picture is exactly what lets us recommend a fix that lasts instead of a stopgap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why has my Redlands well slowly lost flow over the years?

Two things usually combine: long-term drawdown of the Santa Ana basin water table and gradual fouling of the well screen with hard-water scale and iron bacteria. A yield test plus a screen inspection tells us which is dominant.

Could the problem just be my pump instead of the well?

Often, yes. Worn impellers and a pump set too high for today's water level both reduce flow while the aquifer itself is fine. We measure water levels and drawdown so we can tell the pump from the well before recommending anything.

Does hard water really clog a well?

It does. Redlands' mineral-rich groundwater deposits scale on the screen and casing, and iron bacteria add a biofilm that plugs the openings. Rehabilitation with brushing and acid treatment reopens them.

How much water does my household need?

A single-family home generally needs 3–5 GPM; groves and larger properties need more. Many wells in the Redlands foothills produce less than that but run perfectly well with a storage tank and booster system.

Is deepening cheaper than a new well?

Usually, if your existing casing is in good shape and there's a productive zone below. When the casing is collapsing or silted, a new well can be the better long-term value. We decide based on your well's test results.
