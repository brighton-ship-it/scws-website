Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

Citrus Grove Well Services in Moreno Valley, Riverside County

Moreno Valley sits in the western reach of Riverside County, where the broad inland basin between the Box Springs Mountains and the lifting San Jacinto foothills has carried an agricultural identity for well over a century. Long before the area filled with rooftops and the runways of March Air Reserve Base, this was orchard and grove country, and citrus has always had a quiet but durable place in that story. Today a mix of legacy commercial blocks, hobby groves, and rural-residential parcels on the city's eastern and southern fringes still depends on private wells to keep navel oranges, Valencias, lemons, and mandarins productive. At Southern California Well Service, a licensed C-57 water well contractor with local experience since 2020 and a 4.9-star reputation, we help Moreno Valley growers drill, equip, and maintain the agricultural wells that make citrus farming possible in this dry, sun-drenched corner of the Inland Empire.

Citrus is an unforgiving crop when it comes to water. Trees that go short during the summer heat or during the critical spring bloom and fruit-set window will drop fruit, lose size, and weaken for the following season. Because Moreno Valley's groves rarely have access to dependable surface deliveries, the well in the corner of the property is usually the entire irrigation supply. Getting it sized correctly, equipped properly, and kept running is not optional.

How Much Water a Moreno Valley Citrus Grove Really Needs

Mature citrus in the hot interior of Riverside County is a thirsty crop. A reasonable planning figure for a healthy bearing grove is roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the high end of that range applying during long, hot, low-humidity summers like those Moreno Valley regularly experiences. An acre-foot is about 326,000 gallons, so even a modest ten-acre block can consume in the neighborhood of 13 to 14 million gallons across a single growing year.

Translated into pumping terms, a grove needs enough sustained well yield to refill that demand during the irrigation season without running the trees short. Many small-to-mid citrus operations are served by wells producing anywhere from 50 to several hundred gallons per minute (GPM), depending on acreage and how irrigation is scheduled. The exact figure depends on your grove size, your irrigation method, and how much storage you have, which is why we always start with a site-specific assessment rather than a generic rule.

Frost Protection Changes the Math

What surprises many new growers is how dramatically frost protection raises peak water demand. When a cold night threatens the groves on the valley floor, sprinkler-based frost protection can call for on the order of 50 or more GPM per acre, delivered continuously through the coldest hours. That is an enormous instantaneous draw compared with routine summer irrigation. A well and storage system sized only for average summer use will not protect a grove on a freeze night. We design pumping and storage so the peak frost event, not just the average day, is covered.

Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage Together

A productive citrus irrigation system is really three coordinated parts: the well itself, the pump, and on-site storage.

  • Well yield: The borehole must reliably deliver the sustained flow your acreage demands without excessive drawdown. We evaluate static water level, drawdown under pumping, and recovery before recommending equipment.
  • High-capacity pumps: Agricultural citrus wells typically use a high-capacity submersible pump or, for some deeper or higher-volume installations, a line-shaft turbine pump. These move far more water than a residential pump and are matched precisely to the well's yield and the pressure your irrigation design requires.
  • Storage ponds and tanks: Because peak demand, especially frost demand, can exceed what the well produces minute-to-minute, storage is the great equalizer. A lined pond or a bank of tanks lets a steady well fill a reservoir around the clock, while pumps draw on that reservoir to meet short, intense peaks. Storage also gives you a buffer if the pump needs service mid-season.

Matching these three elements is where experience pays off. An oversized pump on a modest well will pull the water level down and cycle hard; an undersized system will leave trees stressed in August. We size the whole package to your grove.

Water Quality Matters for Citrus

Citrus is among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, and Inland Empire groundwater can carry dissolved minerals that quietly damage a grove over years. The three constituents we watch most closely are salinity, chloride, and boron.

  • Salinity (EC): High total salts in irrigation water reduce a tree's ability to take up moisture and gradually depress yield. Citrus tolerates only modest salinity, so we recommend testing electrical conductivity (EC) before committing a grove to a given water source.
  • Chloride: Citrus is notably sensitive to chloride, which accumulates in the leaves and produces the classic tip and margin leaf burn growers dread. Rootstock choice matters here, but so does keeping chloride in the irrigation water within reason.
  • Boron: Boron is a trace nutrient that becomes toxic to citrus at surprisingly low concentrations, often cited around the 0.5 to 1 part-per-million (ppm) range. Because the safe window is narrow, boron is one of the first things a thorough water test should flag.

We can pull a sample, interpret the lab results in the context of citrus tolerances, and recommend blending, treatment, or rootstock-aware strategies if your numbers run high.

Drought and Groundwater in the Region

Southern California's recurring droughts put real pressure on inland groundwater, and Moreno Valley is no exception. Falling water tables during dry cycles can reduce well yield, lower pumping water levels, and in older or shallower wells lead to declining performance. California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) has also brought new attention to long-term basin health across Riverside County, so growers increasingly think about efficiency and stewardship alongside raw capacity. Drip and micro-sprinkler irrigation, soil-moisture monitoring, and right-sized pumping all help a grove get more crop per gallon, which matters both economically and for the basin.

Common Citrus Well Issues We See

  • Declining yield over time as a well ages, the screen fouls, or mineral encrustation builds up on the casing and pump.
  • Pump wear and failure from sediment, sand, or simply the heavy duty cycle of irrigation-season pumping.
  • Loss of pressure or flow to drip and micro-sprinkler systems, often traced to clogged emitters, failing filtration, or a pump no longer matched to the well.
  • Water quality drift as salinity or chloride creeps upward during prolonged dry periods.
  • Frost-night shortfalls when an existing system simply cannot deliver the peak flow needed to protect trees.

Maintenance That Protects Your Grove

Citrus wells reward routine attention. We recommend periodic well performance testing to track yield and drawdown, scheduled pump and motor inspections, monitoring of pressure and flow at the irrigation manifold, and clean, well-maintained filtration to keep drip emitters from plugging. Annual or seasonal water testing catches creeping salinity or chloride before it shows up as crop damage. Catching a worn pump or a fouling screen early is far cheaper than an emergency failure during a July heat wave or a January freeze.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many gallons per minute does a Moreno Valley citrus grove well need?

It depends on acreage and irrigation method, but many small-to-mid groves run on wells producing 50 to several hundred GPM. Frost protection can push instantaneous demand to 50 or more GPM per acre, so we size the well and storage to the peak event, not just the summer average.

How much water does mature citrus use per year?

Plan on roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year for healthy bearing citrus in this hot inland climate, with the high end during long, dry summers.

Why does my well need storage if it already pumps enough in summer?

Peak demands, especially frost protection, can far exceed steady well output. A pond or tank bank lets a steady well fill a reservoir around the clock while pumps draw on that storage to meet short, intense peaks.

What water quality problems hurt citrus most?

Salinity, chloride, and boron. Citrus is salt-sensitive, chloride causes leaf burn, and boron turns toxic at low levels (often cited around 0.5 to 1 ppm). A water test catches these before they damage the grove.

How much does a new agricultural citrus well cost?

A turnkey agricultural well typically runs about $25,000 to $60,000 or more depending on depth and equipment. A high-capacity pump is roughly $3,000 to $8,000, and hydrofracturing to boost a marginal well is about $3,000 to $8,000.

Do you serve backyard citrus as well as commercial groves?

Yes. We handle everything from small rural-residential plantings to full commercial blocks across Moreno Valley and Riverside County.
