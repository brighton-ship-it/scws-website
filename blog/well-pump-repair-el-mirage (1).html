Diagnosing a Failed Desert Pump

The pumps serving El Mirage are overwhelmingly deep-set submersible units, because the water table generally sits well beyond the reach of a suction-style jet pump. A submersible hangs at the bottom of the well and pushes water up through the drop pipe, driven by a sealed motor that starts with help from a control box or capacitor at the surface. A smaller number of shallower properties near the lakebed drainages may use a jet pump, which mounts above ground and either draws water by suction or through a downhole ejector.

Knowing which system you have shapes the diagnosis. On a submersible we test the motor windings for resistance and insulation to ground, measure the amperage the pump draws, and check the control box, capacitor, pressure switch, and the wire splices at the wellhead. A motor that reads open or shorted is done; a control box or capacitor that has failed can often be swapped at the surface without pulling anything. On a jet pump we look instead at prime, the impeller, the foot valve, and the pressure control. The single most useful step is to rule out the simple, above-ground causes before committing to a costly pull, because in the desert a full pump-out is a significant job.

Warning Signs El Mirage Wells Show

Desert wells tend to announce their troubles in familiar ways. Here are the symptoms we hear about most from El Mirage callers, and what each usually means.

  • No water and a tripped breaker. If the pump breaker trips and will not stay reset, stop resetting it. A breaker that pops the moment the pump starts is signaling a shorted motor, a failed capacitor, or a control-box fault, and repeated resets risk damage at the panel.
  • Low pressure that keeps dropping. Falling flow over days or weeks often means a worn impeller stack, or a water level in the well that has dropped below normal during a dry stretch, which is a real concern in the Mojave. It can also be a waterlogged pressure tank.
  • Short-cycling. A pump that clicks on and off rapidly is usually fighting a failed pressure tank bladder or a bad pressure switch. In the desert heat, that constant restarting is especially hard on a motor and will shorten its life quickly.
  • Air spitting from the taps. Sputtering, air-filled water frequently points to a dropped water level uncovering the pump intake, or air entering through a crack in the drop pipe. Given El Mirage's variable water table, a declining level is a common culprit.
  • Sandy or silty water. Fine desert sediment is abrasive. Grit in your water can mean the well is producing sand, the pump is set too low, or the screen is failing, and all of it wears out impellers and seals faster.
  • A climbing electric bill. A pump that runs longer and harder than it used to, without more water to show for it, is losing efficiency and heading toward failure.

The Pull-and-Inspect Process

When the trouble is genuinely downhole, we bring a well-service truck to pull the pump. The rig lifts the entire string — pump, motor, drop pipe, safety rope, and wire — up out of the casing so we can inspect it in daylight. At El Mirage's average of around 207 feet, that is a substantial length of pipe to handle, which is exactly why this is not a do-it-yourself job. We look for burnt or discolored motor windings, sand scoring on the pump end, a failed check valve, corroded splices, and any wear on the pipe or wire.

From there the decision is straightforward. If the motor is good and only a check valve, a length of wire, or a worn pump end is the issue, we rebuild and reset. If the motor is burnt out or the pump is worn from years of pushing sandy Mojave water, we install a new unit while the well is already open, which saves you the cost of a second pull down the road. We use quality pumps from Franklin Electric, Grundfos, Goulds (Xylem), and Sta-Rite (Pentair), and we test the full system before we leave.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my El Mirage well pump needs repair?

Watch for no water or low pressure, rapid on-off short-cycling, unusual noises, dirty or sandy water, and higher-than-normal electric bills. Any one of these is worth a professional inspection, and in the desert catching a problem early can prevent a total loss of water.

Why are pump repairs sometimes more expensive out here?

El Mirage wells average around 207 feet and some go much deeper, so a downhole repair means pulling more drop pipe and handling heavier equipment than a shallow well requires. Surface repairs like a switch or capacitor, though, cost the same here as anywhere.

Can a dropping water table cause my pump to fail?

It can. If the water level in the well falls below the pump intake during a dry period, you will see air spitting and lost pressure even though the pump itself is fine. Sometimes the fix is lowering the pump; sometimes it points to a deeper well issue. We test for this during diagnosis.

Can I repair the pump myself?

Surface tasks like resetting a breaker or adjusting a pressure switch are reasonable for a homeowner. Anything that involves pulling the pump from the well requires specialized equipment and should be handled by a licensed contractor to avoid dropping the pump or damaging the casing.
