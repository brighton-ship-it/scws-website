What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Clairemont water.

Rusty Water in Clairemont: What Causes It in a Classic San Diego Suburb

Clairemont is one of San Diego's original postwar suburbs, a large set of neighborhoods, Clairemont Mesa, Bay Park, Bay Ho, North Clairemont, spread across the mesas of central San Diego in San Diego County. Built out rapidly in the 1950s and early 1960s, Clairemont is defined by single-story ranch homes from that era, almost all of them connected to the City of San Diego's municipal water supply. Private wells inside Clairemont are practically unheard of. So when residents see rusty, orange, or brown water, the cause is rarely the city's water and almost always the home's own plumbing, specifically the galvanized-steel pipe that was standard when these houses were built. This guide explains that common scenario and also covers well-sourced iron for those who own rural property elsewhere.

The Galvanized Pipe Connection

Clairemont's defining housing boom happened in the 1950s, precisely the period when galvanized-steel pipe was the go-to material for residential water lines. Galvanized pipe is steel with a protective zinc coating, and that coating wears away over time. Once it is gone, after fifty, sixty, or seventy years of service, the bare steel rusts from the inside out. The rust builds up on the interior walls and intermittently flakes off, traveling to faucets as orange particles that discolor the water. Because so many original Clairemont homes still have at least some of their factory plumbing, this is by far the most common reason for rusty water here. The classic giveaway is rust that is worst first thing in the morning, after water sits overnight, and that clears after running the tap briefly.

Rust and Falling Water Pressure

The same corrosion that colors the water also restricts it. As rust and scale coat the inside of a galvanized pipe, the open diameter narrows, sometimes to a fraction of the original. The result is weak showers, slow-filling sinks and tubs, and noticeably lower pressure throughout the house. Many Clairemont homeowners experience the discoloration and the pressure decline together, and both trace back to the same aging pipes. A filter may catch some of the rust, but it does nothing for the pressure or the underlying corrosion, which is why repiping is the real solution for older homes.

How Iron Shows Up in Well Water

If you own a rural or East County property served by a private well, your rusty water has a different origin. Groundwater picks up naturally occurring iron as it travels through soil and fractured rock, carrying it invisibly in dissolved form until it reaches air at the tap and oxidizes into rust. Wells can also develop iron bacteria, organisms that feed on iron and leave a slimy reddish film and a musty odor. Well-sourced iron is treated by oxidation and filtration, while iron bacteria require shock chlorination of the well plus filtration. These are well-service remedies, separate from the household repiping that resolves a Clairemont plumbing issue.

What to Check First

  • Run cold water at several faucets to see whether the rust is whole-house or limited to certain fixtures.
  • Compare hot and cold; rust only on the hot side usually points to the water heater and its anode rod.
  • Note the timing; morning-heavy rust that clears with running is the signature of galvanized corrosion.
  • Factor in the home's age; an original 1950s Clairemont house almost certainly had galvanized plumbing.
  • On a private well, check the toilet tank for orange slime and note any musty smell, signs of iron bacteria.

Why a Mid-Century Suburb Sees This So Often

Clairemont's history makes it a textbook case for galvanized-pipe trouble. The neighborhood went from open mesa to tens of thousands of homes in barely a decade, all built to the same era's standards with the same materials. That means a huge number of houses crossed the half-century mark at roughly the same time, and galvanized pipe tends to fail on a predictable timeline. Homes that were repiped during a remodel in the 1990s or 2000s usually run clear, while those still on original plumbing increasingly show rust and pressure loss. Knowing your home's renovation history is one of the quickest ways to predict whether galvanized corrosion is your culprit, and it helps us focus the diagnosis the moment we arrive.

Protecting Your Fixtures and Appliances

Letting rusty water continue costs more than the nuisance suggests. Iron particles etch orange stains into sinks, tubs, and toilets, ruin white laundry, and collect as sediment in water heaters, shortening their life and efficiency. Dishwashers and washing machines develop clogged inlet screens and valves, and faucet aerators need constant cleaning. Each of these is a downstream expense driven by the same upstream corrosion. Addressing the root cause, whether that is repiping or, on a well, proper iron treatment, protects the appliances and finishes you have already paid for and spares you a cascade of smaller repairs over the coming years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there wells in Clairemont?

No. Clairemont is fully on City of San Diego municipal water, so rusty water here is almost always caused by corroding galvanized plumbing in these classic 1950s homes, not by the source water.

Why does my Clairemont faucet run orange in the morning?

That timing is the classic sign of galvanized pipe corrosion. Water standing overnight picks up rust from the pipe walls, then clears once you run the tap for a minute.

Is the rusty water safe?

Iron is not a recognized health hazard at typical levels, but it tastes metallic and stains fixtures and laundry. The larger issue is the corrosion's effect on your plumbing and water pressure.

Will a whole-house filter solve it?

A sediment filter reduces visible rust but does not stop the pipes from corroding or restore lost pressure. For galvanized plumbing, repiping with PEX or copper is the lasting fix.

Could my low water pressure be related to the rust?

Almost certainly. The same internal corrosion that releases rust narrows the pipes and cuts pressure. Repiping corrects both at the same time.
