Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

While urban development has reshaped much of the landscape, working citrus blocks, hobby groves, and family orchards still dot Rialto and the surrounding communities of Bloomington, Fontana, and Colton. For these growers, a dependable agricultural well is not a convenience — it is the difference between a healthy, productive grove and trees stressed into decline. Southern California Well Service is a licensed C-57 water well drilling contractor with more than 30 years of experience serving San Bernardino County, and we design, drill, equip, and maintain the water systems Rialto citrus depends on.

How Much Water Citrus Trees Really Need

Citrus is a thirsty, evapotranspiration-driven crop, and inland Rialto's hot, dry summers push demand higher than coastal districts. A mature commercial grove in this part of the Inland Empire typically consumes roughly 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the bulk of that applied between May and October. Translated into pumping terms, sustaining even a modest block requires a well and pump capable of delivering steady gallons-per-minute (GPM) flow through the peak season without drawing the water level down faster than the aquifer can recharge.

Sizing for Peak and Frost Demand

Two demand spikes drive well and pump sizing for citrus. The first is mid-summer peak evapotranspiration, when trees pull the most water. The second — and the one growers most often underestimate — is frost protection. When a hard freeze threatens the San Bernardino Valley, growers may run overhead or under-tree sprinklers continuously through the coldest hours, and water-based frost protection can demand 50 or more GPM per acre for the duration of the event. A well sized only for routine irrigation will starve a grove exactly when the crop is most vulnerable, which is why we size systems around the worst-case night, not the average day.

Designing and Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage System

A citrus water system is more than a hole in the ground. We engineer the complete package so supply matches demand at every point in the season.

Well Yield and Pump Selection

The starting point is well yield — how many gallons per minute the aquifer will reliably give up. Once we confirm sustainable yield through testing, we match it to the right lifting equipment. For deeper agricultural wells we typically install high-capacity submersible pumps; for shallower or high-volume applications, a vertical lineshaft turbine pump may be the better choice. Correct sizing protects both the crop and the pump: an oversized pump that outruns the well will cycle, cavitate, and burn out, while an undersized pump leaves the grove short during peak demand.

Storage Ponds and Tanks Bridge the Gap

When peak or frost-protection demand exceeds what a well can deliver instantaneously, storage closes the gap. We design systems that pump steadily into lined ponds or large tanks during off-peak hours, then draw on that reserve to meet short, intense demand spikes. Storage lets a grower run a smaller, more efficient well around the clock instead of paying for an oversized well that sits idle most of the year, and it provides a critical buffer during a freeze night or a pump failure.

Water Quality: The Hidden Threat to Citrus

Citrus is notably salt-sensitive, and water chemistry matters as much as water quantity. Before we recommend a system, we test for the parameters that damage citrus:

  • Salinity (EC): Elevated total salinity reduces water uptake and yield. Citrus tolerates less salt than many crops, so even moderate EC warrants attention and a leaching strategy.
  • Chloride: Citrus is sensitive to chloride, which accumulates in leaf tissue and produces the classic leaf-tip and margin burn that signals trouble. Rootstock choice and irrigation method both influence how much chloride a grove can tolerate.
  • Boron: Boron is an essential micronutrient at trace levels but becomes toxic to citrus above roughly 0.5 to 1 part per million. Boron problems are easy to miss because the early symptoms mimic other stresses, so testing is the only reliable way to catch them.

If your well water carries high salinity, chloride, or boron, we can recommend blending, leaching practices, rootstock-aware irrigation scheduling, or treatment so your trees are not slowly poisoned by the very water meant to sustain them.

Groundwater and Drought Conditions Around Rialto

Rialto draws on groundwater within the broader San Bernardino-area basins, which fall under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) framework. For growers, that means long-term planning matters: pumping practices, water-level monitoring, and efficient irrigation are increasingly part of responsible grove management. After successive dry years, water tables in parts of the Inland Empire have come under pressure, and a well that performed adequately a decade ago may now need deepening, a lowered pump setting, or rehabilitation to keep up. We help growers understand where their well stands and plan ahead rather than react to a dry hole mid-season.

Common Issues, Maintenance, and When to Call a Pro

Common Problems We See

  • Declining yield and longer pump run times as the aquifer draws down through summer
  • Mineral scale and biofouling that clog drip emitters and micro-sprinklers
  • Pump motor failures from cycling, sediment, or electrical fluctuations
  • Pressure tank and control problems that interrupt irrigation scheduling
  • Casing or screen deterioration in older agricultural wells reducing yield

Maintenance That Pays for Itself

Routine maintenance is far cheaper than an emergency during a heat wave or a freeze. We recommend periodic well and pump inspections, water-level checks, flow testing, water-quality retesting every year or two, and filtration upkeep to protect your emitters. When yield drops, hydrofracturing or well rehabilitation can often restore production at a fraction of the cost of a new well.

When to Call Southern California Well Service

Call a professional when you notice falling pressure or yield, air spitting from the system, sudden water-quality changes, rising power bills from a struggling pump, or any sign your grove is not getting the water it needs. Citrus is unforgiving of interruptions during peak demand, and early diagnosis prevents crop loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a citrus grove in Rialto need each year?

A mature grove in inland San Bernardino County typically uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with the heaviest demand from late spring through early fall. Frost-protection nights can briefly require 50 or more GPM per acre on top of normal irrigation.

Why is my citrus showing leaf-tip burn even though I water regularly?

Leaf-tip and margin burn is a classic sign of chloride or salinity stress, not necessarily drought. Citrus is salt-sensitive, so we recommend testing your well water for EC, chloride, and boron to identify the cause before it reduces your yield.

Do I need water storage for my grove?

Storage ponds or tanks let you run a smaller, efficient well steadily and draw on a reserve during peak summer demand or a frost-protection event. It is often more economical than drilling an oversized well, and it provides backup if a pump fails.

How much does a new agricultural well cost in Rialto?

A turnkey agricultural well generally runs $25,000 to $60,000 or more depending on depth, casing, and required capacity. We provide a clear written estimate after evaluating your site and water needs.

Can a low-yielding well be improved without drilling a new one?

Often, yes. Hydrofracturing, well rehabilitation, or adjusting the pump setting can restore production in many older wells for $3,000 to $8,000 — far less than a replacement. Our diagnostic visit ($125, credited toward the work) tells us which approach fits.

Is groundwater regulated in the Rialto area?

Yes. Rialto-area groundwater falls under California's SGMA framework, which encourages monitoring and efficient use. We help growers plan sustainable pumping and stay ahead of changing water-table conditions.
