Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

How Much Water Citrus Trees Need in Sun City

Mature citrus is a thirsty, year-round crop. In our warm inland climate, a producing grove typically uses on the order of 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre per year, with peak demand landing in the hot, dry months from roughly June through September. An acre-foot is about 326,000 gallons, so even a modest 5-acre Sun City grove can draw well over a million gallons in a single peak season.

Translating Demand Into Well Yield

To keep up during peak evapotranspiration, growers generally want a well and pump capable of delivering enough flow to refill storage and run irrigation sets without falling behind. As a rough planning figure, many citrus operations target somewhere in the range of 15 to 25 gallons per minute (GPM) per acre of sustained capacity, though the right number depends on your soils, irrigation method, and how much storage you carry. Drip and micro-sprinkler systems stretch every gallon further than flood irrigation, which matters a great deal where groundwater is a shared, regulated resource.

Frost Protection Changes the Math

Citrus is frost-sensitive, and Perris Valley does see cold snaps. Growers who use sprinklers for freeze protection need a very different flow profile: overhead frost protection can demand 50 or more GPM per acre for several hours at a time during a hard freeze, all at once and often in the pre-dawn hours. A well sized only for routine summer irrigation will not cover that surge, which is why frost-protected groves lean heavily on storage rather than asking the well to deliver everything live.

Sizing the Well, Pump, and Storage for a Citrus Grove

A productive citrus water system is really three coordinated parts: the well itself, the pump, and storage.

The Well

An agricultural well for citrus is built for volume and longevity. Proper casing, screen placement, and gravel pack matched to the local formation help a well produce reliably and resist sand and collapse over decades of heavy seasonal pumping.

The Pump

Most high-capacity citrus wells run either a submersible pump set deep in the borehole or a vertical lineshaft turbine driven from the surface. Both can move large volumes against significant lift. Correct sizing matters: an oversized pump can overdraw and damage a well, while an undersized one leaves trees short during peak heat. We match pump curve, motor, and column to your well's tested capacity.

Storage Ponds and Tanks

Storage is the secret weapon of a well-run grove. By pumping into a lined pond or steel/poly tanks during off-peak hours and then irrigating from storage, you let a modest well serve a larger grove, ride out short outages, and meet that enormous frost-protection surge without an oversized well. Storage also buffers the system against power interruptions, which are not unheard of on rural Sun City circuits.

Water Quality for Citrus: Salinity, Chloride, and Boron

Citrus is one of the more salt-sensitive tree crops, so water quality is just as important as quantity. Before you build or rehabilitate a grove, the well water should be tested.

  • Salinity (EC): Citrus tolerates only modest salinity before yield and growth decline. Elevated total dissolved solids stress roots and reduce production over time.
  • Chloride: Citrus is sensitive to chloride, which accumulates in leaves and causes the classic tip and margin leaf burn. Rootstock choice influences tolerance, but high-chloride water is a long-term liability.
  • Boron: A trace nutrient that turns toxic quickly. Citrus can show injury when boron climbs much above roughly 0.5 to 1 part per million, so even modest concentrations deserve attention.

If a water test flags any of these, options range from blending sources and leaching salts with good irrigation management to treatment and rootstock selection. We can test your Sun City well water and explain what the numbers mean for your trees.

Drought, Groundwater, and SGMA in the Perris Valley

Sun City draws on the broader San Jacinto Groundwater Basin, the alluvial basin that underlies the San Jacinto, Perris, Moreno, and Menifee Valleys in western Riverside County. The basin is managed under California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA); it is classified as a high-priority basin, and a Groundwater Sustainability Plan covering the area was developed and is being implemented, with Eastern Municipal Water District serving as the groundwater sustainability agency for much of it. Portions of the wider basin are also adjudicated under the Hemet-San Jacinto Watermaster.

For citrus growers, the practical takeaway is that groundwater is a shared, regulated resource. Efficient irrigation, accurate metering, and well-maintained equipment are not just good economics; they help you stay in step with long-term basin management. During drought years, static water levels can drop and pumps that were set too shallow may begin to break suction or pull air, so periodic review of your well's performance is wise.

Common Citrus Well Problems We See Near Sun City

  • Declining yield or pump cavitation as seasonal water levels fall, especially late summer and during drought.
  • Mineral scaling and encrustation on screens and pump components in mineral-rich groundwater, which chokes flow over time.
  • Clogged drip emitters and micro-sprinklers from sediment, iron, or biological fouling when filtration is inadequate.
  • Worn or undersized pumps that cannot meet peak summer demand or frost-protection surges.
  • Pressure-tank and control problems that cause short-cycling and premature equipment wear.

Maintenance That Keeps a Grove Watered

Citrus wells earn their keep with preventive care. We recommend periodic flow and water-level testing to catch declining capacity early, routine inspection of the pump, motor, and electrical controls, and keeping filters and emitters clean so the whole irrigation system delivers what the trees need. When a well loses production to mineral buildup or fine sediment, well rehabilitation and hydrofracturing can often restore yield at a fraction of the cost of drilling new. Periodic water testing also lets you track salinity and chloride trends before they harm the grove.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big a well do I need for a citrus grove in Sun City?

It depends on acreage, soils, irrigation method, and whether you need frost protection. As a planning rule, many growers target enough sustained capacity to cover peak summer use plus storage. We test your well and size the pump and storage to match your grove rather than relying on a generic number.

Why are my citrus leaves showing burned tips and margins?

Classic tip and margin leaf burn often points to chloride or general salinity in the irrigation water, to which citrus is especially sensitive. A water test will confirm it, and we can discuss leaching, blending, treatment, or rootstock options.

Is boron really a problem at low levels?

Yes. Citrus can show boron toxicity when concentrations rise much above roughly 0.5 to 1 ppm, so even modest amounts in well water deserve testing and a management plan.

Do I need a separate setup for frost protection?

If you use overhead sprinklers for freeze protection, you'll need a much higher instantaneous flow than routine irrigation requires, often 50 or more GPM per acre. Most growers meet that with on-site storage rather than oversizing the well itself.

How does SGMA affect my well in the Perris Valley?

The San Jacinto Groundwater Basin underlying the Menifee and Perris Valleys is a high-priority basin managed under SGMA with an adopted sustainability plan. In practice that means efficient irrigation, metering, and well maintenance help you use a shared resource responsibly while keeping your grove productive.

Can a low-yield citrus well be improved without redrilling?

Often yes. Hydrofracturing and well rehabilitation can restore lost capacity in many wells for far less than a new well. We start with a diagnostic to see what's realistic.
