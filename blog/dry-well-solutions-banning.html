Why Wells Go Dry

  • Drought Conditions: Extended dry periods lower the water table
  • Increased Demand: More wells in the area drawing from the same aquifer
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: Water table naturally drops in summer/fall
  • Pump Position: Pump may be positioned above current water level
  • Well Age: Mineral buildup reducing water entry points
  • Aquifer Depletion: Long-term overdrawing of groundwater

Solutions for Dry Wells in Banning

1. Well Deepening

If the water table has dropped, deepening your existing well may reach water again. This is often less expensive than drilling a new well.

2. Hydrofracturing

High-pressure water treatment can open new fractures in bedrock, allowing more water to flow into your well.

3. Pump Lowering

Sometimes simply lowering your pump deeper in the well can restore water production.

4. New Well Drilling

When rehabilitation isn't viable, we can drill a new well in a better location on your property.

Signs Your Banning Well Is Going Dry

A well almost never quits without warning. The earlier you recognize the signs, the cheaper the fix tends to be. Watch for these in your home:

  • Sputtering or spitting faucets — air in the lines is a classic sign the pump is sucking air as the water level drops to the intake.
  • Pressure that fades after heavy use — the well keeps up for a shower but falls behind when you irrigate or run the washer, then recovers overnight.
  • Cloudy, sandy, or gritty water — a sign the pump is drawing from near the bottom and pulling in sediment.
  • The pump running far longer, or short-cycling on and off — both point to a pump struggling against a falling level.
  • Worse symptoms in late summer and fall — a strong seasonal pattern usually means the regional water table, not the equipment, is the culprit.

If you are seeing two or more of these around Banning, call us before the well goes completely dry — catching it early keeps your options open and your costs down.

Why Wells Go Dry Around Banning

In Riverside County, a handful of causes account for nearly every dry-well call we take in the Banning area. Usually it is a combination of several.

Drought and a Dropping Water Table

The single biggest factor is the pass area's dry cycles and pressure on the San Timoteo-area groundwater. When the level in the San Gorgonio Pass alluvial basin falls below your pump intake — or below the fractures and sand layers that feed your well — production drops even though the well itself is sound. Wells in the pass area commonly run 300 to 700 feet through layered alluvium.

Over-Pumping and Demand Exceeding Recharge

When more water is drawn from an aquifer than nature puts back, levels fall over time. Growth, irrigation, and neighboring wells all draw on the same shared water, and in dry years recharge cannot keep pace. Heavy demand during a hot summer can also outrun a single well faster than the aquifer can refill it day to day.

A Well Drilled Too Shallow

Some older wells around Banning were completed only as deep as they needed to be when water was higher. As the long-term table has declined, those shallower wells are the first to feel it — they were never drilled to reach today's water level.

Seasonal Decline

Water levels naturally rise in winter and spring and fall through summer and autumn. A well that is marginal will fail during that seasonal low and then “miraculously” recover after winter rains — a strong clue that the table, not the hardware, is the issue.

Sediment, Encrustation, and a Clogging Screen

Over years, mineral scale, iron bacteria, and fine sediment build up on a well's screen and in the surrounding gravel pack, choking off the paths water uses to enter. The aquifer may be fine while the well's inflow has quietly been strangled. This is common in Riverside County's mineral-rich water and is very often repairable.

How We Diagnose a Dry Well in Banning

Guessing is expensive. Before recommending anything, our technicians run a straightforward diagnostic on your Banning well to find out what is really going on:

  • Static water level — we measure how high the water stands in the well at rest. This tells us where the table actually sits today.
  • Pumping (drawdown) level — we run the pump and watch how far the level falls. A large drawdown points to limited inflow; a stable level points to a pump or table issue.
  • Recovery rate — we shut the pump off and time how quickly the well refills. Slow recovery usually means a clogged screen or a struggling aquifer.
  • Yield test — we measure sustainable gallons per minute so we can size any solution to your household's real demand.

Our diagnostic visit is $125, and we credit it toward any work you authorize. You walk away knowing whether you are facing a $1,000 fix or something larger — no scare tactics, no upselling.

Solutions for a Dry Banning Well — From Least to Most Expensive

We always start with the cheapest fix that will actually solve your problem. Here is the full ladder, in order of cost.

1. Lower the Pump or Adjust the Setting — $800 to $2,000

If the water table has dropped but there is still water below your pump, simply lowering the pump and adjusting its setting often restores full service. It is the first thing we check, and for many Banning wells it is the whole answer.

2. Add a Low-Yield Flow Controller with a Storage Tank and Booster — $3,000 to $8,000

If your well makes water steadily but slowly, we can install a flow-control system that meters the well's modest output into a storage tank or cistern, then uses a booster pump to deliver strong household pressure on demand. This lets a low-yield well comfortably serve a home it otherwise could not keep up with — a popular, cost-effective fix in Riverside County's hard-rock and desert areas alike.

3. Well Rehabilitation — $1,500 to $6,000

When sediment, scale, or iron bacteria have clogged the screen, rehabilitation can restore much of the well's lost yield. We use mechanical brushing, surging, and chemical treatments to clean the screen and the surrounding formation, reopening the paths water uses to enter. For a well whose aquifer is fine but whose inflow has been choked, this is often the best value on the list.

4. Hydrofracturing — $3,000 to $8,000

In the fractured bedrock common around inland Riverside County, hydrofracturing injects water under high pressure to open and connect existing fractures, creating new pathways for water to reach the borehole. It can meaningfully boost a low-yield hard-rock well and is far cheaper than drilling new.

5. Deepen the Existing Well, or Drill a New, Deeper Well — $15,000 to $45,000+

When the table has fallen below the bottom of your well, deepening the existing borehole may reach water again. If the well cannot be deepened safely or the geology calls for it, a new, deeper well in a better-sited location is the final step. It is the largest investment — but for many Banning properties it is a long-term solution that adds real value, and we offer flexible financing for major work.

Preventing Future Dry-Well Problems

Once your water is restored, a little maintenance keeps it that way. Around Banning we recommend:

  • Installing a simple water-level monitor so you can see decline coming instead of being surprised by it.
  • Adding a storage tank or cistern as a buffer for peak demand and short outages.
  • Spreading out heavy irrigation rather than running the well hard all at once.
  • Scheduling an annual well inspection and a yield check, especially heading into late summer.
  • Addressing declining yield early, while a cleaning or a pump adjustment will still do the job.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Banning well gone for good if it stops producing water?

Almost never. Most “dry” wells are suffering from a dropped pump setting, a clogged screen, or a seasonally low table — all fixable. A diagnostic tells us which, and only a small share of wells truly need replacement.

How much does it cost to fix a dry well near Banning?

It depends on the cause. Lowering the pump runs about $800 to $2,000; a storage-tank and booster system $3,000 to $8,000; rehabilitation $1,500 to $6,000; hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000; and deepening or drilling new $15,000 and up. Our $125 diagnostic (credited toward the work) pins down which you need.

Why does my well go dry only in late summer?

That seasonal pattern almost always points to the water table, which falls through summer and fall and recovers after winter rains. A marginal well fails at the seasonal low. Lowering the pump, adding storage, or improving yield usually solves it.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Banning well?

It works best in the fractured bedrock common across inland Riverside County. If your well is in hard rock and producing low yield, hydrofracturing is often a strong, affordable option. We confirm with a yield test before recommending it.

Can you provide water while my well is being repaired?

Yes. We offer temporary water solutions and, where possible, same-day or next-day assessment so your Banning household is not left without water during the repair.
