What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Pacific Beach Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Pacific Beach

In Pacific Beach, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Pacific Beach wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in Pacific Beach: What's Realistic

Pacific Beach — PB — stretches between the ocean and Mission Bay as one of San Diego's busiest coastal neighborhoods, part of the City of San Diego in San Diego County. Like its beachfront neighbors, it runs entirely on municipal water and has no private bedrock wells. If you found this page researching hydrofracturing, the honest answer is that the technique does not apply to property inside Pacific Beach. It matters only if you own a well on an inland or mountain property elsewhere in the county. Here is the full picture.

Why Pacific Beach Geology Rules It Out

Hydrofracturing works only in fractured bedrock, opening the rock's natural fissures with high-pressure water. Pacific Beach has none of the conditions it needs:

  • Sandy terrace and bay sediments. The ground is coastal terrace sand and bay-margin deposits — loose sediment, not solid rock that can be fractured.
  • Brackish shallow water. Near the coast and the bay, shallow groundwater is salty and tidally influenced.
  • Municipal supply. Every property is on city water; private wells simply are not used here.

Rather than chase a treatment that cannot succeed, we tell PB owners exactly where things stand.

How Hydrofracturing Works on a True Candidate

On a genuine bedrock well the method is clean and simple. We lower an inflatable packer and seal off a section of the borehole, then inject clean water above the seal at high pressure — commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The water pushes into the rock's natural hairline fractures, clears out the silt and mineral scale plugging them, and widens the connected network so groundwater reaches the well more freely. The job uses only water, takes four to eight hours, and on a good candidate can multiply yield several times over.

Where the Good Candidates Are

The wells that respond are inland and uphill from the coast, in the foothill and mountain communities of San Diego County where properties draw from decomposed granite and fractured crystalline bedrock — Ramona, Julian, Alpine, Jamul, and the backcountry. If you live in Pacific Beach but also own a rural parcel like that, the rural well may be an excellent hydrofracturing candidate, and that is where our well crews concentrate.

Signs a Bedrock Well Might Need It

  • Declining yield over the years on a previously strong well.
  • A new bedrock well that came in low.
  • Slow recovery after the well is pumped hard.
  • Running short during the dry season.

What to Check Before You Call (For an Inland Well)

  • Confirm it is a private well rather than a shared or municipal connection.
  • Find the well log. Granite or fractured rock near the bottom is encouraging; all sand and gravel is not a candidate.
  • Note the symptoms. Air at the tap and slow recovery point to the well; steady weak pressure points to the pump or pressure tank.
  • Check the equipment. A worn pump, tired pressure tank, or failing switch can imitate a dying well at a fraction of the cost.
