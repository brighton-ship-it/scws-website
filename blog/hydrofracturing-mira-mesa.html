What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mira Mesa Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Mira Mesa, San Diego County

Mira Mesa is one of San Diego's larger master-planned communities, a grid of suburban streets stretching between Interstate 15 and Interstate 805 in the northern part of the city. Nearly every home here is connected to municipal water delivered by the City of San Diego and the San Diego County Water Authority, most of it imported from the Colorado River and Northern California and treated at the Miramar plant just to the east. Because of that, the honest starting point for any conversation about hydrofracturing in Mira Mesa is this: the great majority of properties inside the developed neighborhood do not have, and have never needed, a private water well.

That matters because hydrofracturing only helps a very specific kind of well. If you are reading this from a tract home off Mira Mesa Boulevard, Camino Ruiz, or New Salem Street, you are almost certainly on city water, and a well is not part of your picture. The owners who genuinely benefit from this service tend to sit on the rural-flavored edges of the area — the canyon rims and larger parcels that grade toward Scripps Ranch, Sycamore Canyon, and the back country east of the I-15 corridor — where decomposed granite and fractured metavolcanic bedrock actually hold groundwater.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

Hydrofracturing (sometimes called hydrofracking, though it has nothing to do with oil-and-gas fracking and uses no chemicals) is a well-rehabilitation method for hard-rock wells. A technician lowers an inflatable packer into the borehole and uses it to seal off a chosen section of the well. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated zone at high pressure — commonly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure flushes silt and mineral scale out of the natural fractures in the surrounding bedrock and pushes those hairline cracks open a little wider, reconnecting the borehole to water-bearing seams that had silted shut. When it works, a tired hard-rock well can see its yield climb by two to five times.

The key requirement is bedrock. Hydrofracturing widens fractures in solid granite, schist, or metavolcanic rock. It does essentially nothing in loose sand, gravel, or alluvial fill, because there are no fractures to open — the material simply caves and re-packs. This single fact decides whether a given Mira Mesa-area property is a candidate.

Why Most Mira Mesa Addresses Are Weak Candidates

The developed core of Mira Mesa is the wrong setting for hydrofracturing for two reasons. First, those homes are on municipal water, so there is no well to treat. Second, the shallow geology under much of the mesa is Linda Vista terrace gravel and sedimentary fill rather than the deep fractured crystalline rock that responds to fracturing. If you have a low-flow problem in central Mira Mesa, the answer is almost always a plumbing, pressure-regulator, or water-heater issue on a municipal line — not a well treatment.

If you are one of the rare well owners on a larger parcel toward the canyon edges and your well is screened in alluvium rather than rock, we will tell you plainly that hydrofracturing is unlikely to help. In that situation the better paths are deepening the existing well to reach a more productive zone, or drilling a new well sited with the bedrock contact in mind.

Common Causes of Low Yield Near Mira Mesa

  • Drought-lowered water table — after dry San Diego winters, shallow rock wells on the back country edges recover slowly.
  • Mineral scaling — calcium and iron build up in fractures and on the screen, choking flow over years.
  • Biofouling — iron bacteria coat the borehole and reduce the open fracture area.
  • A well that never produced well — some bedrock wells simply missed the best fractures when drilled.
  • Pump or pressure-tank problems — frequently mistaken for a dry well when the well itself is fine.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Confirm you are actually on a well. In Mira Mesa proper this is the first thing to rule out — check for a meter and a City of San Diego water bill.
  2. Note when the flow drops. A well that fades only during long showers or irrigation points to limited yield; one that is weak everywhere, all the time, points to the pump or pressure tank.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid on-off cycling usually means a waterlogged pressure tank or a failing pressure switch, not the aquifer.
  4. Look for grit or air spitting at the taps. That can signal a dropping water level or a casing problem worth a camera inspection.
  5. Find your well log. The driller's report tells us the depth and whether the well sits in rock or fill — the single most important clue for candidacy.

Hydrofracturing Versus Drilling a New Well

For the relatively few well owners on the rural edges near Mira Mesa, the real decision is usually hydrofracturing versus drilling fresh. When a well is sound structurally and is set in fractured rock, fracturing is the clear first move: it is cheaper, faster, and reuses the borehole you already paid to drill. Drilling a new well makes more sense when the casing is collapsing, when the well was poorly sited in the first place, or when fracturing has already been tried without lasting results. We walk every customer through that comparison honestly, because spending $3,000–$8,000 on a treatment that has little chance of helping serves no one. Where the geology is wrong — alluvial fill rather than rock — we say so up front and point you toward deepening or a new well sited on the bedrock contact.

It is also worth remembering that results vary with the rock. In San Diego County's decomposed granite and fractured crystalline formations, hydrofracturing improves yield in a strong majority of suitable wells, with typical gains of two to five times the pre-treatment flow. No reputable contractor can promise a specific number in advance, which is exactly why the before-and-after flow test matters: it documents what you actually gained.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do homes in central Mira Mesa even have wells?

Almost none do. The developed neighborhood is served by City of San Diego municipal water. Private wells in this area are limited to larger parcels on the rural edges toward Scripps Ranch and the back country.

Will hydrofracturing help my Mira Mesa well?

Only if the well is drilled into fractured bedrock. If your well log shows it is screened in sand, gravel, or alluvial fill, fracturing will not help and we will recommend deepening or a new well instead.

Is hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure to clear and widen existing natural fractures. No chemicals, sand proppants, or additives are used.

How much does it cost?

How long does the job take?

What if my well isn't a good candidate?

We will tell you honestly and walk you through deepening the existing well or drilling a new one, with clear pricing for each.
