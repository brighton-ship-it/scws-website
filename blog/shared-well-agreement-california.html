What Is a Shared Well?

A shared well (also called a community well, joint well, or common well) is a single water well that serves two or more properties. Unlike a public water system regulated by the state, a shared well is a private arrangement between individual property owners who rely on the same water source.

Shared wells are common in rural California for several reasons:

  • Cost savings: Drilling and maintaining one well costs less than each property having its own ($15,000-$50,000+ per well in California)
  • Geological challenges: Some areas have difficult drilling conditions or deep water tables that make individual wells impractical
  • Lot size constraints: Small rural lots may not have adequate space for proper well spacing (100+ feet from property lines and septic systems)
  • Historical development patterns: Many older subdivisions were designed with shared wells before modern water districts existed
  • Water availability: Limited groundwater may not support multiple wells in close proximity without interference

Types of Shared Well Arrangements

Two-Party Shared Well: The most common arrangement where two neighboring properties share a single well. The well is typically located on one property with an easement granting the other property access to the water and well location for maintenance.

Multi-Party Shared Well: Three or more properties share a well, common in small rural subdivisions. These arrangements are more complex and require detailed cost-sharing and governance structures.

Mutual Water Company: A formal corporation or association that owns and operates a well system serving multiple properties. Property owners hold shares in the company, which is governed by bylaws and a board of directors. Subject to additional regulations.

This guide focuses primarily on two-party and small multi-party shared wells. Mutual water companies have additional legal requirements beyond the scope of this article.

California Water Rights Basics

Understanding California water law is essential for shared well arrangements. California recognizes several types of water rights, each with different rules and priorities.

Groundwater Rights in California

Unlike surface water (rivers, lakes, streams), which is tightly regulated, groundwater has historically been governed by the "reasonable use" doctrine under common law. However, California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) enacted in 2014 has added new layers of regulation in certain areas.

Water Right Type Who Has It Key Principles
Overlying Rights Landowners above the aquifer Equal rights to reasonable use; correlative sharing during shortage
Appropriative Rights Those who put water to beneficial use off the overlying land Junior to overlying rights; based on priority of use
Prescriptive Rights Long-term users who meet specific legal criteria Acquired through continuous, open use for 5+ years

Reasonable Use Doctrine

California Constitution Article X, Section 2 requires all water use to be "reasonable and beneficial." This means:

  • You can't waste water or use more than necessary for your beneficial use
  • During water shortages, overlying landowners must share groundwater proportionally (correlative rights)
  • You can't use water in a way that unreasonably harms other users
  • Commercial or export use is subordinate to domestic and agricultural use on overlying land

⚖️ Legal Precedent

California courts have consistently held that when multiple property owners share an aquifer, each has a correlative right to reasonable use. If one party's pumping causes another party's well to fail or significantly reduces water availability, the affected party may have legal recourse. This principle applies whether wells are formally "shared" or just drawing from the same aquifer. See our guide on neighbor well interference rights for more details.

SGMA and Groundwater Sustainability

SGMA requires critically overdrafted groundwater basins to develop Groundwater Sustainability Plans (GSPs) and establish Groundwater Sustainability Agencies (GSAs). If your property is in a SGMA-regulated basin, you may face:

  • Pumping allocations or limits
  • Fees or assessments to fund sustainability programs
  • Reporting requirements for well depth, pumping volumes, and water levels
  • Restrictions on new wells or increased pumping

Most rural residential shared wells fall below SGMA's de minimis use thresholds (typically 2 acre-feet per year), but commercial or agricultural use may be regulated. Check with your county's GSA for specific requirements.

Essential Elements of a Shared Well Agreement

A comprehensive shared well agreement should address every foreseeable scenario—from routine maintenance to catastrophic failure. Here are the critical elements every agreement must include:

1. Parties and Property Identification

Include: Full legal names of all current property owners, complete legal descriptions of all properties served by the well, addresses (Assessor's Parcel Numbers), and date of the agreement.

Why it matters: Clear identification prevents confusion when properties change hands and ensures the agreement binds future owners.

2. Well Location and Description

Include: Legal description of the property where the well is located, exact location on the property (with measurements or coordinates), well depth, casing diameter, pump specifications, and well permit number if available.

Why it matters: Precise location is essential for maintenance access, property surveys, and title insurance.

3. Water Allocation and Usage Rights

Include: Each party's right to use water (typically equal for residential use), permitted uses (domestic, agricultural, commercial), any restrictions on high-volume use, and allocation method during water shortage.

Common approaches:

  • Equal rights: Each party has equal rights to reasonable domestic use (most common for 2-party residential)
  • Metered allocation: Water meters track each party's usage; may affect cost-sharing
  • Priority use: One party (often the well owner) has priority during shortages
  • Usage caps: Maximum gallons per day or month per household

Why it matters: Prevents disputes over water consumption and establishes rights enforceable in court if pumping capacity becomes insufficient.

4. Cost-Sharing Formula

Include: Percentage each party pays for different expense categories:

  • Routine maintenance (annual pump servicing, testing)
  • Repairs (pump replacement, electrical work, pressure tank)
  • Utilities (electricity to run the pump)
  • Major replacement (drilling new well if current well fails)
  • Upgrades or improvements

Common formulas:

  • 50/50 split: Simple and fair for two equal residential users
  • Proportional to usage: Based on water meters, household size, or property acreage
  • Proportional to benefit: Agricultural or commercial users pay more than residential

Why it matters: The #1 source of shared well disputes is disagreement over who pays what. A clear formula prevents arguments and provides legal recourse for cost recovery.

5. Maintenance Responsibilities and Schedule

Include: Who is responsible for scheduling and coordinating maintenance, minimum maintenance schedule (annual pump inspection, water testing frequency), notification requirements before maintenance or repairs, and procedure for emergency repairs.

Recommended schedule: Annual pump inspection and servicing, water quality testing every 1-3 years (more frequently if issues arise), and pressure tank inspection every 3-5 years.

6. Easement Rights and Access

Include: Permanent easement granting non-owner properties the right to access the well location, width and location of access easement (typically 10-20 feet), right to cross property for maintenance and repairs, and notice requirements before accessing the easement (except emergencies).

Include: Easement for water lines from the well to each property served, rights to maintain, repair, and replace water lines, and responsibility for pipe maintenance (typically each property maintains its own lines).

Why it matters: Without a recorded easement, the non-owner property has no legal right to access the well or continue receiving water, which can kill property value and prevent financing.

7. Water Quality Testing and Standards

Include: Minimum testing frequency (typically annually or every 2-3 years), which tests are required (bacteria, nitrates, basic minerals at minimum), who pays for testing (usually split equally), and action required if contamination is found.

Why it matters: Water quality problems affect all users. Regular testing catches issues early. See our well water testing guide for recommended tests.

8. Dispute Resolution Procedures

Include: Requirement for good-faith negotiation first, mandatory mediation before litigation, selection process for mediator, how mediation costs are split, and governing law (California law) and venue for legal action.

Why it matters: Litigation is expensive and time-consuming. A tiered dispute resolution process encourages settlement and can save tens of thousands in legal fees.

9. Well Replacement or Abandonment

Include: Conditions that trigger well replacement (well failure, contamination, inadequate yield), decision-making process (who decides when to replace), cost-sharing for replacement (typically same as other costs), location of replacement well (may need to be on different property), and option to drill individual wells instead of replacing shared well.

Why it matters: Well replacement can cost $20,000-$50,000+. Without a clear agreement, disputes over whether to replace, where to drill, and who pays can drag on for years while parties lack water.

10. Transfer Provisions

Include: Agreement runs with the land and binds future owners, requirement to disclose agreement to prospective buyers, whether new owners must formally accept the agreement, and process for amending the agreement (typically requires all parties' consent).

Why it matters: Ensures continuity when properties change hands and protects buyers from purchasing property without understanding their well obligations.

11. Termination Provisions

Include: Conditions under which agreement can be terminated, whether termination requires all parties' consent or can be unilateral, what happens to shared infrastructure if terminated, and notice period required before termination.

Important for FHA/VA loans: Agreements must state they cannot be unilaterally terminated, or lenders won't approve financing.

Who Pays When One Party Refuses?

If one party refuses to pay their share of agreed-upon costs, the other party has several options:

  • Pay the full cost and seek reimbursement: Keep detailed records and invoices
  • File a lien: In California, you may be able to place a mechanic's lien on the non-paying party's property for unpaid well maintenance costs
  • Invoke dispute resolution: Follow the mediation or arbitration process in your agreement
  • Small claims court: For amounts under $10,000, small claims is fast and doesn't require an attorney
  • Civil lawsuit: For larger amounts, file a breach of contract lawsuit to recover costs plus attorney fees (if agreement allows)

A well-drafted shared well agreement should specify that the prevailing party in any dispute is entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees and costs. This discourages frivolous refusals to pay.

Easement Rights and Access

If the well is located on one property but serves another, an easement is essential. An easement is a legal right to use someone else's property for a specific purpose—in this case, accessing water from a shared well.

Types of Easements for Shared Wells

Easement for Water Access: Grants the non-owner property the right to receive water from the well. This is the fundamental easement without which the property has no water rights.

Easement for Physical Access: Grants the right to access the well location for maintenance, repairs, inspections, and emergencies. Should specify the width of access route (typically 10-20 feet) and any restrictions on use (e.g., no heavy equipment without notice).

Easement for Water Lines: Grants the right to install, maintain, repair, and replace water pipes running from the well to the served property. Should specify pipe depth, location, and responsibility for repairs.

Why recording is critical: Easements must be recorded with the county recorder's office to "run with the land" and bind future owners. An unrecorded easement may not be enforceable against subsequent purchasers and will cause problems with title insurance and financing.

What Happens If There's No Easement?

Operating a shared well without a recorded easement creates serious risks:

  • Loss of water access: The property owner can legally deny access to the well at any time
  • Unmarketable title: Title companies won't insure a property that depends on water access without a recorded easement
  • Financing problems: No bank will provide a mortgage for a property without guaranteed water access
  • Property value destruction: Without assured water access, the property may be virtually unsellable

If you're buying property on a shared well, verify during escrow that:

  • A recorded shared well agreement and easement exist
  • Title insurance covers the easement
  • The easement is sufficient for lender requirements (especially FHA/VA)

What Happens When the Well Fails or Needs Replacement

Well failure is one of the most stressful scenarios for shared well users. Without water, properties are uninhabitable, and replacement costs can easily exceed $20,000-$50,000. Here's how to handle it:

Common Causes of Well Failure

  • Pump failure: Most common—submersible pumps last 10-20 years (replacement: $1,200-$3,500)
  • Declining water table: Well runs dry due to drought, increased pumping, or aquifer depletion
  • Well collapse or casing damage: Structural failure of the well bore or casing
  • Contamination: Bacteria, nitrates, or other contaminants make water unusable
  • Electrical failure: Wiring, controls, or pressure systems fail

Decision-Making Process for Replacement

Your shared well agreement should specify how decisions are made when major work is needed:

Step 1: Professional Assessment
Hire a licensed well contractor to diagnose the problem and provide repair/replacement options with cost estimates. Both parties should be present or receive copies of the assessment.

Step 2: Evaluate Options
Options may include: pump replacement (if that's the only issue), well rehabilitation (hydrofracturing, acidizing), drilling a deeper well at the same location, or drilling a new well at a different location.

Step 3: Get Multiple Bids
For major work, obtain at least 3 written bids from licensed contractors. Compare scope of work, warranties, and timelines.

Step 4: Agree on Approach
If parties disagree on whether to repair or replace, the agreement should specify how disputes are resolved—mediation, majority vote (for 3+ parties), or default to the least expensive option that restores water service.

Step 5: Document Everything
Put decisions in writing, signed by all parties, including: chosen contractor and scope of work, total cost and each party's share, payment schedule, and timeline for completion.

Alternative Options When Replacement Is Needed

If the well needs complete replacement, parties may consider:

  • Drilling a new shared well: Split the cost of a new well (typically $15,000-$50,000+ in California)
  • Each party drilling individual wells: More expensive total but eliminates future sharing issues ($15,000-$50,000+ per well)
  • Connecting to public water (if available): May require connection fees, extension of lines, and ongoing water bills
  • One party buying out the other: One party takes sole ownership of the well and other party drills their own or connects to public water

Common Disputes and Resolution

Even with a detailed agreement, disputes arise. Here are the most common shared well disputes and how to resolve them:

Top 5 Shared Well Disputes

1. Excessive Water Use
Issue: One party uses significantly more water (large family, irrigation, pool filling)
Solution: Install water meters to track usage; adjust cost-sharing based on consumption; set reasonable use caps in the agreement

2. Refusal to Pay for Repairs
Issue: One party won't pay their share of maintenance or repair costs
Solution: Document expenses with invoices; send written demand; file lien or pursue small claims court; agreement should allow prevailing party to recover attorney fees

3. Access Disputes
Issue: Well owner blocks access for maintenance or unreasonably restricts access
Solution: Recorded easement with clear access terms; require reasonable notice except emergencies; mediation if access is repeatedly denied

4. Water Quality Problems
Issue: Disagreement over whether/how to address contamination or quality issues
Solution: Agreement should mandate testing frequency; specify what contamination levels require action; follow health department recommendations

5. Disagreement Over Replacement
Issue: Well needs replacement but parties disagree on whether to repair, replace, or go separate ways
Solution: Get professional assessment; obtain multiple bids; agreement should specify decision process (mediation, majority vote, binding arbitration)

Dispute Resolution Process

Follow this escalation ladder to resolve disputes without destroying neighbor relationships or spending thousands on lawyers:

Level 1: Direct Communication
Talk to the other party calmly and in good faith. Many disputes arise from misunderstanding or lack of communication. Document the conversation in writing (email summary).

Level 2: Written Notice
Send a formal written notice stating the issue, referencing the agreement provision, and proposing a solution. Give 15-30 days for response. Send via certified mail for documentation.

Level 3: Mediation
Hire a neutral mediator (typically $200-$500 for a half-day session, split between parties). Mediation is informal, confidential, and resolves 60-80% of disputes without litigation.

Level 4: Arbitration (if required by agreement)
Binding arbitration by a neutral arbitrator. More formal than mediation; decision is enforceable like a court judgment. Typically faster and cheaper than litigation.

Level 5: Litigation
File a lawsuit for breach of contract, easement interference, or other claims. Litigation is expensive ($10,000-$50,000+), time-consuming (1-3 years), and stressful. Use only as a last resort.

⚖️ When to Involve an Attorney

Consult a water rights or real estate attorney if: the dispute involves significant costs ($5,000+), water access has been cut off, the other party has hired an attorney, you're considering litigation, or property is changing hands. An attorney can often resolve disputes with a single letter, which is far cheaper than litigation.

Selling Property with a Shared Well: Disclosure Requirements

California law requires sellers to disclose all "material facts" about the property that could affect a buyer's decision. A shared well is definitely a material fact. Failure to disclose can result in:

  • Contract rescission (buyer cancels the purchase)
  • Financial damages (buyer sues for cost to drill individual well)
  • Criminal fraud charges (in extreme cases)

What You Must Disclose

  • That the property is served by a shared well (not individual well or public water)
  • Location of the well (on your property or neighbor's)
  • Copy of the recorded shared well agreement and easement
  • Recent water quality test results (within 1 year recommended)
  • History of maintenance, repairs, and costs for the past few years
  • Any known problems: water quality issues, past disputes, inadequate supply, etc.
  • Contact information for the other well users
  • Whether the well is adequate for the property's use and meets lender requirements

Buyer Due Diligence for Shared Wells

If you're buying property on a shared well, protect yourself with these steps:

  • Review the shared well agreement: Have your real estate attorney review it for FHA/VA compliance and fair terms
  • Order a well inspection: Professional well inspection ($300-$600) to assess well condition, pump, and system
  • Test water quality: Get bacteria, nitrate, and mineral testing ($150-$300) within 30 days of closing
  • Verify easement is recorded: Title company should confirm the easement is recorded and insurable
  • Talk to the other well users: Ask about water adequacy, maintenance history, costs, and any issues
  • Confirm lender requirements: If using FHA/VA, verify the agreement meets their specific requirements
  • Include contingencies: Make your offer contingent on satisfactory well inspection and water quality test

FHA/VA Loan Implications for Shared Wells

If you're financing a property with an FHA or VA loan, the shared well arrangement must meet strict requirements. Many informal shared well agreements don't qualify, which can kill financing and sink the sale.

FHA Requirements for Shared Wells

FHA loans (HUD Handbook 4000.1) require shared wells to meet these conditions:

  • Recorded agreement: The shared well agreement must be recorded with the county and run with the land
  • Perpetual maintenance: Agreement must include provisions for perpetual maintenance and cost-sharing
  • Cannot be cancelled: The agreement cannot be cancelled or terminated by any single party without consent of all parties
  • Adequate supply: Well must provide adequate water quantity for all properties served
  • Potable water: Water quality must meet local health department standards (bacteria and nitrate tests required)
  • Clear easements: All necessary easements for access and water lines must be recorded
  • Legal description: Properties served and well location must be clearly described

VA Requirements for Shared Wells

VA loans have similar requirements to FHA:

  • Recorded shared well agreement with perpetual maintenance provisions
  • Cannot be unilaterally terminated
  • Adequate supply and potable water quality
  • Proper easements for access and water distribution
  • Agreement binds future owners

VA additionally requires that the property appraiser note the shared well in the appraisal report and confirm it doesn't adversely affect property value.

What If Your Agreement Doesn't Meet FHA/VA Standards?

If the existing shared well agreement doesn't meet lender requirements, you have options:

  • Amend the agreement: All parties sign an amendment that adds required provisions, then record it with the county
  • Replace with new agreement: Draft a new agreement that meets requirements, get all parties to sign, and record it
  • Use conventional financing: Conventional loans have fewer restrictions on shared wells (though lenders still want to see a solid agreement)
  • Cash purchase: If financing isn't required, the agreement standards are less strict (but still important for protection)

Many sellers facing an FHA/VA buyer will work with all well parties to amend the agreement rather than lose the sale. Get this done early in escrow—it can take 2-4 weeks to coordinate signatures and recording.

How to Draft or Update a Shared Well Agreement

Whether you're creating a new shared well arrangement or updating an outdated agreement, follow these steps:

Step-by-Step Process

Step 1: Gather Information
Collect: legal property descriptions (from deeds), well location and specifications, existing easements or agreements, water quality test results, and maintenance history.

Step 2: Determine Key Terms
Discuss and agree on: cost-sharing formula, maintenance responsibilities, usage rights and any restrictions, dispute resolution process, and replacement procedures.

Step 3: Draft the Agreement
Options: hire a water rights or real estate attorney ($800-$2,500), use a template and have an attorney review it ($300-$800), or DIY with online templates (risky—may not hold up or meet lender requirements).

Step 4: Review and Negotiate
All parties review the draft. Negotiate any disputed terms. Each party should have their own attorney review it (optional but recommended for large investments).

Step 5: Sign and Notarize
All property owners sign the agreement in front of a notary public. Each party should receive an original signed copy.

Step 6: Record with County
File the signed agreement with your county recorder's office (fee: typically $50-$150). Recording makes the agreement run with the land and protects future owners.

Step 7: Update Title Insurance
Notify your title insurance company that the easement and agreement have been recorded. Confirm coverage.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a shared well agreement in California?

A shared well agreement is a legally binding contract between two or more property owners who share a single water well. The agreement outlines each party's rights to use the well water, responsibilities for maintenance and repairs, cost-sharing arrangements, access rights, and procedures for resolving disputes. In California, shared wells are common in rural areas where individual wells aren't feasible due to cost, geology, or lot size. A properly drafted shared well agreement protects all parties by clearly defining expectations and preventing future conflicts over water access, maintenance costs, and property rights.

How do I write a shared well agreement in California?

A California shared well agreement should include: 1) Identification of all parties and properties served, 2) Legal description of the well location and easement rights, 3) Water allocation and usage rights for each party, 4) Cost-sharing formula for maintenance, repairs, electricity, and replacement, 5) Maintenance responsibilities and schedules, 6) Access rights to the well for maintenance and repairs, 7) Testing and water quality standards, 8) Dispute resolution procedures, 9) Terms for adding or removing parties, 10) Well replacement or abandonment procedures, and 11) Provisions for property transfer. Have a real estate attorney review the agreement to ensure it complies with California water law and is properly recorded with the county. Consider hiring a water rights attorney if the situation is complex or involves more than two parties.

Can FHA or VA loans be used for property with a shared well?

Yes, FHA and VA loans can be used for properties with shared wells, but strict requirements must be met. The shared well agreement must be recorded with the county, include provisions for perpetual maintenance and cost-sharing, establish clear easement rights, and cannot be terminated without consent of all parties. FHA requires that the agreement can't be cancelled by any single party, ensures adequate water supply and quality, and includes provisions for replacement or repair. VA has similar requirements. If the existing shared well agreement doesn't meet FHA/VA standards, it must be amended and re-recorded before loan approval. Many lenders require the shared well agreement to be reviewed by their attorney before closing.

What happens when a shared well fails or needs replacement?

When a shared well fails, the shared well agreement should specify who pays for repairs or replacement and how decisions are made. Typically, costs are split according to the cost-sharing formula (often 50/50 for two parties, or based on usage). If one party refuses to pay, the other party may pay the full cost and place a lien on the non-paying party's property, or pursue legal action. For major failures requiring well replacement ($15,000-$50,000+), parties should obtain multiple bids, agree on the scope of work, and document everything in writing. If the agreement doesn't address replacement, California law generally requires all benefiting parties to share costs proportionally. Disputes often require mediation or litigation. This is why a well-drafted agreement with clear replacement procedures is essential.

Do I have to disclose a shared well when selling property in California?

Yes, California law requires sellers to disclose all material facts about the property, including shared well arrangements. You must provide buyers with: the recorded shared well agreement, recent water quality test results (recommended within 1 year), maintenance history and costs, any known problems or disputes, and current contact information for other well users. Failure to disclose a shared well can result in legal liability, contract rescission, or financial damages. The shared well agreement should be provided during escrow and reviewed by the buyer's attorney. Buyers should also order a well inspection and water quality test. Lenders will require the shared well agreement for loan approval, especially for FHA and VA loans.

How are maintenance costs typically shared for a shared well?

Shared well maintenance costs are typically divided based on one of these methods: 1) Equal split (50/50 for two parties, 33/33/33 for three parties, etc.) - simple but doesn't account for different usage levels, 2) Proportional to usage - measured by water meters, household size, or property size, 3) Proportional to benefit - accounts for different water needs (e.g., residential vs. agricultural), or 4) Based on property value or acreage. The shared well agreement should specify which method applies to different expenses: routine maintenance (pump servicing, testing), repairs (pump replacement, wiring), utilities (electricity for pump operation), and major replacement (drilling new well). Most agreements split electricity 50/50 since it's ongoing, while major repairs are handled based on the primary cost-sharing formula. Annual costs for a shared well typically range from $200-$800 per party depending on usage and maintenance needs.

What are my rights if my neighbor's well interferes with mine in California?

In California, groundwater rights are governed by the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) and common law reasonable use doctrine. If your neighbor's well pumping causes your well to go dry or significantly reduces your water supply, you may have legal recourse under the 'correlative rights' principle - overlying landowners have equal rights to reasonable use of groundwater. You can: 1) Document the interference with well logs, water level measurements, and pumping records, 2) Request the neighbor reduce pumping to reasonable levels, 3) File a complaint with your local Groundwater Sustainability Agency (GSA) if your area is under SGMA management, or 4) Pursue legal action for unreasonable use or interference with your property rights. However, if you share the same well, interference disputes are resolved through your shared well agreement. California courts generally require groundwater users to share the resource equitably, especially during drought conditions. Learn more in our guide on neighbor well interference rights.

Can I drill my own well if I'm currently on a shared well?

Whether you can drill your own well while on a shared well depends on your shared well agreement, local regulations, and water rights. Check your shared well agreement for provisions about opting out or drilling individual wells - some agreements prohibit this, while others allow it with proper notice. You'll need to comply with county well permits and spacing requirements (typically 100+ feet from property lines and existing wells). Consider water availability - will there be enough groundwater for both wells without interfering? Some agreements require you to continue paying a portion of shared well maintenance even if you drill your own well (to maintain property values and easement rights). If your agreement is silent on this issue, consult a water rights attorney before proceeding. Drilling your own well typically costs $15,000-$50,000+ in California, so ensure you understand all legal and financial implications before proceeding.
