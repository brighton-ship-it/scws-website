Citrus Water Requirements

Citrus trees need consistent water for optimal production:

  • Young trees: 10-20 gallons per week
  • Mature trees: 50-75 gallons per week in summer
  • Per acre: Approximately 3-4 acre-feet annually

That translates to serious well capacity requirements for commercial groves.

Grove Well Systems

  • Agricultural wells: High-capacity for irrigation demand
  • Drip irrigation: Efficient water delivery to root zones
  • Micro-sprinklers: Coverage with minimal waste
  • Filtration: Keep drip emitters clean
  • Automation: Timed irrigation cycles

Water Quality for Citrus

Citrus is sensitive to water quality issues:

  • Salinity: High salt damages trees over time
  • Boron: Toxic to citrus at moderate levels
  • Chloride: Causes leaf burn

We can test your well water and recommend treatment if needed.

Citrus Well Service Across the Chino Hills, San Bernardino County

Chino Hills rises along the southwestern corner of San Bernardino County, a landscape of rolling hills and canyon country where the Inland Empire gives way to the Orange County line and Carbon Canyon threads down toward Brea. This was working agricultural land for generations, part of the Chino Valley's broad sweep of dairies, row crops, and orchards before suburban development arrived. Citrus has long thrived in the area's warm days and protected pockets, and many properties here, from estate groves on the hillsides to small commercial plantings on the valley floor, still depend on a private agricultural well. Southern California Well Service helps Chino Hills growers put in, equip, and look after the wells that keep their citrus producing.

Sitting at the edge of the Chino Basin, an actively managed groundwater system with deep agricultural roots, Chino Hills presents its own mix of terrain and water conditions. The rolling topography, the former dairy and farm land, and the basin management all shape what it takes to run a reliable citrus well. We are a licensed C-57 water well contractor founded in 2020, with 60+ years of family heritage, and we tailor every system to the property in front of us.

The Water Appetite of a Citrus Grove

Citrus trees stay green and active all year, transpiring even through winter, and in the heat of a Chino Hills summer their water needs run high. A useful planning benchmark is that a mature citrus grove draws somewhere around 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet of water per acre annually. Where any given grove lands within that range depends on tree age, variety, rootstock, canopy size, and just how hot and dry the year turns out to be.

For designing a well, peak demand carries more weight than the annual average. When a heat wave settles over the valley in midsummer, daily water use surges and the irrigation system has to deliver fast enough to keep the root zone moist. We help growers turn acreage and tree counts into a realistic gallons-per-minute (GPM) goal anchored to that peak, ensuring the well and pump can carry the grove through the hardest stretch of the season.

Frost Protection in the Hills and Canyons

The varied terrain of Chino Hills means cold air can pool in low spots and canyon bottoms on clear winter nights, putting citrus at risk. Growers who guard against freeze damage with water-based frost protection place a heavy load on their wells. A micro-sprinkler frost system can demand on the order of 50 or more GPM per acre, running steadily for hours overnight as temperatures dip toward freezing. Because that far exceeds ordinary irrigation flow, frost capability has to be planned into the system from the start if a grower wants it.

Matching Well Yield, Pump, and Storage

A dependable citrus water supply comes from three parts working in concert: the well's yield, the pump's capacity, and the cushion that storage provides.

Evaluating Well Yield

We determine what a well can sustainably deliver through proper testing, measuring flow and how far the water level draws down under load rather than relying on assumptions. When an existing well underperforms, methods such as hydrofracturing can sometimes improve yield, depending on the rock and aquifer conditions.

High-Capacity Pumps

Most commercial citrus relies on a high-capacity submersible pump, or a vertical turbine pump in larger or shallower settings. These are built to push substantial volume against orchard system pressure. Proper selection is critical: a pump correctly matched to the well and grove runs efficiently and endures, while a mismatch burns energy and shortens equipment life.

Storage Ponds and Tanks

Since peak irrigation and frost demand can outpace a well's minute-to-minute output, storage frequently bridges the gap. A lined pond or a bank of tanks lets the well fill the reserve steadily over many hours, and the grove draws from that stored water at a high rate exactly when demand spikes. On the uneven ground common in Chino Hills, thoughtfully placed storage can also help with pressure and distribution across the grove.

Water Quality and Citrus Sensitivity

Citrus is among the more salt-sensitive tree crops, which makes well-water chemistry a real concern rather than an afterthought. Three factors top our list.

  • Salinity (EC): The total salt load, read as electrical conductivity, governs how easily citrus takes up water. High salinity stresses trees and trims yield over time, and controlling it can require lower-salt water or intentional leaching of salts below the roots.
  • Chloride: Citrus accumulates chloride in its foliage, which surfaces as burned leaf tips and margins. Water carrying high chloride can scorch leaves and weaken trees, particularly on sensitive rootstocks.
  • Boron: A nutrient in trace amounts and a toxin just above them, boron generally becomes a problem for citrus above roughly 0.5 to 1 part per million, producing leaf burn much like chloride does.

Given the area's dairy and farming past, groundwater in parts of the Chino Basin can also contain nitrate, a topic growers sometimes raise. For citrus irrigation, however, salinity, chloride, and boron usually shape the management plan. We sample your well, translate the lab numbers into plain language, and recommend blending, treatment, or rootstock and irrigation changes when the water warrants it.

Drought and Chino Basin Groundwater Management

Recurring drought is a constant in Southern California, and it weighs on private wells. As the basin draws down across dry years, water levels fall and yields can dip right when citrus needs the most. The Chino Basin is an actively managed groundwater system, overseen through structures such as the Chino Basin Watermaster that balance how much is pumped against how the basin is recharged. For Chino Hills growers, the practical lessons are to irrigate efficiently, watch well performance season to season, and respond to early signs of decline before they become a shutdown.

Citrus Well Issues Common in Chino Hills

  • Yield falling off as a well ages or the water table drops, leaving the grove short at the summer peak.
  • Pump wear and failure from sediment, mineral scale, age, or a pump poorly suited to the well.
  • Emitters and micro-sprinklers clogging with sediment, mineral deposits, or biological growth in the lines.
  • Water-quality drift, where salinity or chloride rises over a season until leaf burn appears.
  • Electrical and control failures on older systems, and pressure imbalances across hilly terrain that interrupt even watering.

Maintenance That Pays for Itself

Preventive care costs far less than an emergency in the middle of a heat wave. We advise periodic water testing, routine inspection of the pump and pressure system, flushing of filtration and irrigation lines, and steady tracking of flow and drawdown so a downward trend shows up early. A well that is watched is a well that rarely catches you off guard.

When to Bring in a Pro

Call us when flow or pressure slips, when emitters spit air, when the pump short-cycles or runs nonstop, when energy bills climb, when leaf burn points toward water chemistry, or when a frost night reveals that supply cannot keep up. Each is a cue worth checking promptly, and early attention usually keeps the repair small.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a Chino Hills citrus grove require?

Mature citrus typically uses about 3.5 to 4.5 acre-feet per acre per year, with demand peaking in summer. If you want frost protection, plan for up to 50 or more GPM per acre during a freeze. We design the well and pump around your peak need.

Could my well water harm my citrus trees?

It can if the chemistry is off. Citrus is salt-sensitive, so we test salinity, chloride, and boron, and we can address nitrate questions common to the Chino Basin. Testing shows whether blending or treatment is needed.

Do I really need a storage pond or tanks?

Often yes. Storage lets a steady well meet short bursts of heavy demand, such as a hot afternoon or an overnight frost. On hilly ground it can also help even out pressure across the grove.

What should I do about declining well yield?

Get it evaluated quickly. Depending on conditions, the fix might be pump service, well rehabilitation, or hydrofracturing to improve flow. Early action keeps your trees supplied through peak season.

Is groundwater here actively managed?

Yes. Chino Hills sits at the edge of the Chino Basin, an actively managed groundwater system. We help growers pump efficiently and stay productive within that structure.

How soon can you respond to an emergency?

We offer same-day emergency service. When citrus is going without water in the heat, we move fast to get your system running again.
