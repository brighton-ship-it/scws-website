Understanding Radon Risks

Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas and the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. When dissolved in water, radon releases into household air during showering, dishwashing, and other water use. The primary risk is inhalation, not ingestion—though both contribute to exposure. Homes with both private wells and elevated radon should test water specifically.

Testing Well Water for Radon

Radon water testing requires special collection procedures—samples must be taken to avoid releasing gas. Use a certified lab that provides proper collection containers and instructions. Results are reported in picocuries per liter (pCi/L). Test during normal usage periods for accurate results. If home air radon is elevated, water testing helps determine how much the well contributes to total exposure.

Aeration Treatment Systems

Aeration systems strip radon from water by spraying it into an air stream that vents outdoors. Point-of-entry systems treat all household water before it reaches taps. Aeration removes 95-99% of radon with no ongoing media costs—just electricity to run the blower. Systems require venting to outside air, away from windows and air intakes. Maintenance involves periodic cleaning and motor service.

Granular Activated Carbon Treatment

GAC systems filter water through activated carbon that adsorbs radon gas. Effective removal (95%+) for moderate radon levels. The carbon eventually becomes radioactive and requires disposal as low-level radioactive waste per local regulations. Carbon typically needs replacement every few years. GAC works well combined with other treatment needs but aeration is generally preferred for high radon levels due to disposal concerns.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does radon get into well water?

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas produced by decaying uranium in rock and soil. It dissolves into groundwater as water moves through radon-containing formations. Granite and certain other rock types produce higher radon levels.

What radon level in water is dangerous?

The EPA recommends action at 4,000 pCi/L in water or higher. Some states have lower guidelines. Testing both water and indoor air is important since radon in water contributes to overall airborne radon exposure in your home.

Which is better: aeration or carbon for radon?

Aeration removes 95-99% of radon and requires no media replacement—preferred for high radon levels. Granular activated carbon removes 95%+ but the carbon must be replaced periodically and becomes radioactive, requiring careful disposal.
