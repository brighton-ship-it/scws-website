Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Big Bear City well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well Water Realities at 6,700 Feet in Big Bear City

Big Bear City sits high in the San Bernardino Mountains, at roughly 6,700 feet of elevation in the eastern end of the Big Bear Valley, in San Bernardino County. Winters here bring snow, hard freezes, and the seasonal rhythm of cabins and second homes that empty out and fill up with the weather. It is a beautiful alpine community, and it is emphatically not avocado country. Avocados are subtropical trees that die in a hard frost, so growing a commercial grove at Big Bear's elevation is simply not possible. We will not pretend otherwise. What Big Bear City property owners actually need is dependable mountain well service, and that is exactly what Southern California Well Service provides.

If you found this page hoping for avocado-grove irrigation, the honest reframing is this: your real water challenges here are fractured-granite wells, freeze protection, variable yield, and keeping a reliable supply running at a cabin that may sit empty through a snowy stretch. Those are the problems we solve, and they are very different from anything a coastal avocado grower faces.

How Mountain Wells Work in the Big Bear Valley

The geology beneath Big Bear is dominated by hard crystalline granite, the same rock that forms the surrounding peaks. Unlike the porous, sandy aquifers of valley floors, Big Bear's groundwater largely occupies fractures and fissures within that granite, fed by snowmelt and rainfall percolating down through cracks. Alluvial deposits do thicken toward the center of the valley and near the lake, where some properties tap shallower basin water, but hillside and higher-elevation lots often must drill through solid granite and depend on intersecting fractures to find a productive zone.

Recharge in the Big Bear Valley comes almost entirely from snow and rain, which makes the system sensitive to drought years. After a dry winter, the fractures that feed a well can carry less water, and yields that were comfortable in a wet year can tighten noticeably. Planning for that variability, with adequate storage and a pump sized to the well's sustainable yield rather than its best-day output, is part of building a mountain water system that lasts.

That fractured-rock setting has real consequences. Two wells a few hundred feet apart can have very different yields depending on whether they intersect a good fracture network. Valley-bottom parcels near the lake may reach water at shallower depths, while hillside lots in areas like Sugarloaf or the higher neighborhoods can require deeper drilling. A submersible pump lifts the water, a pressure tank and switch regulate delivery, and freeze-protected plumbing keeps the system alive through winter. Getting all of that right at altitude is a specialty, not a generic plumbing job.

Common Mountain Well Scenarios We See

  • Low or variable yield from fractured-granite wells that depend on hitting water-bearing cracks.
  • Freeze damage to wellheads, pressure tanks, and exposed plumbing during hard winters.
  • Cabins left unattended where a failure goes unnoticed until the owner returns to no water or a burst line.
  • Pump and pressure-tank wear on seasonal systems that cycle through freeze-thaw stress.
  • Iron, manganese, or sediment in mountain groundwater that stains fixtures and clogs filters.
  • Hydrofracturing candidates, where an underperforming granite well may benefit from fracture stimulation to boost yield.

Seasonal ownership shapes nearly everything about water service up here. A cabin that sits empty for weeks at a time gives small problems room to grow unnoticed, so a slow leak or a tank losing its charge can turn into a frozen, split line or a burned-out pump before anyone is on site to catch it. Owners who schedule a pre-season inspection and a winterization visit consistently spend less over the life of their system than those who wait for a failure to announce itself.

What to Check Before You Call

A few observations help us diagnose a mountain well quickly, especially if you are arriving at a cabin after time away:

  1. Confirm power to the pump and check that the dedicated breaker has not tripped during a winter storm.
  2. Listen for rapid pump cycling, which signals a waterlogged pressure tank or failing switch.
  3. Inspect exposed plumbing and the wellhead for freeze cracks or split fittings.
  4. Watch the pressure gauge for swings that indicate tank or switch problems.
  5. Note whether the well struggles only during heavy use, which can reveal a yield limitation in the granite.

The freeze-thaw cycle is the single biggest stress on mountain well equipment. Water expanding as it freezes can crack a pressure tank, split a fitting, or rupture a buried line, and the damage often is not visible until the system is pressurized again in spring. Wellheads exposed to the wind chill of a Big Bear winter need insulation and sometimes heat tape, and pressure tanks belong in conditioned or heated space wherever possible. Building these protections in from the start is far cheaper than repairing freeze damage after the fact.

It also helps to understand what your water is doing chemically. Mountain groundwater filtered through granite and alluvium can pick up iron and manganese, which leave rust and black staining on fixtures and gradually clog filters and aerators. Hardness from dissolved minerals is common as well. None of this is dangerous when properly treated, but it does call for the right filtration matched to a tested water sample rather than a one-size-fits-all softener. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you grow avocados in Big Bear City?

No. At roughly 6,700 feet with hard winter freezes, Big Bear's climate kills subtropical avocado trees. Commercial avocados are grown in frost-free inland and coastal areas of San Diego County, not in the mountains.

Why does my granite well produce less water than my neighbor's?

Big Bear's groundwater sits in fractures within granite, so yield depends on whether your well intersects productive cracks. Two nearby wells can differ greatly, which is why local drilling knowledge matters.

Can a weak mountain well be improved?

Often yes. Hydrofracturing can open or extend fractures around the borehole to increase yield, typically costing $3,000 to $8,000, and it is frequently cheaper than drilling a new well.

How do I protect my well from freezing?

Insulated and heated wellheads, properly buried lines, and freeze-protected pressure tanks all help. We can winterize a seasonal cabin's system and inspect it before you return for the season.
