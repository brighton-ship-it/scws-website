Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many Riverside County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Flow Well Solutions in Cabazon and the San Gorgonio Pass

Out in the wind-swept San Gorgonio Pass, water is precious and wells work hard. If your Cabazon well is giving you less than it used to — weak pressure when two taps run, a tank that never quite catches up, or a slow fade over several dry years — you are dealing with a low-yield well. In this part of Riverside County, where groundwater is the only source of potable water, that is a problem worth solving promptly. The encouraging news is that most low-flow wells around Cabazon can be improved without starting over.

Cabazon lies within the San Gorgonio Pass groundwater subbasin, one of several storage units — Beaumont, Banning, and Cabazon among them — that hold the area's water. The subbasin has been stressed by decades of pumping and recurring drought, and it is now actively managed under a state-adopted Groundwater Sustainability Plan. For individual well owners, that regional picture often shows up as a slowly dropping water table.

How Well Yield Works and Why We Test It

Well yield is the sustained gallons-per-minute your well can deliver without running dry — separate from the pressure you feel at the faucet. We measure it with a flow test combined with drawdown and recovery: pumping at a steady rate, watching how far the level falls, and timing how fast it rebounds. A well that draws down deeply and recovers slowly is aquifer- or fracture-limited; one that holds level but delivers little is usually fouled or under-pumped. For a Cabazon home, 5 to 10 GPM is comfortable, and a careful test tells us which fix your well actually needs.

What Causes Low Flow in Cabazon Wells

  • A declining water table. Long-term pumping and drought in the pass have lowered groundwater levels, leaving some pumps starved.
  • Mineral scale on the screen. The area's hard water deposits calcium and iron that gradually plug the screen and gravel pack.
  • A pump set too high above the current water column or productive zone.
  • A shallow or aging well that never reached, or has fallen below, the reliable producing depth.
  • Sediment and fine sand from the basin's alluvial fill clogging the intake.

Proven Fixes for a Low-Yield Cabazon Well

Rehabilitation and redevelopment clear scale and fouling from the screen and gravel pack to restore lost capacity — usually the first and cheapest step. Lowering the pump recovers flow when the water table has dropped. Hydrofracturing can help where the well is completed in fractured bedrock along the pass margins. Deepening or a new well reaches a more reliable zone when the existing one is tapped out. And a storage tank with a booster or constant-pressure pump banks a modest well's 24-hour output so your home gets strong pressure on demand — a practical answer in this dry, windy climate.

When to Call a Professional

Get in touch if flow has dropped, you are pulling air or sand, pressure collapses under normal use, or you are adding demand the well cannot meet. A flow and drawdown test pinpoints the lowest-cost solution that will actually restore your water.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good well yield for a home in Cabazon?

For a Cabazon household in the San Gorgonio Pass, 5 to 10 GPM is comfortable. Some wells in the Cabazon storage unit produce well, but properties on the rocky margins or affected by the area's high winds and dry climate can run lower, especially as regional groundwater levels decline.

Why has my Cabazon well lost flow?

Cabazon sits in the San Gorgonio Pass groundwater subbasin, where groundwater is the only source of potable supply and levels have been stressed by long-term pumping and drought. A dropping water table, a scaled screen from mineral-rich water, or a pump set too high are the usual reasons flow falls off.

Does hydrofracturing work near Cabazon?

It depends on geology. Where wells are completed in fractured bedrock along the pass margins, hydrofracturing can reopen fractures and raise yield. Wells in deeper alluvial fill of the subbasin usually benefit more from rehabilitation, a deeper pump setting, or added storage.

Can a storage tank fix low flow at my Cabazon property?

Frequently, yes. A storage tank collects your well's output over 24 hours so a booster or constant-pressure pump can deliver strong household pressure even from a modest well. It is one of the most cost-effective ways to handle peak demand in the windy, dry pass.

How deep are wells in the Cabazon area?

Wells in the San Gorgonio Pass subbasin vary widely with location in the basin, commonly ranging from roughly 200 to 500 feet. A driller familiar with the Cabazon storage unit can advise on the most reliable producing depth for your parcel.

What will it cost to improve a low-yield Cabazon well?

Rehabilitation usually runs $1,500 to $6,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, and storage-plus-booster systems $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey well ranges from $18,000 to $42,000. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward approved work.
