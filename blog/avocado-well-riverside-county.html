Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in Riverside County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • High-capacity agricultural wells
  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Riverside County well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well and Irrigation Service Across Riverside County's Many Landscapes

Riverside County is one of the most geographically varied counties in California, and no two private wells in it behave quite the same way. Southern California Well Service works the full span of the county — from the alluvial valleys of the western Inland Empire around Riverside, Corona, Moreno Valley, Norco, and Eastvale, out through Temecula, Murrieta, and the rolling avocado-and-citrus country of De Luz, up into the Anza Valley where we keep a local office, and east across Hemet and the San Jacinto Valley into the Coachella Valley desert (Palm Springs, Palm Desert, La Quinta, Indio, Coachella) and on to Blythe and the Palo Verde Valley along the Colorado River. Each of those zones sits on a different kind of water, and that drives everything about how a well is drilled, equipped, and maintained.

In the western Santa Ana watershed valleys, wells generally tap alluvial groundwater basins and can be a few hundred feet deep. In the granitic highlands around Anza, De Luz, and the Temecula hills, wells are commonly drilled into fractured bedrock and run anywhere from 400 to 800 feet or more, with yield depending on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole intersects. In the desert east, deep alluvial aquifers like the Whitewater subbasin under the Coachella Valley and the basin beneath Palo Verde feed both farms and homes. We carry equipment and parts sized for all of these conditions so we are not guessing on the first visit.

How a Private Well System Works Here

Whatever corner of Riverside County you live in, the system is built from the same core parts: a drilled and cased well, a submersible (or sometimes a deep-well jet) pump set down in the water column, electrical controls, a pressure tank, a pressure switch, and any treatment needed for your specific water. The pump lifts water to the surface, the pressure tank stores it and smooths out demand so the pump is not short-cycling, and the pressure switch tells the pump when to turn on and off. On a grove or larger property, that core is often paired with a storage tank and a booster or constant-pressure system to feed drip lines and micro-sprinklers at a steady pressure.

On the avocado and citrus groves in the De Luz hills, the Temecula-adjacent benchlands, and the Anza area, irrigation is the whole point of the well. A mature avocado tree can drink 40 to 70 gallons a day in peak summer, and groves are typically planned around several acre-feet per acre per year. That means a grove well has to deliver a dependable flow during fruit set and sizing, and the irrigation side — storage, filtration, pressure regulation, and uniform drip or micro-sprinkler coverage — matters as much as the well itself. We design and service both ends of that system.

Common Well Scenarios We See in Riverside County

The problems that bring people to call us cluster by region. Understanding which one you are dealing with usually points straight to the fix.

  • Bedrock low-yield wells in Anza, De Luz, and the Temecula highlands — a fractured-rock well that no longer keeps up with the house or grove, often a candidate for hydrofracturing to open up additional fractures.
  • Hard, mineral-rich water in the valleys and desert — scale buildup that shortens pump life, fouls pressure tanks, and clogs drip emitters.
  • Iron, manganese, and sulfur — rust staining on fixtures, a rotten-egg smell, or discolored water that needs targeted filtration.
  • Sediment and sand — common as desert and alluvial wells age or draw down, wearing pumps and plugging screens.
  • Pressure problems — a waterlogged pressure tank or failing pressure switch causing the pump to rapid-cycle, or simply not enough pressure to run an irrigation zone.
  • Drought drawdown — falling water levels during dry years that leave a pump set too high and sucking air.
  • Salinity and chloride — a real concern for avocado growers, since avocados are unusually sensitive to chloride in irrigation water.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick checks can tell you whether you have a simple fix or something that needs a technician, and they help us arrive prepared.

  1. Confirm the well breaker has not tripped at the panel, and reset it once. If it trips again, stop — that points to an electrical or pump fault.
  2. Look at the pressure gauge on the tank. Pressure that drops to zero fast or bounces wildly usually means a waterlogged tank or a bad switch.
  3. Tap the pressure tank low and high. A tank that sounds full of water all the way up has lost its air charge and needs servicing or replacement.
  4. Check whether the problem is the whole property or just one zone or fixture, which separates a well issue from a plumbing or irrigation-valve issue.
  5. Note any change in the water itself — new smell, color, grit, or staining — and roughly when it started.
  6. If you are on a grove, walk the drip lines for clogged emitters and check that storage tank levels are recovering between irrigation sets.

When to Call a Professional

Resetting a breaker once is reasonable. Beyond that, well work involves high-voltage wiring, heavy equipment hanging hundreds of feet down a borehole, and pressurized systems, so it is not a DIY job. Call us when the breaker trips repeatedly, when you have lost water entirely, when pressure will not hold, when the pump runs but no water comes, when water quality changes suddenly, or when a grove well is no longer keeping up with irrigation demand. As a licensed C-57 water well contractor with more than 30 years in Southern California, we can pull and inspect a pump, test yield, evaluate whether hydrofracturing makes sense on a bedrock well, and size treatment correctly the first time rather than throwing parts at the problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep are wells in Riverside County?

It depends heavily on where you are. Wells in the western alluvial valleys may be only a few hundred feet deep, while fractured-bedrock wells in Anza, De Luz, and the Temecula highlands often run 400 to 800 feet or more. Desert wells in the Coachella and Palo Verde valleys tap deep alluvial aquifers. We size pumps and casing to your specific location and water level.

Can avocados and citrus really be grown on wells here?

Yes. The hillside groves around De Luz, the Temecula-adjacent benchlands, and the Anza area genuinely grow avocados and citrus on private wells. Those crops need dependable summer flow and careful water quality management, which is exactly the kind of grove well and irrigation work we specialize in.

My bedrock well's yield has dropped — do I need a new well?

Not necessarily. Low-yield wells drilled into fractured granite often respond well to hydrofracturing, which uses pressurized water to open additional fractures and improve flow, typically for $3,000–$8,000. That is usually far cheaper than a new well, and we will tell you honestly whether it is a good candidate before you commit.

Why does my water stain fixtures or smell like sulfur?

Iron and manganese cause rust-colored staining, and hydrogen sulfide gas causes a rotten-egg smell. Both are common in Riverside County groundwater and both are treatable with the right filtration, usually in the $1,500–$3,500 range for a filter or softener, or $300–$900 for sediment-only issues.
