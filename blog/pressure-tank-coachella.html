How Pressure Tanks Work

A pressure tank is the buffer between your well pump and your home's plumbing. Inside the tank, a rubber bladder separates an air charge from the water supply. When the pump fills the tank, the air compresses. When you open a faucet, that compressed air pushes water through your pipes without the pump needing to run.

Your pressure switch controls the cycle — typically set at 30/50 PSI or 40/60 PSI. The pump kicks on at the lower number and shuts off at the higher number. The difference between those settings determines your "drawdown" — the amount of usable water the tank delivers between pump cycles. A properly sized tank gives you enough drawdown so the pump only cycles a handful of times per hour.

When the bladder fails or the air charge leaks out, the tank fills entirely with water — "waterlogged." Without the air cushion, every faucet turn triggers the pump. That rapid on-off cycling (short cycling) is the number one killer of well pumps. A pump rated for a few hundred cycles per day might suddenly be doing thousands, burning out the motor, overheating the wiring, and wearing out the pressure switch contacts.

Signs Your Pressure Tank Needs Service

Pressure tank problems usually start subtly and get worse over weeks or months. Here's what Coachella homeowners should watch for:

🔴 Pump Short Cycling

Your pump turns on and off every few seconds when water is running. This is the most damaging symptom and the most common sign of a waterlogged tank. In Coachella's heat, a short-cycling pump can overheat and fail within weeks rather than months.

🟡 Fluctuating Water Pressure

Pressure surges when the pump kicks on then drops rapidly. You feel pulsing in the shower or see sprinklers sputter between strong and weak flow. This often means the air charge is low but the bladder hasn't fully failed — catch it now before it gets worse.

🟡 Tank Feels Heavy All Over

Knock on your tank from top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow at the top (air) and solid at the bottom (water). If it sounds and feels heavy everywhere, the tank is waterlogged and the bladder has likely failed.

🟠 Visible Rust, Corrosion, or Leaking

Rust spots on the tank shell, weeping fittings, or water pooling around the base. In the Coachella Valley, extreme UV exposure and temperature cycling can degrade exterior coatings faster than in coastal areas, accelerating corrosion on steel tanks.

🟠 Air Sputtering from Faucets

Bursts of air mixed with water when you open faucets. The bladder has ruptured, allowing air and water to mix freely. You may also notice rusty or discolored water as the tank interior corrodes from direct water contact.

Coachella Valley Well Challenges

The Coachella Valley presents some of the toughest operating conditions for well equipment in Southern California. Understanding these challenges helps explain why professional installation and maintenance matter so much here:

  • Extreme heat stress: Summer temperatures in Coachella regularly exceed 115°F, with ground surface temperatures reaching 150°F+. Pressure tank bladders are made of rubber (butyl or EPDM), and sustained heat accelerates rubber degradation. Tanks in unshaded outdoor installations can lose 3-5 years of service life compared to shaded or indoor installations. We strongly recommend covered or insulated pump house enclosures for all Coachella installations.
  • High mineral content and hard water: The Coachella Valley aquifer produces water with elevated total dissolved solids (TDS), hardness, and sometimes arsenic or fluoride. Mineral scale builds up on pressure switch sensing ports, tank fittings, and check valves, causing false pressure readings, stuck contacts, and restricted flow. We clean and descale these components during every service call.
  • Dropping water tables: The eastern Coachella Valley has experienced declining groundwater levels due to agricultural demand and population growth. As water tables drop, pumps work harder to lift water from greater depths, putting more stress on pressure tanks and switches. Proper tank sizing becomes even more critical when your pump is already working near capacity.
  • Agricultural demand cycles: Many Coachella properties include date palm groves, citrus orchards, or other agricultural operations alongside residential use. These properties experience dramatic demand swings — minimal household use plus massive irrigation draws. Standard residential tank sizing doesn't account for these peaks.
  • Sand and sediment: Some wells in the Coachella area produce fine sand, especially as water tables decline and pump intake gets closer to the well bottom. Sand can damage bladders, clog pressure switch ports, and accumulate in the tank bottom, reducing effective capacity. Sand separators or spin-down filters upstream of the tank can prevent these issues.
  • Power quality issues: Electrical supply in rural areas of the eastern Coachella Valley can experience voltage sags during peak summer demand when the grid is strained. Low voltage damages pump motors and pressure switch coils. We recommend surge protection and motor protection devices with every installation.

Pressure Tank Maintenance Tips for Desert Climates

The Coachella Valley's extreme environment demands more attention to pressure tank maintenance than moderate climates. Here's what we recommend:

  1. Check air precharge every 6 months (not annually). Desert heat causes air to permeate through bladder walls faster. Turn off the pump, drain the tank, and check the Schrader valve with a tire gauge. Precharge should be 2 PSI below your cut-in setting (28 PSI for a 30/50 switch, 38 PSI for 40/60). Top up with a bicycle pump or small compressor as needed.
  2. Shade and insulate your tank. Direct sun on a steel pressure tank can raise internal temperatures past 140°F, degrading the bladder. A simple shade structure, reflective insulation wrap, or enclosed pump house dramatically extends tank life. If your tank sits in the open, this single improvement can add 3-5 years of service.
  3. Listen for short cycling. Run water and stand near the tank. If the pump kicks on more than 6-8 times per hour during normal use, something is wrong. In summer, a short-cycling pump can overheat within days. Don't ignore this.
  4. Inspect fittings and connections. High TDS water deposits mineral scale on threaded connections and brass fittings. Check for white or green buildup, drips, or weeping. Catch small leaks before they become big problems.
  5. Flush sand and sediment annually. If your well produces any sand, open the tank drain valve once a year and flush until the water runs clear. Accumulated sediment reduces effective tank capacity and can damage the bladder from the inside.
  6. Protect electrical components. Ensure your pressure switch has a cover to keep out dust and insects. Desert ants and wind-blown dust are notorious for causing pressure switch failures in the Coachella Valley.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size pressure tank do I need?

For most residential wells, we recommend a minimum 30-gallon pressure tank. Homes with higher water demand or multiple bathrooms benefit from 50-85 gallon tanks. Proper sizing reduces pump cycling and extends pump life.

How do I know if my pressure tank is failing?

Signs of a failing pressure tank include: pump short cycling (turning on and off frequently), waterlogged tank (heavy when you tap it), fluctuating water pressure, and the tank feeling uniformly heavy rather than having an air-filled top section.

How long do pressure tanks last?

Quality pressure tanks typically last 10-15 years. Bladder-type tanks (like Well-X-Trol) tend to last longer than diaphragm tanks. Annual pressure checks can extend tank life significantly.
