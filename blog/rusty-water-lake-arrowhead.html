Rusty Water from Your Lake Arrowhead Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Lake Arrowhead well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Lake Arrowhead well.

Casing Replacement

Plumbing Updates

Rusty Well Water in Lake Arrowhead: A Mountain-Community Guide

Lake Arrowhead sits high in the San Bernardino Mountains at roughly 5,200 feet, where dense pine forest, granite ridgelines, and a patchwork of private wells define life off the main water lines. While the village core is served by the Lake Arrowhead Community Services District, many homes in the outlying ridges and unincorporated pockets of San Bernardino County draw from private wells drilled deep into fractured granite. If you have turned on a tap in Cedar Glen, Rim Forest, or Skyforest and watched orange water swirl into the sink, you are dealing with one of the most common — and most fixable — water-quality complaints in the mountains.

Why Lake Arrowhead Well Water Turns Rusty

Discolored water comes from one of two broad sources: minerals and bacteria coming up from the ground through your well, or corrosion happening inside your own plumbing after the water is already in the house. Telling them apart is the whole game, because the fixes are completely different.

Dissolved Iron and Manganese in Granite Aquifers

The San Bernardino Mountains are built on granitic and metamorphic bedrock, and Lake Arrowhead wells pull water from fractures in that rock at depths that commonly run 300 to 800 feet — averaging around 430 feet, with static water levels often near 70 feet down. Water moving slowly through those fractures picks up dissolved iron and frequently manganese. Underground, with little oxygen, the iron stays invisible. The moment it hits air at your faucet, it oxidizes and falls out as rusty orange particles; manganese leaves brown-black staining instead. Granite aquifers vary sharply from one parcel to the next, so your neighbor's clear water tells you little about your own.

Iron Bacteria

Some mountain wells host iron bacteria — harmless-to-drink organisms that feed on dissolved iron and leave behind a slimy, reddish-brown sludge. You will often smell a swampy or oily odor and find gelatinous buildup in toilet tanks. Iron bacteria are common in older wells and in systems that sit idle, which describes a lot of Lake Arrowhead's seasonal and vacation properties.

Corroding Steel Well Casing

Many older mountain wells were completed with steel casing. After decades in the ground, that steel corrodes, and flakes of rust enter the water column directly. If your water runs clear for a moment then turns rusty, or if discoloration worsens when the pump kicks on hard, an aging casing is a prime suspect.

Galvanized Pipe and Water-Heater Corrosion

Plenty of rusty-water calls in Lake Arrowhead have nothing to do with the well at all. Cabins and homes built before the 1970s often still carry galvanized-steel supply lines. The zinc coating wears away, the steel beneath rusts from the inside, and you get brown water — usually worse at the first draw of the morning and worst at the hot tap. Your water heater is another classic culprit: the sacrificial anode rod inside eventually dissolves, and once it is gone the steel tank itself begins to rust. If only your hot water is discolored, the heater is almost certainly the source, not your well.

Is Rusty Water Safe, and How Bad Is It?

Iron and manganese are aesthetic contaminants, not acute health hazards at the concentrations found in most wells. The EPA lists secondary standards of 0.3 mg/L for iron and 0.05 mg/L for manganese — thresholds set for taste, color, and staining rather than toxicity. That said, rusty water is far from harmless to your household:

  • It stains sinks, tubs, toilets, and laundry a stubborn orange-brown
  • It tastes metallic and ruins coffee, tea, and ice
  • Iron deposits clog aerators, fixtures, and appliance valves over time
  • Iron bacteria slime can choke pressure tanks, pumps, and treatment equipment

Common Lake Arrowhead Scenarios

A handful of patterns come up again and again on our mountain service calls:

  • The reopened cabin: A property sits empty all winter, the owner returns in spring, and the first week of water runs rusty and smells off. This is usually stagnation plus iron bacteria — a shock chlorination and a few days of flushing often resolve it.
  • The hot-water-only complaint: Cold water is clear, hot water is brown. The well is fine; the water heater anode rod and tank are the problem.
  • The morning-only rust: The first draw each day is discolored, then clears. Classic sign of galvanized pipe corrosion settling overnight.

What You Can Check Before Calling

A few minutes of investigation will tell us — and you — a great deal about where the rust is coming from.

  1. Cold vs. hot: Run only the cold tap at an outside spigot or the closest fixture to where water enters the house. If the cold is clear and only the hot is rusty, your problem is the water heater, not the well.
  2. First draw vs. sustained flow: Note whether the rust appears immediately and clears, or builds the longer you run the water. Clears quickly suggests plumbing; persists or worsens suggests the well or casing.
  3. One fixture or whole house: A single rusty faucet is a local plumbing issue. Every fixture means the source is upstream — well, pressure tank, or main line.
  4. Catch a sample: Fill a clear glass and let it sit. Particles that settle to the bottom are oxidized iron or sediment; a faint clear-orange that does not settle is dissolved iron; reddish slime is iron bacteria.
  5. Check the pressure tank and toilet tanks: Open a toilet tank lid and look for orange staining or slime — a quick window into what is coming up from the well.

How the Problem Gets Fixed

The right solution depends entirely on the cause, which is why we always start with a water test and a system inspection rather than selling a filter blindly.

Iron and Manganese Filtration

For dissolved iron and manganese coming up from the aquifer, an oxidizing filtration system — air-injection or catalytic media, sometimes paired with a softener for combined hardness and iron — is the workhorse solution. These systems oxidize the metals and trap the particles before they reach your fixtures, and they self-clean on a backwash cycle.

Sediment Filtration

If the water carries gritty particles from casing flaking or aquifer fines, a properly sized sediment filter ahead of any other equipment protects the whole system and is often the cheapest meaningful upgrade.

Shock Chlorination and Well Rehabilitation

When iron bacteria are the culprit — common in idle vacation wells — shock chlorination disinfects the well, pump, and plumbing, and a follow-up flush clears the slime. Severe cases may need mechanical cleaning of the casing and screen.

Casing Repair or Replacement

A badly corroded steel casing may be lined or, in extreme cases, the well replaced — assessed with a downhole evaluation before any major work.

Plumbing and Water-Heater Repairs

If the rust originates in the house, the fix is replacing galvanized lines with PEX or copper, or replacing a corroded water heater and keeping a fresh anode rod in it going forward.

When to Call a Professional

You can flush a fixture or drain a water heater yourself. Call a licensed contractor when rusty water appears at every tap, comes with low pressure or air spitting from faucets, brings sulfur odor or bacterial slime, follows a short-cycling pump, or simply has not cleared after a day or two of flushing. Those symptoms point to the well, pump, pressure tank, or casing — components that need the right tools and a C-57 license to service safely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is only my hot water rusty in my Lake Arrowhead cabin?

When cold water runs clear and only the hot is discolored, the source is almost always your water heater. The sacrificial anode rod has dissolved and the steel tank is now corroding. Draining the tank and replacing the anode rod — or the heater itself if it is old — usually solves it, and your well is in the clear.

Is the iron in my well water dangerous to drink?

Iron is an aesthetic concern, not an acute health hazard at typical well concentrations, which is why the EPA classifies it as a secondary contaminant. It tastes metallic and stains everything, but it will not make a healthy adult sick. Elevated manganese, however, is worth treating, so a water test is the smart first move.

My water was clear for years and is now turning rusty. What changed?

Gradual onset usually means rising dissolved iron in the aquifer or a steel casing that has begun to corrode after decades underground. A sudden change can follow heavy snowmelt or a pump issue stirring up sediment. A downhole inspection and water test pinpoint which one you are dealing with.

Will a standard water softener remove rust from my well water?

A softener can handle low levels of dissolved iron along with hardness, but it is not designed for heavy iron loads or for iron bacteria. For Lake Arrowhead wells with significant iron or manganese, a dedicated oxidizing iron filter — sometimes paired with a softener — is the more reliable solution.

I just reopened my cabin after winter and the water is brown and smells bad. Help?

This is one of the most common mountain calls we get. Water that has sat stagnant all winter often grows iron bacteria and picks up odor. Shock chlorinating the well, then flushing the system for a few days, usually restores clean water. If it returns, treatment equipment may be warranted.

How deep are wells in Lake Arrowhead and does depth affect rust?

Local wells commonly run 300 to 800 feet deep into fractured granite, averaging around 430 feet. Depth itself does not cause rust, but deeper wells in low-oxygen rock tend to carry more dissolved iron and manganese that only show their color once the water reaches the surface and meets air.
