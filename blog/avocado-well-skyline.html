Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Skyline well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Urban Hillside Conditions That Shape Water Systems

Skyline's terrain of mesas, slopes and canyon edges means water pressure and irrigation behave differently than on flat ground. Higher lots and the upper floors of multi-unit buildings sometimes need a booster pump to get municipal pressure where it is needed, especially for landscape zones on steep slopes. The neighborhood's soils and the older infrastructure on some streets also make sediment and pressure fluctuations a recurring theme. While true private wells are rare here, landscape irrigation pumps, booster systems and pressure-regulation equipment are common and very much in our wheelhouse.

How Booster and Irrigation Pump Systems Work

A booster system takes existing municipal supply and raises its pressure for properties or zones that the street main cannot adequately serve. It uses a pump, a pressure tank or constant-pressure controller, and a pressure switch or transducer - the same family of components as a well system, minus the borehole. When demand rises and pressure drops, the controller starts the pump to hold a steady target pressure. For a hillside Skyline lot with extensive landscaping, a properly sized booster or constant-pressure system means even coverage across every zone instead of strong pressure near the house and a trickle at the top of the yard.

Common Scenarios We See in Skyline

  • Weak pressure on upper floors or hillside zones that a booster pump resolves.
  • Failing irrigation pumps and timers that mimic a "water-supply" problem.
  • Pressure-tank waterlogging causing booster pumps to short-cycle.
  • Sediment from older mains clogging emitters and screens.
  • Backflow and pressure-regulation issues on multi-unit and HOA properties.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  • Power. If a booster or irrigation pump is dead, check its breaker and any fuse first.
  • Controller. Many "no water to the yard" calls are really a tripped timer or a stuck zone valve.
  • Gauge. Note whether a booster system cycles between two pressures or sits flat.
  • Tank. A short-cycling, solid-sounding pressure tank is usually waterlogged.
  • Zones. Check whether the pressure problem is in one zone (valve) or system-wide (pump/supply).

When to Call a Professional

Resetting a breaker or a timer is fine. Solving Pressure Problems on Hillside Lots

Skyline's mesas and canyon slopes create a recurring challenge: water that arrives with plenty of pressure at the street can struggle to reach the top of a sloped yard or the upper floor of a multi-level home. The fix is rarely complicated once it is correctly diagnosed. A properly sized booster or constant-pressure system lifts and steadies pressure so every irrigation zone and every fixture performs the same, whether it sits at the bottom of the lot or the top of the rise. We map out where pressure drops, confirm the cause is supply rather than a clogged valve or kinked line, and size the equipment to the property rather than selling a one-size unit.
