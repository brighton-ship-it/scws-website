What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Hydrofracturing in Morongo Valley: Restoring Yield in High-Desert Wells

Morongo Valley sits at the lower, western mouth of the Morongo Basin, a small high-desert community in San Bernardino County tucked just north of the Riverside county line at roughly 2,600 feet of elevation. Water out here is not something you take for granted. Many homes along the canyon-front parcels and the scattered rural lots above Highway 62 depend on a private well, and when that well starts giving you less than it used to, the first instinct is to fear the worst — a dry hole and a five-figure bill for a brand-new well. Before you go there, it is worth understanding hydrofracturing, a well rehabilitation method that often restores production for a fraction of the cost of redrilling.

This is not the same thing as oil-and-gas fracking. Well hydrofracturing uses nothing but clean water under pressure to reopen and clean the natural fractures that already carry groundwater into your well. There are no chemicals, no proppants, and no permanent alteration of the aquifer. For the right well in the right formation — and a good portion of Morongo Valley sits on exactly that kind of formation — it can be the difference between living with a trickle and going back to a reliable supply.

Why Morongo Valley Geology Matters

The reason hydrofracturing works at all comes down to what your water is actually moving through underground. Much of the high ground around Morongo Valley is fractured granitic bedrock, the weathered and cracked crystalline rock that defines so much of the desert mountains in this part of San Bernardino County. Water does not sit in that rock like it would in a sponge; instead it travels through a network of fractures and seams. A well drilled into that rock produces water only as fast as those fractures can feed it.

Down on the valley floor and along the canyon mouths near Big Morongo Canyon Preserve, the picture changes. Here you find canyon-fed alluvium — sand, gravel, and decomposed granite washed down from the surrounding slopes. Wells completed in that loose material draw from the pore space between grains rather than from rock fractures. This distinction is the single most important thing to understand before anyone talks to you about hydrofracturing, because the technique only helps one of these two situations.

The canyon-front parcels are particularly interesting. Where Big Morongo Canyon channels groundwater down toward the valley, fractures in the bedrock can act like natural conduits, concentrating flow. A well that intercepts one of those fracture systems can be an excellent candidate for treatment, because hydrofracturing can connect the borehole to even more of that fracture network.

How Hydrofracturing Actually Works

The mechanics are straightforward once you picture them. Our crew lowers an inflatable device called a packer into your well casing and sets it at a chosen depth, sealing off a section of the borehole below it. Some treatments use a pair of packers to isolate a specific zone between them. Once that section is sealed, we pump clean water into it at high pressure — generally in the range of 1,500 to 3,000 PSI, sometimes higher depending on the rock.

That pressure does two things. It flushes out the silt, fine sediment, and mineral scale that have accumulated in existing fractures and choked off flow over the years. And it pushes open hairline fractures that were too tight to carry meaningful water, and can extend those fractures outward to intersect others. The result is a more open, better-connected fracture network feeding the well. The whole job typically runs four to eight hours on site, and because it uses only water, there is nothing left behind in your aquifer afterward.

Bedrock or Alluvium? Being Honest About Candidacy

We would rather tell you the truth up front than take a job that will not help you. Hydrofracturing is a bedrock technique. It shines in fractured granite and crystalline rock because that is where pressure can open and link fractures. If your Morongo Valley well is completed in hard, fractured bedrock and it is underperforming, you are likely a strong candidate.

If, on the other hand, your well draws mainly from loose canyon alluvium — sand and gravel basin fill — hydrofracturing is generally a poor fit. There are no rock fractures to open in unconsolidated material, and high-pressure water can actually disturb the formation in unhelpful ways. Wells like that are usually better served by other approaches: rehabilitating or replacing the screen, surging and developing the gravel pack, or addressing pump and pressure-system issues. Part of what you are paying us for is the judgment to tell those situations apart before any pressure goes downhole.

Why Bedrock Wells Lose Yield Over Time

If you have lived with the same well for a decade or two and watched it slowly weaken, there is a reason. Fractures that once carried water silt up gradually as fine particles migrate and settle. Mineral deposits — calcium, iron, manganese — precipitate out of the groundwater and coat fracture walls, slowly narrowing the pathways. Over enough time, fractures near the borehole can partially close under the weight of overlying rock.

The desert climate compounds all of this. Morongo Valley sees hot, dry summers and long stretches of drought, and when regional groundwater levels drop, the static water level in your well drops with them. A well that was always marginal can fall below the most productive fracture zones, and suddenly the yield that carried you for years no longer keeps up with summer demand. Hydrofracturing cannot create water that is not there, but in a well that is silting and scaling, it can reopen the connections to the water that still is.

What to Check and What to Expect

A good hydrofracturing job is bracketed by measurement, not guesswork. Here is how a typical project unfolds:

  • Pre-treatment flow test. We measure your well's current yield in gallons per minute and note the static and pumping water levels. This is your baseline, and without it there is no honest way to prove improvement later.
  • Video inspection. A downhole camera lets us see the casing condition, find the fracture zones, and confirm whether the well is producing from rock or from fill. This is also where we catch problems — a corroded casing or a collapsed section — that would change the plan.
  • Treatment. Packers isolate the target zone and we apply high-pressure water, often working zone by zone from the bottom up.
  • Post-treatment flow test. Once the well settles, usually within a day or two, we re-measure yield and water levels under the same conditions as the baseline.
  • Results report. You receive a documented before-and-after comparison so you know exactly what changed.

Realistically, well-suited bedrock wells often see yield improve by a factor of two to five, though every well is different and we do not promise a specific number before we have seen yours.

When to Call a Professional

A few signs are worth acting on rather than waiting out. If your well runs dry during peak summer use, if recovery after the pump shuts off has gotten noticeably slower, if the pump is short-cycling on and off, or if your overall water pressure has fallen year over year, those point to a yield problem that is unlikely to fix itself. Hydrofracturing involves serious pressure and specialized equipment, and it is not a do-it-yourself project — an improperly set packer or over-pressurized casing can damage the well. This is a job for a licensed C-57 contractor who can diagnose the cause before treating the symptom.

Realistic Costs and the New-Well Comparison

For most Morongo Valley properties, hydrofracturing runs roughly $3,000 to $8,000, depending on well depth, the number of zones treated, and access to the site. Compare that to drilling a new well in this terrain, which commonly lands between $18,000 and $42,000 once you account for depth, casing, and permitting. If the existing well also needs a new submersible pump, that is typically another $2,500 to $5,500. The math usually favors trying to rehabilitate the well you already have before committing to a new borehole — which is exactly why a pre-treatment assessment matters so much. Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Morongo Valley well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

It depends on your formation. Wells completed in fractured granitic bedrock respond well; wells drawing only from loose alluvium generally do not. We assess this first before recommending treatment.

How do I tell what my well produces from?

A driller's log plus a video inspection usually reveal whether your water comes from rock fractures or basin fill. We review both during the diagnostic visit so the decision is based on evidence, not assumptions.

What does hydrofracturing cost compared to a new well?

Hydrofracturing generally runs $3,000 to $8,000, versus $18,000 to $42,000 for a new well in this area. The $125 diagnostic fee is credited toward the job if you proceed.

How long does the process take?

Most treatments take about four to eight hours on site, followed by a post-treatment flow test a day or two later to confirm the improvement.

Are any chemicals used?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean high-pressure water to open existing fractures — nothing is added to your aquifer.
