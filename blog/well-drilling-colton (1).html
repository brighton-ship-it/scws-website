How New-Well Drilling Works in Colton

A new residential or agricultural well follows a predictable sequence, though the ground beneath Colton has its own quirks. Here is the turnkey process from start to finish:

  1. Site assessment and siting. We evaluate your parcel, review nearby well completion reports on file with the state, and locate the well a safe distance from septic systems, property lines, and the Rialto-Colton and San Jacinto fault traces that cut through the valley. Setbacks matter here because those faults act as underground barriers to groundwater flow.
  2. Permitting. San Bernardino County requires a well construction permit before drilling. We handle the paperwork, the sanitary seal specifications, and the required inspections so you don't have to navigate county offices.
  3. Mobilizing the rig. Our rotary drill rig is set up over the borehole. In Colton's valley alluvium, rotary drilling with a mud or air system is typically the most efficient method.
  4. Drilling the borehole. The bit advances through layers of gravel, sand, silt, and clay. Drillers log each formation change and watch for the water-bearing zones that produce the best yield.
  5. Setting casing. Once target depth is reached, we install steel or PVC casing to keep the hole open and seal out surface contamination. A cement sanitary seal is placed in the upper portion, and perforated screen is positioned across the productive aquifer.
  6. Gravel pack and development. A graded gravel pack is placed around the screen, then the well is developed by surging and pumping to remove drilling fines and maximize water flow.
  7. Pump installation. A properly sized submersible pump, drop pipe, wiring, and a pressure tank complete the system so you have usable water at the tap.

Our drilling fleet includes a Gefco rotary drill rig capable of reaching 1,000+ feet, and we complete most Colton wells within one to three days of active drilling. The full turnkey timeline, including permitting, pump installation, and plumbing tie-in, usually runs three to five business days once the permit clears.

Colton Geology & Water Table

Colton lies within the Rialto-Colton groundwater basin, part of the upper Santa Ana River watershed. The aquifer system here is built from valley-fill sediments, gravel, sand, silt, and clay, that can exceed 1,000 feet in total thickness. Younger alluvium near the Santa Ana River channel is shallow but highly permeable, while the older, deeper alluvium below carries the bulk of the region's stored groundwater. Two fault barriers frame the basin: the San Jacinto fault to the northeast and the Rialto-Colton fault to the southwest, both of which influence how much water moves from one block to the next.

For a Colton property owner, this means the right depth depends heavily on where your parcel sits relative to the river and the faults. Wells near the river bottoms may hit water at relatively shallow depths, while parcels on higher ground toward the mountain front generally need to go deeper to reach a dependable producing zone. Local well records show completed depths across the community ranging widely, with many residential wells landing in the roughly 150 to 400 foot range and deeper agricultural or high-demand wells extending further into the older water-bearing units.

Because groundwater accounts for the majority of drinking water in the Inland Empire, water quality is a real consideration. Colton's aquifer is generally productive, but we recommend testing every new well for nitrates, hardness, and total dissolved solids. If your results call for treatment, sediment filtration runs roughly $300 to $900, a water softener for hard mineral content runs $1,500 to $3,500, a UV disinfection system runs $800 to $1,800, and reverse osmosis for drinking water runs $300 to $1,200.

Permits, Depth & Realistic Cost

Well construction permits in Colton are issued through San Bernardino County's Division of Environmental Health Services. Turnaround varies but typically runs a couple of weeks, and we manage the entire application, seal, and inspection process for you.

Cost is the question every homeowner leads with. A complete turnkey new well in Colton generally falls between $18,000 and $42,000, depending on final depth, casing diameter, whether steel or PVC is used, formation hardness, and pump sizing. The individual components that make up a working system have their own typical ranges: a submersible pump runs $2,500 to $5,500, a control box or capacitor replacement runs $400 to $900, a pressure tank runs $600 to $1,500, a pressure switch runs $150 to $350, and a booster or constant-pressure system for consistent household pressure runs $2,000 to $4,500. A pre-purchase well inspection runs $150 to $400, and a diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward any work we perform.

We provide a written estimate before any work begins, so there are no surprises once the rig is on site.

Common Colton Scenarios

  • Replacing an aging well. Many older Colton wells were completed decades ago with undersized casing or worn screens. When yield drops, a new well beside the old one often makes more sense than repeated repairs.
  • New rural construction. Building on unincorporated land near Reche Canyon or the Colton hills usually means no municipal hookup, so a private well is part of the plan from day one.
  • Agricultural irrigation. Citrus, pasture, and small vineyards in the area need higher-yield wells and often a larger pump and constant-pressure setup.
  • Drought resilience. Owners who already have city water sometimes add a private well to reduce cost and stay supplied during allocation cutbacks.
