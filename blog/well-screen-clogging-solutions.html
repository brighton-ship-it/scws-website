Understanding Well Screen Function and Importance

The well screen is your water supply's filter, allowing water to flow into the well while keeping sediment out. Located at the water-bearing zone, screen openings are precisely sized for your aquifer's geology—small enough to block sand and gravel while permitting maximum water flow. Surrounding the screen, a gravel pack provides additional filtration. When screens clog, this filtration becomes restriction. Water must force its way through increasingly smaller openings, reducing flow and increasing pumping energy. The pump works harder for less output, shortening its life. Unlike pump problems that announce themselves suddenly, screen clogging develops gradually and can go unnoticed until severe decline occurs. Regular monitoring catches clogging before it becomes critical.

Diagnosing Screen Clogging vs. Other Problems

Accurate diagnosis prevents wasting money on the wrong repairs. Screen clogging typically shows gradual flow decline over months or years—not sudden changes. If your pump suddenly produces less water, suspect pump problems rather than screens. Compare current output to historical records if available. Measure specific capacity (flow rate divided by drawdown) before and after pumping; declining specific capacity indicates well efficiency problems like screen clogging. A video inspection visually confirms screen condition, showing mineral buildup, bacterial slime, or clear openings. Water quality tests revealing high iron, manganese, or bacterial counts suggest conditions prone to screen fouling. Professional well evaluation distinguishes screen problems from pump issues, dropping water tables, or other causes.

Professional Cleaning Methods and Their Applications

Different clogging types require different treatment approaches. Chemical treatment targets mineral and bacterial deposits: acid treatments dissolve calcium and iron scale while chlorine treatments kill bacteria and disperse organic buildup. Chemicals are introduced into the well, allowed contact time, then thoroughly pumped out. Mechanical cleaning physically removes deposits using specialized brushes, surge blocks, or high-pressure jetting. Surging rapidly moves water in and out through the screen, loosening material that gravity alone won't dislodge. Most professional cleanings combine methods—chemical treatment loosens deposits, followed by mechanical action to remove them. Well development pumping then removes loosened material from the well. Proper cleaning requires knowledge of your specific water chemistry to select appropriate treatments.

DIY Limitations and When to Call Professionals

Well screen cleaning is inherently professional work. The equipment required—surge blocks, jetting apparatus, chemical injection systems—isn't available to homeowners. More importantly, improper cleaning can damage screens, contaminate the well, or create safety hazards. Chemical treatments involve concentrated acids and chlorine requiring careful handling and neutralization. Working in wells poses drowning and equipment-drop risks. However, homeowners can prevent clogging through water treatment and regular monitoring. If you suspect screen clogging based on declining flow, don't delay professional evaluation. Early intervention when screens are partially clogged achieves better results than waiting until flow drops dramatically. Prevention Strategies for Long-term Screen Health

Prevention starts with proper initial well construction and development—ensuring screens are correctly sized and the well thoroughly developed removes formation fines. For existing wells, water treatment addressing problematic minerals reduces deposition in screens. Iron filters, water softeners, and chlorination systems treat water after it leaves the well but indicate conditions that also affect screens. Schedule periodic professional cleaning based on your water chemistry rather than waiting for problems. Maintain records of flow rates and pump performance to detect gradual decline. Avoid over-pumping, which can accelerate fine sediment migration toward screens. For wells with chronic clogging issues, consider rehabilitation options like screen replacement with more appropriate materials or sizing. Consistent maintenance extends well life and maintains optimal production capacity.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes well screens to clog?

Well screens clog from several sources: mineral deposits (especially iron and manganese), bacterial growth (iron bacteria create slimy buildup), sand and silt accumulation, and scale formation from hard water. In Southern California, calcium and magnesium deposits are common. Screens can also clog from well age as the gravel pack surrounding them deteriorates. Improper well development initially can leave fine sediments that progressively block screen openings over time.

How do I know if my well screen is clogged?

Symptoms of clogged screens include gradually declining water flow, reduced well yield, pump running longer to deliver the same water volume, and increasing drawdown (water level drops more during pumping). You might notice decreased pressure at fixtures or longer pump cycle times. Unlike sudden pump failure, screen clogging produces gradual symptoms over months or years. If output has declined 20% or more from original capacity, screen clogging should be investigated.

Can clogged well screens be cleaned?

Yes, several cleaning methods restore clogged screens. Chemical treatment dissolves mineral deposits and bacterial buildup using acids, chlorine, or specialty chemicals. Mechanical cleaning uses brushes, scrapers, or surge blocks to physically remove deposits. High-pressure jetting blasts debris from screen openings. Surging alternately pushes and pulls water through the screen, loosening accumulated material. Combination approaches using chemicals followed by mechanical cleaning often achieve best results. Professional cleaning can restore 70-90% of original capacity in many cases.

How often should well screens be cleaned?

Cleaning frequency depends on water chemistry and local conditions. Wells with high iron content or bacterial issues may need cleaning every 3-5 years. Wells in clean aquifers might go 10-15 years between cleanings. Monitor your specific flow rate over time—when output drops 15-20% from baseline, schedule cleaning. Proactive maintenance cleaning before severe clogging is more effective and less costly than waiting for major decline. Annual water tests help predict maintenance needs.

Is well screen replacement ever necessary?

Yes, severely deteriorated or damaged screens may require replacement rather than cleaning. Screens can corrode, develop holes (allowing sand entry), or become so encrusted that cleaning cannot restore function. Screen replacement is a significant project requiring pulling the pump and potentially rehabilitating the well. However, it's still typically less expensive than drilling a new well. A video inspection determines whether cleaning or replacement is the appropriate approach.
