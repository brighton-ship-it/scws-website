How a Well System Supplies an Avocado Grove

A grove irrigation system is a chain, and every link has to hold. Understanding the parts helps you spot trouble before your trees pay for it.

The Well and Pump

Most agricultural wells in the county draw from fractured granite and decomposed-granite aquifers, so depth and yield vary block to block. A submersible pump sized to your well's safe yield lifts water to the surface. Oversizing the pump is a classic mistake: pull water faster than the formation recharges and you draw the well down, pump air, and burn out the motor. We test recovery rate and match the pump to what the well can sustain, typically with a flow-control or a cycle-sensing setup to protect against dry running.

Pressure, Storage, and Delivery

Few grove wells produce enough instantaneous flow to irrigate directly, so growers store water. A storage tank or pond buffers the gap between the well's slow, steady output and the high flow an irrigation set needs for a few hours. From storage, a booster pump pressurizes the lines. A pressure tank and pressure switch smooth out cycling on smaller systems, while constant-pressure (variable-frequency) booster pumps hold steady PSI across changing demand, which keeps emitter output uniform from the first tree to the last.

The Irrigation System Itself

Drip lines and micro-sprinklers dominate avocado irrigation because they apply water slowly and precisely to the shallow, oxygen-hungry avocado root zone. Uniform pressure and clean water keep emitters from clogging. Filtration at the wellhead and at the manifold protects those tiny orifices from the sand and mineral floc that San Diego County groundwater often carries.

Water Quality: The Make-or-Break Factor for Avocados

No tree crop is fussier about water chemistry than the avocado. Before you invest in trees, and every year afterward, the water itself deserves a hard look.

Chloride and Salinity

Avocados are exceptionally sensitive to chloride. Growers generally aim to keep irrigation-water chloride below roughly 100 ppm; above that, leaves develop the classic tip burn, drop early, and yields fall. Sodium and overall salinity (measured as electrical conductivity) compound the damage, especially on salt-sensitive rootstocks. In parts of inland San Diego County, native groundwater carries enough chloride that growers blend well water with lower-salinity imported water, apply extra leaching irrigation to flush salts below the root zone, and choose salt-tolerant rootstocks. We test for chloride, sodium, EC, and the sodium adsorption ratio so you know exactly what you are putting on your trees.

Hardness, Iron, and Manganese

Hard, mineral-rich water is the norm in the backcountry. Calcium and magnesium scale shortens pump life and crusts emitters. Iron and manganese stain, feed bacterial slime, and plug drip lines from the inside. Sediment and sand wear impellers and clog filters. Each of these has a treatment answer, from sediment filtration to iron and manganese removal, but the right fix starts with an accurate water test rather than a guess.

Common Scenarios We See Around San Diego County Groves

  • Summer drawdown. Demand peaks exactly when the aquifer is lowest. A well that coasted through spring suddenly pumps sand or runs dry mid-afternoon.
  • Creeping chloride. Leaf tip burn shows up across the grove; testing reveals salinity has climbed as the water table dropped and concentrated.
  • Clogged emitters. Iron bacteria or fine sediment slowly starve the far end of a drip block, and trees there decline before anyone connects it to water quality.
  • Aging pump and tank. Short-cycling, fluctuating pressure, and rising power bills point to a waterlogged pressure tank or a failing pump on a grove that was planted decades ago.
  • Power-quality damage. Rural feeders sag and surge, cooking pump motors and control boxes that lack proper protection.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Note whether the whole grove or only one zone has weak flow; a single zone usually means a line, valve, or filter, not the well.
  2. Look at your pressure gauge while the booster runs. Wild swings or rapid on-off cycling suggest a tank or switch problem.
  3. Listen for the pump short-cycling or for air spitting from emitters, both signs the well may be drawing down.
  4. Check filters and screens for sand, rust-colored slime, or scale.
  5. Watch the trees: uniform tip burn points to water chemistry, while patchy decline points to delivery or clogging.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does an avocado grove really need?

A mature tree uses 40 to 70 gallons per day in summer heat, and a producing acre typically needs four to six acre-feet over a year. Peak demand during fruit set and sizing is the real test of a well, which is why we size pumps and storage around summer flow, not spring averages.

Why are my avocado leaves showing tip burn?

Uniform leaf-tip burn across the grove is the signature of chloride and salt accumulation. We test your well for chloride, sodium, and electrical conductivity, then help you decide between blending, extra leaching irrigation, or treatment to protect the trees.

Can a low-yielding well still support a grove?

Often, yes. By pairing the well with adequate storage and a properly sized booster, a modest but steady well can irrigate a surprising amount of acreage. Where yield is truly inadequate, hydrofracturing or a new well may be the better long-term investment.

How do I keep my drip emitters from clogging?

Clean water and good filtration are the answer. Sediment filters handle sand, while iron and manganese removal stops the bacterial slime that plugs lines from the inside. We match the filtration to your actual water test.

Do you handle both the well and the irrigation pumping?

Yes. We service the well, the submersible pump, storage and booster systems, pressure controls, and water treatment, so one licensed C-57 contractor covers the whole chain that feeds your grove.

How fast can you respond if my grove loses water in summer?

We offer same-day emergency service across San Diego County. When trees are at risk during peak heat, call right away and we will prioritize getting water back to your grove.
