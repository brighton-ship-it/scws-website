What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mountain Center Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Mountain Center wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Improving Well Yield at the Mountain Center Crossroads

Mountain Center sits where Highway 74 meets Highway 243 at about 4,500 feet, the crossroads that ties together Idyllwild, Garner Valley, and Lake Hemet in the San Jacinto Mountains of Riverside County. It's a small mountain community of homes, cabins, and rural parcels, and like its neighbors it runs almost entirely on private wells drilled into the granitic bedrock of the range. When a Mountain Center well slows down — sputtering during a shower, running short by late summer — the trouble is rarely a dry aquifer. It's usually a fracture network that has narrowed or clogged with sediment. Hydrofracturing reopens and extends those fractures, and for most owners it's a far cheaper fix than drilling a new well.

The Granite That Holds Your Water

The San Jacinto Mountains are a massive uplifted block of granitic rock, mostly granodiorite and quartz diorite, cut by joints, faults, and fracture sets. This rock has almost no pore space, so groundwater travels through it only along those breaks. A Mountain Center well produces water where the borehole crosses water-bearing fractures, and its yield depends entirely on how many it intersects. That's why two wells on nearby lots can perform so differently — and why simply drilling deeper into solid rock often adds cost without adding water. Hydrofracturing works outward from the existing bore to reach more of the fracture system.

How Hydrofracturing Works on Hard Rock

We seat inflatable packers in the open-hole portion of the well to isolate a section of rock, then inject clean water — never chemicals — at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. That pressure flushes mineral scale and silt from existing fractures and propagates them deeper into the formation, often linking the well to productive seams just beyond its original reach. On the San Jacinto's competent granite, reopened fractures tend to hold and keep producing. A typical Mountain Center job takes four to eight hours, with flow measured before and after so the gain is documented.

Why Mountain Center Wells Lose Capacity

Groundwater here is recharged mostly by winter rain and snow, while demand climbs through the dry summer and fall. A well with a limited fracture network can draw down faster than the rock refills it, leaving owners short exactly when they need water most. Over time, mineral deposits in granitic groundwater also settle into the narrowest, most productive cracks closest to the bore, steadily throttling flow. Fracturing addresses both: it clears the clogged fractures and opens new ones, giving the well stronger inflow and more storage to carry it through the dry season.

Signs Your Well May Be a Candidate

  • The well produced more water a few years ago than it does today.
  • The pump short-cycles or runs dry during back-to-back household use.
  • Recovery is slow — the well takes many hours to refill after heavy use.
  • You ration water every late summer until the winter storms recharge the mountain.
  • A recently drilled well hit hard rock but never delivered the flow you expected.

What to Check Before Calling

Locate your well completion report if you can and note the total depth, casing depth, and the original measured yield. Watch your system over a week: when does pressure fade, and how long until it recovers? Rule out the simple causes first, because a waterlogged pressure tank, a tired pump, or a failed check valve can imitate a low-yield well. We confirm all of that during the assessment so you never pay to fracture a well that only needed a new pump.

When to Bring In a Professional

Hydrofracturing uses high pressure and specialized down-hole equipment and is never a do-it-yourself project. We begin with a pre-treatment flow test and a downhole video inspection to verify your casing is sound and to map the fracture zones worth targeting. That inspection also catches problems — casing corrosion or a collapsed section — that must be addressed first. A licensed contractor's assessment ensures you spend money on the fix that will actually solve your water shortage.

A Gateway Community With Seasonal Demand

As the junction that funnels traffic up to Idyllwild and out across Garner Valley, Mountain Center includes its share of cabins, vacation homes, and short-term rentals. These properties can see water demand swing sharply with the seasons, and a well sized for quiet occupancy may fall short when a rental fills up every summer weekend. Before assuming such a well is finished, it's worth testing whether fracturing can raise its capacity to meet the heavier use — in sound rock it frequently does.

A Common Scenario We See

One pattern we encounter is the older mountain home converted from seasonal to full-time living. The original well was drilled for weekend use, and once a family is there year-round the daily load of laundry, showers, and landscape watering outpaces it. Rather than abandon a structurally sound well, fracturing often lifts its yield enough to support full-time occupancy, sparing the owner the cost and permitting delay of a new bore.

Good documentation is one of the cheapest ways to protect your home's value.

How the San Jacinto Faults Shape Local Wells

Mountain Center lies near the heart of the San Jacinto fault system, one of the most active fault zones in Southern California, and that tectonic history is good news for well owners. The repeated movement along these faults has shattered and fractured the granite over geologic time, creating the very crack networks that store and transmit groundwater. Productive wells in this area almost always tap into a fracture set or shear zone associated with that faulting. When a well sits close to such a zone but only clips its edge, hydrofracturing can extend the borehole's connection into the more heavily fractured rock nearby, often turning a marginal well into a dependable one. Understanding where the productive fractures lie is exactly what our pre-treatment video inspection is designed to reveal, so the high-pressure injection can be aimed where it will do the most good rather than applied blindly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much more water can hydrofracturing produce?

It depends on the rock, but successful treatments in San Jacinto granite often raise yield two to five times over the pre-treatment rate. We measure flow before and after so the improvement is documented.

Are any chemicals used?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water. Nothing is introduced into the ground that could affect your water quality.

Can it be done in winter?

Sometimes, though snow at this elevation can delay access. Many owners schedule the work from late spring through fall when roads are clear.

How long do the results last?

In sound granite, gains commonly last many years. A well may eventually benefit from a second treatment, but most owners enjoy a long, stable run of improved flow.

Is this like oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses clean water at far lower pressures and volumes and targets a shallow domestic borehole, with no chemicals or sand proppants.

What if my well isn't suited to it?

We'll tell you honestly and recommend the right alternative. We don't fracture wells in loose formations or with compromised casing.
