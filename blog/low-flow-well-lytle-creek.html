Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

When Your Lytle Creek Well Cannot Keep Up

Lytle Creek sits in a narrow canyon community at the foot of the San Gabriel and San Bernardino Mountains, in San Bernardino County, where the year-round creek and its broad alluvial wash define the local water picture. Many homes here draw from the Lytle Creek wash and the upper and lower Lytle groundwater basins, where Pleistocene-age older alluvium is the principal water-bearing unit and supplies water to more than a thousand wells across the region. That sounds reassuring until you live through a dry stretch and watch your faucet slow to a trickle. A low-flow or low-yield well is one that simply cannot deliver enough gallons per minute to keep up with how your household actually uses water, and in a canyon community like Lytle Creek the causes are usually a mix of geology, well age, and pump configuration.

The good news is that low yield is one of the most solvable well problems we handle. Before you assume the well is "going dry," it is worth measuring exactly what you have, understanding why the number is what it is, and then matching the right fix to your situation. Below we walk through how we evaluate and improve low-flow wells specifically for Lytle Creek properties.

How to Measure Your Well's Real Yield (GPM)

"Low flow" is a measurable thing, not a feeling. Yield is expressed in gallons per minute (GPM), and there are two numbers that matter: the rate your pump delivers at the faucet, and the rate your aquifer can actually sustain over hours of pumping. A simple kitchen test gives you the first number: run an outside hydrant into a five-gallon bucket and time how long it takes to fill. Twenty seconds to fill five gallons is 15 GPM; if it takes a full minute, you are at 5 GPM; two minutes means roughly 2.5 GPM.

That bucket test only tells part of the story, though, because a pressure tank can mask a weak well by storing water between cycles. The more important measurement is a sustained well yield test, where we draw the water level down and watch how fast the well recovers. A well that pours out 12 GPM for three minutes and then collapses to 1 GPM has a storage problem and an aquifer-yield problem, and the cure for each is different. A typical Lytle Creek single-family home needs 3 to 5 GPM of dependable supply; larger households and properties with irrigation or livestock need considerably more. Wells in the alluvial wash often perform well, but properties up the canyon slopes that tap fractured bedrock can produce as little as 1 to 3 GPM.

Common Causes of Low Yield in Lytle Creek

When we diagnose a slow well in this area, the cause almost always falls into one of these categories:

  • Genuinely low aquifer yield. Properties drawing from thin or fine-grained zones of the older alluvium, or from fractured bedrock above the wash, may simply have a formation that releases water slowly. This is geology, not a defect, and it calls for managing supply rather than chasing a bigger pump.
  • A declining or seasonal water table. Lytle Creek's supply is recharged by snowmelt and storm runoff. After dry winters the static water level can drop, leaving less saturated screen for the pump to draw from. Wells that were marginal to begin with feel this first.
  • Clogged or encrusted well screen. The mineral-rich water common in San Bernardino County deposits calcium and iron scale on the screen slots and gravel pack over years of service. As those openings close, the well chokes even though the aquifer itself is unchanged.
  • Biofouling. Iron and sulfur bacteria build slimy colonies that plug screens and reduce flow, often alongside a rotten-egg odor or rusty staining.
  • A pump set at the wrong depth or sized wrong. A pump hung too high in the casing loses its draw the moment the water level dips, and an oversized pump can pull a weak well down faster than it recovers, causing short cycling and air in the lines.

Solutions, and the Tradeoffs Between Them

There is rarely one "right" answer. The best path depends on whether your problem is the aquifer, the well, or the equipment.

Well Rehabilitation and Cleaning

If scale or biofouling is strangling a screen, mechanical brushing, surging, and chemical treatment can restore much of the original yield. Rehabilitation is the most cost-effective first move when an older well has gradually slowed, typically running $1,500 to $5,000. It will not create water that the aquifer cannot supply, but it frequently recovers flow that was quietly lost to encrustation.

Hydrofracturing

For wells finished in the fractured bedrock above the wash, hydrofracturing injects high-pressure water to open and connect existing fractures, often doubling or tripling yield. It is most effective in hard-rock wells and runs roughly $3,000 to $8,000. In a true alluvial well it offers little, which is why an accurate diagnosis comes first.

Deepening the Well

Drilling deeper can reach more productive zones, but in the layered Lytle Creek alluvium "deeper" does not guarantee "wetter," and below the older alluvium you may hit low-permeability material. We recommend deepening only when geology and nearby well logs support it.

Storage Cistern Plus a Booster or Constant-Pressure Pump

For chronic low yield, this is the workhorse solution and the one we most often recommend. Instead of fighting the aquifer, you let a slow well do what it does best: trickle water around the clock into a large storage cistern, often 2,500 to 5,000 gallons. A booster pump or constant-pressure system then draws from that tank to deliver strong, steady household pressure on demand. A 2 GPM well running continuously yields nearly 2,900 gallons a day, far more than a typical home uses, so the tank absorbs your peak morning and evening demand while the well quietly refills it. Expect roughly $3,000 to $7,000 installed depending on tank size and controls. The tradeoff is footprint and a bit more equipment to maintain, but it transforms a frustrating well into a dependable one without touching the geology.

Right-Sizing the Pump

Sometimes the fix is simply matching the pump to the well, lowering it to the correct depth, or installing a flow-control valve so it cannot outrun the aquifer. A correctly sized pump and a properly charged pressure tank cost $2,500 to $5,500 and resolve a surprising number of "low water" complaints.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Lytle Creek well is low yield or just has a bad pump?

A sustained yield test settles it. If the well draws down quickly and recovers slowly, the aquifer is the limit; if water level holds steady but pressure is weak, the pump or pressure tank is the culprit. Our $125 diagnostic measures both.

Will a bigger pump fix my low-flow well?

Usually not. A larger pump cannot pull more water than the formation releases, and oversizing often makes things worse by drawing the well down and causing it to short cycle. Matching the pump to the well, or adding storage, is the real fix.

Is a storage tank and booster pump really better than drilling deeper?

For chronic low yield it often is. A storage cistern lets a slow well fill it around the clock, then a booster delivers full pressure on demand. It avoids the cost and uncertainty of deepening, which in Lytle Creek's layered alluvium may not reach better water.

How much does it cost to improve a low-yield well in Lytle Creek?

Rehabilitation runs $1,500 to $5,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, a storage-plus-booster system $3,000 to $7,000, and a right-sized pump and tank $2,500 to $5,500. The right number depends on your diagnosis.

Does Lytle Creek's water table change with the seasons?

Yes. The basin is recharged by snowmelt and storm runoff, so static water levels can fall after dry winters and recover in wet ones. Marginal wells feel this most, which is why storage capacity adds valuable resilience here.
