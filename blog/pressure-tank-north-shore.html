The Job a Pressure Tank Does

A pressure tank is a sealed vessel split into two sections by a rubber bladder or diaphragm. One side stores water, the other holds compressed air. When the pump sends water into the tank, it compresses that air, and the stored pressure is what delivers water to your fixtures after the pump turns off. The air chamber is set with a pre-charge, an air reading taken while the tank is empty of water, and getting that pre-charge right is the foundation of a well-behaved system.

The pump is controlled by a pressure switch with a cut-in and a cut-out value, commonly 30/50 or 40/60 PSI. The pump starts at the cut-in and stops at the cut-out. The water you can draw between those points before the pump restarts is the drawdown, and it is much less than the tank's total volume. Drawdown is what guards the pump from harm: each start and stop produces wear and heat, so a tank with healthy drawdown lets the pump run in long, efficient cycles rather than firing constantly. In a remote area like North Shore, where a replacement pump is a costly and time-consuming fix, protecting it is well worth the attention.

Why Pressure Tanks Fail Around the Salton Sea

The heat and hard water of this below-sea-level basin push tanks toward several familiar failure modes. The ones we see most often near North Shore are:

  • Waterlogging: A leaking or depleted bladder lets water fill the entire tank, erasing the air cushion and leaving no reserve for the pump.
  • Short-cycling: A waterlogged or undersized tank makes the pump switch on and off in rapid bursts, the most harmful condition a pump can face and the most urgent to fix.
  • Ruptured bladder: Years of heat and mineral exposure fatigue the diaphragm until it tears, ending the tank's ability to hold a separate air charge.
  • Lost air charge: Air gradually escapes from even a healthy tank, and a low pre-charge makes a large tank act like a small one.
  • Fouled air valve: Scale and corrosion can clog the Schrader valve, blocking any check or adjustment of the pre-charge.
  • Corrosion: Brutal desert temperatures and mineral content rust tank shells inside and out, often appearing first at the base seam.

What You Can Check Yourself

Before you schedule a visit, a quick inspection can narrow things down. Tap the tank with a wrench from top to bottom. The upper portion should sound hollow where the air sits, and the lower portion should sound solid where the water is. If it sounds solid all the way up, the tank has waterlogged.

To test the pre-charge, cut power to the pump, open a faucet, and drain the tank completely so the air side is empty. Press a tire gauge onto the Schrader valve at the top. The reading should land about 2 PSI below your cut-in, roughly 28 PSI on a 30/50 system or 38 PSI on a 40/60 system. If it reads well under that, or water sprays from the valve, the bladder has failed. Also inspect the pressure switch contacts and watch a single open faucet: if the flow pulses while the pump audibly cycles on and off in quick succession, short-cycling is confirmed.

Sizing and Pre-Charge for North Shore Properties

The right tank size keeps the pump running at least one to two minutes per cycle. Larger tanks store more drawdown and cut cycling dramatically, which is why we often recommend up-sizing for North Shore parcels that run irrigation or serve a busy household. A small home may be fine with a 20 to 32-gallon tank, while a property with agricultural draws, livestock water, or simultaneous heavy demand is better served by a 62, 86, or 119-gallon tank.

Whatever capacity you choose, the pre-charge must sit roughly 2 PSI below the cut-in pressure. That pairing between pre-charge and switch settings is what lets the tank deliver its full rated drawdown. Set the pre-charge wrong and even a new tank will short-cycle and wear out the pump. We set and verify the pre-charge on every install so the system runs the way it was designed to.

When to Bring in a Professional

Call us if your pump will not shut off, if you have lost water entirely, if the tank shows rust or is leaking, or if you re-charged the air and the short-cycling returned within days. Those last symptoms typically indicate a ruptured bladder or a deeper pump fault rather than a simple air loss. We also recommend professional sizing any time you replace a tank, since matching the old one can repeat an original undersizing, a common issue on rural wells that have grown into heavier demand over the years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does living below sea level near the Salton Sea affect my well system?

The low elevation itself is not the issue; the heat and hard, mineral-rich groundwater of the basin are what wear on tanks and pumps. Those conditions make annual maintenance especially worthwhile around North Shore.

How often should I check the air charge?

About once a year. A quick pre-charge check with the pump off and the tank drained is the simplest way to catch a slow air loss before it turns into short-cycling.

My water pressure surges then drops. What causes that?

Fluctuating pressure that recovers usually points to a tank losing its air charge or a bladder beginning to fail. It is worth checking the pre-charge and inspecting the tank before the problem worsens.

Can I repair a ruptured bladder instead of replacing the tank?

In most residential tanks the bladder is not serviceable, so a ruptured bladder means replacing the tank. We can confirm the diagnosis and recommend a properly sized unit.

Why does my pump run constantly in summer?

Heavy summer demand combined with a weak or undersized tank can leave the pump running almost nonstop. The fix is usually restoring the air charge or moving to a larger tank with more drawdown.

Yes. As a full-service, C-57 licensed well company, we handle both rural residential and agricultural well systems across the eastern Coachella Valley, from the pressure tank through the pump.
