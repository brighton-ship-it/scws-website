Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your University Heights well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How a Private Well System Works

Every private well, whether it irrigates a Valley Center grove or supplies a single backcountry home, relies on the same chain of components. A submersible pump lifts groundwater from the casing. A pressure tank and pressure switch keep household and irrigation pressure steady without the pump cycling constantly. A storage tank often buffers demand on agricultural sites. And filtration or treatment removes sediment, iron, manganese or excess minerals before the water reaches your fixtures or emitters.

San Diego County's backcountry geology — decomposed granite over fractured crystalline bedrock — means many wells draw from rock fractures rather than a deep sandy aquifer. That makes correct pump sizing, adequate storage and steady pressure especially important, which is where a constant-pressure or booster system ($2,000 to $4,500) frequently earns its keep.

Why Local Geology Matters

An out-of-area contractor who never works our bedrock will misjudge depth, yield and drawdown. We have logged thousands of county wells and know how a fracture-fed well behaves through a dry summer, so we can size and service your system to last.

Common Well Problems Across the County

  • Dropping pressure from a waterlogged pressure tank or a worn switch.
  • Pump failure after 12 to 15 years of service, often with no warning if not monitored.
  • Sediment and turbidity that clogs filters, fixtures and irrigation emitters.
  • Iron, manganese or sulfur staining fixtures and giving water a metallic taste or rotten-egg smell.
  • Declining yield as bedrock fractures mineralize and slowly close over years.

What You Can Check Before Calling

  1. Does the pump rapidly click on and off? That short-cycling usually means a tank or switch problem.
  2. Is pressure low everywhere, or only at distant fixtures? Whole-system low pressure points upstream toward the pump.
  3. Has the water changed color, clarity or smell? Note it — each symptom narrows the diagnosis.
  4. Check your breaker. A tripped pump circuit can mean a simple reset or a failing motor drawing too much current.

Leave the well head, wiring and pump pulling to professionals; high voltage and confined-space risks make DIY dangerous.

Maintaining a Private Well Anywhere in the County

Wherever your well sits — a Valley Center grove, an Alpine homestead, or a Pauma Valley ranch — the maintenance fundamentals are the same, and they are what keep you out of an emergency. An annual service visit checks pump amperage and performance, pressure-tank pre-charge, switch operation and water quality. We also recommend a periodic flow test, because a slow decline in yield is far easier to address before it becomes a dry tap.

Water testing deserves special mention. Backcountry groundwater chemistry changes over time and after wet or dry years, and iron, manganese, hardness or bacteria can creep in gradually. A yearly test catches those shifts while they are still cheap to treat, protecting both your plumbing and your filtration investment.

The Payoff of Prevention

  • Pumps that get monitored tend to be replaced on a planned schedule, not during a holiday-weekend failure.
  • Pressure problems caught early are usually a $150 switch, not a burned-out motor.
  • Treated water means fewer clogged fixtures and longer equipment life across the board.

You never need to handle the well head or wiring yourself. Your job is to notice changes in pressure, clarity, taste or smell; ours is to measure and fix them safely.

Understanding San Diego County Groundwater

San Diego County is geologically diverse, and that variety shapes every well in it. The coastal mesas where neighborhoods like University Heights sit give way, moving east, to the rolling foothills around Ramona and Valley Center and then to the granite highlands of Julian and the Cuyamacas. Each zone behaves differently underground. Coastal sediments can hold shallow water but carry more salts; the inland decomposed-granite and fractured-bedrock country holds water in cracks and fissures, producing wells whose yield depends heavily on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole happens to intersect.

This is why a number that is normal for one property can be alarming for another. A 15-gallon-per-minute well might be excellent in one canyon and merely adequate a mile away. When we evaluate a private well, we interpret its measurements against the specific geology of its location rather than a county-wide average, which is how we give you advice you can actually plan around.

Seasonal and multi-year cycles matter too. After a string of dry years, static water levels across the backcountry drop and some shallower wells struggle; a few wet winters can recharge them substantially. We factor that hydrologic context into every recommendation, so you are planning around the trend, not just a single day's reading.
