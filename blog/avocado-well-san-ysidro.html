Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your San Ysidro well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How a Well and Irrigation System Works

On a property that runs on a private well, a submersible pump set in the casing lifts water to a pressure tank that stores a pressurized reserve, so the pump does not restart every time a valve opens. A pressure switch controls the pump. Larger irrigated parcels add a storage tank that the well fills steadily, with the irrigation system drawing from the tank during peak hours. Even properties on city water sometimes use booster or constant-pressure systems to push adequate flow to distant irrigation zones, hillside landscaping, or large gardens. The lower Tijuana River Valley sits on river-deposited alluvium, so wells here typically tap sand and gravel rather than rock fractures.

For the agricultural and nursery operations on the valley floor, peak summer demand drives the design. Whatever the crop, the principle is the same as it is for a thirsty avocado tree elsewhere in the county: size the pump and storage to the hottest week of the year, not the average, so irrigation never falls behind when plants need water most.

Common Water-System Scenarios in the Area

  • Sand and sediment. Valley-floor alluvial wells move fine sand that wears pumps and clogs drip emitters and filters.
  • Salinity near the coast and border. Low-lying valleys near the coast can carry elevated salinity in groundwater, which matters for salt-sensitive plantings.
  • Booster-pump issues. Properties relying on pressure-boosting systems lose flow when a pump or pressure tank fails.
  • Hard water and scale. Mineral-rich groundwater scales tanks, fittings, and irrigation hardware over time.
  • Aging or unused wells. Older valley properties sometimes have legacy wells that need rehabilitation, testing, or proper decommissioning.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  • Check the pressure gauge — normal cycling runs roughly 40 to 60 psi; a bouncing needle suggests a waterlogged tank or failing switch.
  • Look for sand or grit in filter housings or at faucets, a sign of a worn pump or screen.
  • Notice whether irrigation zones have lost pressure or coverage, which can point to a booster, filter, or emitter problem.
  • Listen for a pump that short cycles, kicking on every few seconds, which threatens the motor.
  • Confirm breakers and the pressure switch before assuming a major failure.

Seasonal Care for a Valley or Landscape Well

The lower Tijuana River Valley's mild winters and hot, dry summers still put a well through a full annual cycle. After winter rains, run a flow test while the water table is high to learn your true yield. In spring, service the pressure tank, clean sediment filters, and flush irrigation lines of scale and grit. Through summer, watch pump run times for the slow creep that signals a tiring pump or a dropping level. Test water chemistry every year or two, especially for salinity in these low coastal valleys. Sizing storage generously keeps irrigation steady through heat waves.
