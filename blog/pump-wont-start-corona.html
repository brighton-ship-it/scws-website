Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Common Reasons a Well Pump Won't Start

When a Corona well pump goes silent, the cause is usually one of these:

  • Tripped breaker or blown fuse — no power to the pump. The most frequent cause of all. A surge, a loose connection, or a struggling motor can drop the dedicated pump circuit.
  • Failed pressure switch or clogged sensing port. The pressure switch tells the pump to start. Temescal-area groundwater is mineral-rich, and that scale can plug the switch's small sensing port so the pump is never commanded on.
  • Burned-out capacitor or control box fault. Start capacitors and relays in the surface control box weaken with age and summer heat, and are a leading no-start cause.
  • Failed or seized motor or pump. Years of cycling, plus mineral buildup from hard Santa Ana watershed water, can wear bearings or short a winding until the motor won't turn.
  • Wiring and corrosion faults. Loose terminals, corroded splices, and rodent-chewed wire in junction boxes interrupt the circuit — especially on foothill parcels with long wiring runs.
  • Stuck check valve. A check valve frozen closed can stall the pump or trip its overload protection.
  • Waterlogged or air-bound pressure tank. A tank that has lost its air charge short-cycles the pump and can trip controls until it appears dead.
  • Power problems. On foothill and outlying Corona properties, long service drops and utility surges can starve a pump of the steady voltage it needs to start.

Safety-First Steps Before You Call

Warning: well pumps operate on 240 volts. That voltage can be fatal. Never open a control box or touch wiring while the power is on. Work through these in order:

  1. Turn the pump breaker OFF at the main panel first. Always cut the power before inspecting anything.
  2. Look at the breaker. If it sits in the tripped middle position, reset it ONCE — fully off, then back on.
  3. If it trips again immediately, stop. Leave it off and call us; repeated tripping points to a short or failing motor and a fire hazard.
  4. Check the pressure tank gauge. A reading stuck at zero, or far above the normal cut-out, is worth reporting to the technician.
  5. Listen at the pressure switch for a click when water should start the pump. No click usually means a switch or control problem.
  6. Inspect any visible wiring and conduit for burns, melted insulation, corrosion, or a scorched smell — keep the power off and call if you find any.
  7. Confirm the rest of the property has power, separating a pump-specific fault from a wider outage.

Electrical Causes Worth Understanding

Corona's foothill wells often sit at the end of long electrical runs, and the supply itself can be the hidden cause of a no-start. Undersized wire on older parcels, long service drops, and utility surges can leave a pump motor without the steady voltage it needs to spin up. A motor that hums but won't turn is frequently a low-voltage or weak-capacitor symptom rather than a dead motor, and confirming that with a meter prevents an expensive, unnecessary pump pull. A three-wire submersible keeps its start capacitor and relay in a surface control box — the cheapest and easiest place to find a fault — while a two-wire pump houses those parts in the motor downhole. We measure incoming voltage, test motor winding insulation resistance, and check amperage draw on every no-start call, because correctly distinguishing a $250 surface fix from a full replacement is what keeps your repair bill honest.

Frequently Asked Questions

I live in Corona on city water — why would I have a well?

While most of Corona is on municipal supply, a number of foothill properties, larger lots near Norco and the Temescal corridor, and older parcels still operate private wells. If your water comes from a pump, the same diagnostic steps apply when it won't start.

Why won't my pump start when the rest of the house has power?

The pump runs on its own dedicated circuit. A tripped pump breaker, a failed pressure switch, or a bad capacitor can keep the pump silent while every other circuit works. Check the dedicated pump breaker first.

Can I reset the pump breaker myself?

Yes, once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call us. Repeated tripping points to a short or a failing motor, and forcing it risks an electrical fire.

Does the area's mineral-rich water affect my pump?

It can. Hard groundwater from the Santa Ana watershed leaves scale that can clog the pressure switch's sensing port and, over time, stress the motor and bind moving parts — both of which can stop a pump from starting.
