Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

How a Well Pump System Works

Whether the pump serves a home or just an irrigation system, the fundamentals are the same. A submersible pump (or in some shallower setups, a jet pump) moves water under the control of a few key parts working in sequence:

  • Power supply: A dedicated 240-volt circuit delivers power from the panel to the pump.
  • Pressure tank: Holds pressurized water so the pump doesn't cycle on for every small draw.
  • Pressure switch: Monitors tank pressure and signals the pump to start and stop.
  • Control box / capacitor: Supplies the starting torque a single-phase motor needs.
  • Motor and wiring: The motor turns the pump; wiring carries power to it.
  • Water level: There must be adequate water above the pump intake to draw from.

If any of these links breaks, the symptom is identical — you turn on the water and nothing happens.

Common Reasons the Pump Stays Off

Electrical issues and tripped breakers

Electrical faults are the leading cause. A tripped breaker, a blown fuse, or a power surge can cut the pump off completely. Always check the dedicated well breaker first — but reset it only once. If it trips again right away, that is a sign of a deeper problem, and repeatedly resetting it risks an electrical fire.

Pressure switch failure

The pressure switch is a small device that cycles constantly, so it wears out comparatively often. Contacts can pit or burn, and the sensing port can clog with sediment. When the switch fails to close, the pump never gets the message to start.

Capacitor or control box trouble

Single-phase pump motors depend on a start capacitor, often inside a surface control box. As capacitors age they lose capacity, and a failing one typically causes the motor to hum or click without spinning up. Replacing it frequently restores the pump.

Motor burnout

After years of service or repeated electrical stress, a pump motor can fail outright. Because a submersible motor sits down in the well, replacing it means pulling the pump — the most involved of the common repairs.

Wiring and connection faults

Corroded splices, loose terminals, or damaged wire anywhere between the panel and the pump can interrupt power. These problems are sometimes intermittent and difficult to find without proper test equipment.

Low water level

If the water level falls near the pump intake, the pump's low-water or thermal protection may shut it down to prevent it from running dry and burning out.

Safe Checks You Can Make

  1. Confirm the property has power and check the well's dedicated breaker; reset it just once.
  2. Open a faucet or valve and listen at the pressure tank for the pressure switch clicking.
  3. Read the pressure gauge — stuck at zero or pinned high both indicate a fault.
  4. Look at visible wiring at the wellhead and pressure tank for damage or scorch marks.
  5. Note whether the trouble began after a storm, a power outage, or a long dry period.

Safety note: Well pump systems run on 240 volts and the pump is located deep in the well. Keep to visual and listening checks only. Do not open the control box or attempt to pull the pump — that is work for a licensed professional.

Reading the Symptoms Before You Call

The way a pump fails often hints at what is wrong, and describing those symptoms clearly when you call helps us arrive prepared with the right parts. A pump that is completely silent — no hum, no click — usually points to a power problem, a tripped breaker, or a pressure switch that isn't closing. A pump that hums or buzzes but produces nothing typically means the motor has power but lacks the torque to start, which directs attention to the capacitor or control box. Rapid clicking on and off, known as short-cycling, suggests a waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air charge or a switch nearing the end of its life. Water that sputters and spits before quitting can indicate the well is drawing air because the level has dropped or the system has lost prime. And if the breaker trips the instant you reset it, you are almost certainly looking at a short in the motor or wiring. None of these on their own confirm a diagnosis, but together with the pressure gauge reading and any recent weather or power events, they give a technician a strong head start — and that means a faster, cheaper repair for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

My well pump hums but no water comes out. What's wrong?

A hum without flow usually means the motor is powered but cannot start — most often a failed start capacitor or control box, sometimes a seized motor. It needs professional testing to confirm.

Why does my well breaker keep tripping?

A breaker that trips repeatedly is protecting against a genuine fault such as a shorted motor or damaged wiring. Don't keep resetting it — that can cause a fire. Turn it off and call for service.

Could low water be why my pump won't start?

Yes. If the water level drops near the pump intake, low-water protection may shut the pump off to prevent dry running. We can measure your water level and pump depth to confirm.

How much does a new well pump cost?

Submersible pump replacement generally runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower. A pressure-switch repair is much less at $150 to $350. We diagnose first so you pay only for what's needed.

Can I work on the pump's electrical system myself?

No. The 240-volt circuit and deep pump placement make DIY repairs dangerous. Visual checks are fine, but leave electrical work and pump pulling to a licensed professional.
