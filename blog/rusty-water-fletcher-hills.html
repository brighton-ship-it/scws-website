Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Fletcher Hills well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Diego County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Fletcher Hills well.

Iron Removal Systems

Casing Replacement

Plumbing Updates

Rusty Water in Fletcher Hills: Causes and Cures

Fletcher Hills is a hillside residential community on the western edge of El Cajon in San Diego County, climbing the slopes between El Cajon proper, La Mesa, and the Grossmont area. It is a neighborhood of established post-war and mid-century homes set on rolling terrain, where the underlying geology is the weathered granitic and metamorphic rock typical of San Diego County's inland valleys. Most Fletcher Hills homes are on municipal water through the Helix Water District, but the area's age and its proximity to the rural lots of greater El Cajon mean rusty water here can come from two very different directions: corroding household plumbing on city water, or iron-bearing groundwater on the private wells that still dot the surrounding hills.

Pinning down which one you are facing is the key to fixing it. The fix for a corroded galvanized line is nothing like the fix for iron-rich well water, and spending money on the wrong solution is the most common mistake homeowners make.

Where the Rust Comes From

Aging galvanized plumbing

A great many Fletcher Hills homes were built in the 1950s and 1960s, when galvanized steel pipe was standard. The interior zinc coating eventually erodes and the steel rusts, releasing reddish water and slowly choking off pressure. This corrosion is usually most obvious in the morning's first draw or after a vacation, when water has sat in the pipes long enough for rust to accumulate.

Water heater corrosion

The anode rod inside a tank water heater is designed to corrode so the tank does not. Once it is spent, the tank rusts and the hot water turns discolored. Rust confined to the hot side is the dead giveaway, and it often means the heater needs a new anode rod or is approaching replacement.

Dissolved iron and manganese from wells

On the private wells around El Cajon's rural fringe, iron and manganese leach naturally from the granitic bedrock and decomposed-granite aquifers. The water can look clear at the wellhead and turn orange in the glass as the dissolved iron oxidizes in air. Manganese leaves darker brown-black stains and is just as common in this terrain.

Iron bacteria and casing corrosion

Well properties can also harbor iron bacteria — the source of slimy orange buildup in tanks and filters — and older steel well casings that corrode and add rust directly to the water.

Helix Water District main work

Occasionally, routine main flushing or a repair in the local distribution system stirs up settled sediment and causes brief, harmless discoloration across a block. This kind of rust clears on its own within a day.

Simple Tests to Find Your Source

  • Cold-tap flush: Run cold water for a couple of minutes. Clears quickly? Likely sediment or a recent main flush. Persists? The issue is in your home or well.
  • Hot vs. cold: Hot only means the water heater.
  • Glass test: Clear at the tap, orange after sitting, means dissolved iron from a well.
  • Neighborhood check: Whole street affected at once usually points to the water district.
  • Slime check: Orange jelly in the toilet tank means iron bacteria, not minerals.

Solutions Matched to the Problem

Repiping for city homes

When galvanized pipe is the source, replacing it with PEX or copper ends the rust for good and brings back lost water pressure — the most durable fix for an older Fletcher Hills home.

Water heater repair

A fresh anode rod stops hot-side rust cheaply; a corroded tank calls for a new heater.

Iron and manganese treatment for wells

Oxidizing filters convert dissolved iron and manganese into a filterable form and trap them. Where hardness and light iron occur together, a properly sized softener can do double duty.

Well rehabilitation

Shock chlorination and casing cleaning eliminate iron bacteria, and camera inspection determines whether a corroded casing needs lining or replacement.

When to Call a Professional

Bring in a licensed contractor when the rust will not clear after flushing, when it returns repeatedly, when it appears only on the hot side, or when it comes with falling pressure or sputtering taps. On well properties, a water test showing iron above the 0.3 mg/L secondary standard, or any sign of iron-bacteria slime, is a clear signal to act. Recurring stains after a filter is already installed usually mean the original diagnosis missed the real source.

The Hillside Factor

Fletcher Hills' sloping terrain adds one practical wrinkle: water pressure and pump performance can vary noticeably with elevation across a property, and corroded pipe restricts flow more dramatically on the uphill fixtures. When we evaluate a rusty-water complaint here, we look at pressure as well as color, because the two often travel together in older hillside plumbing. For well owners on the higher lots, a constant-pressure or booster system sometimes accompanies a treatment upgrade so the whole house gets steady, clean water.

How San Diego County Geology Shapes Your Water

The hills around Fletcher Hills and El Cajon are built on weathered granite and metamorphic rock, and where that rock breaks down into decomposed granite it forms the aquifers that local wells tap. Iron and manganese are woven through these formations, so they naturally find their way into groundwater. The amount varies sharply from one well to the next depending on depth, the specific rock encountered, and how much the water table rises and falls through wet and dry years. That variability is exactly why a water test — rather than a guess based on a neighbor's experience — should drive the choice of treatment equipment. Two wells a few hundred yards apart can need very different systems.

Catching Problems Before They Grow

Whether you are on city water or a private well, rusty water tends to get worse, not better, if left alone. In galvanized plumbing, the corrosion that discolors your water also keeps shrinking the pipe's diameter, steadily eroding pressure until repiping becomes unavoidable. In wells, untreated iron and manganese coat pumps, foul pressure tanks, and feed iron-bacteria colonies that clog screens and filters. Acting at the first sign of color — a faint tint, a metallic taste, a recurring stain — keeps the repair small and the cost low. Annual water testing for well owners and periodic anode-rod checks for everyone are inexpensive habits that head off the bigger jobs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rusty water in my Fletcher Hills home harmful?

Rust from pipes or iron from a well is generally an aesthetic issue rather than an acute danger, but it stains, tastes metallic, and can hint at other problems. Have the source identified before drinking heavily discolored water.

My home is from the 1950s — could the pipes be the issue?

Very likely. Homes of that era commonly have galvanized steel plumbing, which rusts from the inside out as it ages. Persistent house-wide rust in an older Fletcher Hills home is frequently a repiping signal.

Why does my water pressure drop along with the rusty water?

Rust buildup narrows the inside of galvanized pipes, so corrosion and low pressure usually appear together. Repiping resolves both at once.

I am on a well — what removes the iron?

An oxidizing iron filter is the standard solution, sometimes paired with a softener if hardness is also present. We size the system to your tested iron level.

The whole street has discolored water. Is it my house?

Probably not — that usually means the Helix Water District flushed or repaired a main nearby. It typically clears within a day.
