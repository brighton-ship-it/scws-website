What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lakeside Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Lakeside

In Lakeside, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Lakeside wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Lakeside's Foothill and Backcountry Wells

Lakeside sits at about 400 feet in central San Diego County, where the San Diego River valley meets the granite foothills below El Capitan and Lake Jennings, in country long known for its horse ranches and rural lifestyle. Neighborhoods like Eucalyptus Hills and the parcels climbing toward El Monte Valley and the backcountry frequently rely on private wells. Those wells draw from a mix of decomposed granite and fractured crystalline bedrock in the foothills and alluvial fill in the river valley, and that mix determines whether hydrofracturing can help. The technique works on wells finished in competent fractured rock and does little for wells set in loose river-valley alluvium, so the honest first step on any Lakeside property is to establish which formation your well actually taps.

The Decomposed Granite Country Around Lakeside

The foothills around Lakeside, Eucalyptus Hills, and toward El Capitan are built of granitic and metamorphic rock that weathers to decomposed granite near the surface and stays hard and fractured at depth. Where a well is finished in that competent fractured rock, water travels only through cracks and joints, so yield depends on how many water-bearing fractures the borehole intersects. Hydrofracturing improves that connection by injecting clean water at high pressure to widen and link the fractures, and the firm rock holds those openings. Where a well sits instead in loose valley alluvium or crumbly decomposed granite without a true fracture network, the benefit is limited. We assess this distinction carefully, because it separates a treatment that transforms your supply from one that cannot help.

San Diego County's long dry season concentrates demand exactly when the fracture system is at its lowest, after the winter rains have been spent. A foothill well that supports a home, horses, and some landscaping comfortably in spring can fall behind by late summer or during a drought year. Raising the well's sustainable flow through fracturing gives these rural properties the margin they need in the hardest stretches.

Signs a Lakeside Well Needs Attention

  • Declining production over several years from a well you know is in rock
  • Slow recovery after watering livestock, irrigating, or heavy household use
  • Seasonal shortages that deepen in late summer and ease after winter rains
  • Household supply that suffers whenever the pasture or arena is being watered
  • A newer bedrock well that never delivered the flow your property needs

Before any treatment we confirm the formation is the real limit and rule out equipment. A pump undersized for a ranch, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a failing pressure switch can all imitate low yield, and these inexpensive fixes come first.

How the Process Works

  1. Baseline flow test to record current yield as a documented starting point.
  2. Camera survey to check casing condition and locate the best fracture zones.
  3. Packer isolation to seal off a section of open borehole for focused treatment.
  4. High-pressure injection of clean water, typically 1,000 to 3,000 PSI, to clean and extend fractures.
  5. Development and re-testing to flush loosened material and confirm the gain.

Most Lakeside jobs finish in a single day. We use only clean water with no chemicals, so your drinking water and your animals are never at risk, and we schedule around livestock watering needs where we can.

What to Check Before You Call

A little history helps us help you quickly. Try to learn whether your well is in competent rock or in river-valley alluvium, since that largely determines whether fracturing applies; a well log usually says, and we can confirm with a camera. Note whether your shortage is seasonal, which points to a yield limit rather than equipment. Watch the pressure gauge for rapid cycling, a sign of a waterlogged tank, and listen for a pump that runs long then loses prime, a classic low-yield symptom. Sharing this history, along with your well's depth and your livestock or irrigation demand, lets us give you a fast, accurate answer.

Pairing a Stronger Well With Storage

Many Lakeside ranches and rural homes get the most dependable supply by combining improved well flow with adequate storage. A storage tank lets a moderate foothill well recharge during quiet overnight hours and then deliver a heavy draw for livestock, irrigation, or the household during the day, while hydrofracturing raises the rate at which the well refills that storage. In the backcountry where wildfire is a serious seasonal concern, on-site storage also supports defensible-space irrigation and gives you a reserve independent of the well's moment-to-moment output. We evaluate the whole system during your assessment, including pump sizing and storage, so the recommendation fits how your property actually uses water.

Realistic Results and Cost

Where a Lakeside well is genuinely set in fractured rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, typically two to five times the prior flow. A new well in this area runs about $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, while fracturing a suitable existing well usually costs $3,000 to $8,000, with a $125 diagnostic credited toward the job. For a bedrock well that has lost yield, fracturing is the sensible first step before five-figure drilling. We document every result with before-and-after flow testing so there is no guesswork about the gain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Lakeside well is a fracturing candidate?

It depends on the formation. Wells finished in the competent fractured granite of the foothills are often strong candidates, while wells in loose river-valley alluvium are not. We confirm with your well log and a camera survey before recommending treatment, so you get an honest answer up front.

Can hydrofracturing support a ranch or equestrian well?

Yes, if the well is in fractured rock. Lakeside's horse properties carry heavy combined demand from livestock, arenas, and households, and boosting sustainable flow is exactly what they need. We routinely treat ranch wells to support that demand without drilling a costly new well.

Is the process safe for drinking water and livestock?

Yes. Hydrofracturing for water wells uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals. It reopens the natural fractures already feeding your well, so it is safe for your household and your animals.

How much more water can I expect?

On a qualifying Lakeside well, successful treatments commonly yield two to five times the prior flow, with a success rate around 70 to 80 percent. We measure your well before and after so you see the actual, documented gain rather than a promise.

How long does treatment take?

Most jobs take a single day, four to eight hours depending on the number of zones. We reinstall the pump and run a post-treatment flow test before leaving, and water is usually available again the same evening.

What if my well doesn't improve?

About one in five wells shows little gain, which is why we test before and after and assess candidacy honestly up front. If results disappoint, we discuss alternatives such as added storage, deepening the well, or, as a last resort, drilling a new one, and help you weigh the economics.
