Why Storage Tanks Matter for Well Systems

Many San Diego County wells produce less than the household needs during peak demand. A low-yield well producing 2-3 GPM can't keep up when you're running the shower, dishwasher, and filling a horse trough simultaneously. Storage tanks solve this problem.

How it works: Your well pump slowly fills a large storage tank 24/7. A separate booster pump draws from the tank to supply the house with consistent pressure and flow—even if your well only produces a trickle.

Common storage tank scenarios in our service area:

  • Low-yield wells (under 5 GPM) – Tank stores water during off-peak hours
  • Fire suppression – Rural properties need 2,500+ gallon reserves for fire trucks
  • Agricultural properties – Livestock and irrigation need surge capacity
  • Vacation homes – Tank refills while property is vacant
  • Power outage backup – Gravity-fed tanks work without electricity

Underground (Buried) Water Tanks: Detailed Analysis

Types of Underground Tanks

Concrete cisterns – The traditional choice. Cast-in-place or precast concrete tanks hold 1,000 to 50,000+ gallons. Extremely durable (50+ year lifespan) but expensive to install. Common on older San Diego properties.

Fiberglass tanks – Lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and easier to install than concrete. Sizes from 500 to 15,000 gallons. Popular for residential installations.

Polyethylene (plastic) underground tanks – Most affordable buried option. Requires careful backfill to prevent crushing. Lifespan 20-30 years underground.

Underground Tank Installation Process

  1. Site assessment – Check soil type, water table, utilities, and access for excavation equipment
  2. Permitting – San Diego County typically requires permits for buried tanks over 500 gallons
  3. Excavation – Hole must be larger than tank with room for backfill (usually 2-3 feet clearance)
  4. Bedding – Sand or pea gravel base prevents tank damage and allows drainage
  5. Tank placement – Crane or equipment lowers tank into position
  6. Connections – Inlet from well, outlet to booster pump, overflow, and vent
  7. Backfill – Careful layer-by-layer backfill prevents tank damage
  8. Final grading – Surface restored, access ports remain accessible

Pros of Underground Tanks

  • Invisible – No visual impact on your property
  • Freeze-proof – Earth temperature stays 55-60°F year-round in San Diego
  • Cooler water – Underground storage keeps water cooler in summer
  • Protected from damage – No exposure to sun, wind, fallen trees, or vehicles
  • Fire-safe – Tank can't melt or burn during wildfires
  • HOA-friendly – Often the only option in communities with visual restrictions

Cons of Underground Tanks

  • Higher installation cost – Excavation alone can cost $3,000-$8,000
  • Difficult inspection – You can't see leaks, cracks, or contamination
  • Repair challenges – Problems require excavation to fix
  • Groundwater issues – High water table can "float" empty tanks out of the ground
  • Root intrusion – Trees can crack concrete tanks over decades
  • Access limitations – Need vehicle access for initial installation

Above Ground Water Tanks: Detailed Analysis

Types of Above Ground Tanks

Polyethylene (plastic) tanks – Most common residential choice. Black or dark green colors reduce algae growth. Sizes from 250 to 10,000+ gallons. UV-stabilized for outdoor use.

Steel tanks – Corrugated steel with liner, similar to agricultural water tanks. Very large capacities (5,000-100,000 gallons). Common for fire suppression and agriculture.

Fiberglass tanks – Corrosion-resistant, suitable for areas with salt air (coastal Oceanside, Encinitas). Long lifespan but higher cost.

Concrete tanks – Above-ground concrete cisterns on some older properties. Extremely durable but rarely installed new due to cost.

Above Ground Tank Installation

  1. Site selection – Level area, ideally higher than the house for gravity pressure
  2. Foundation – Gravel pad, concrete slab, or compacted earth depending on tank size
  3. Tank delivery – Larger tanks may need crane placement
  4. Plumbing connections – Inlet, outlet, overflow, drain, and vent
  5. Screening – Optional fencing, landscaping, or tank enclosure for aesthetics

Pros of Above Ground Tanks

  • Lower cost – 40-60% less than buried tank installation
  • Easy inspection – You can see leaks, check water level, and spot problems
  • Simple maintenance – Cleaning and repairs don't require excavation
  • Relocatable – Tank can be moved if you reconfigure your property
  • Faster installation – Often same-day setup vs. multi-day excavation
  • Gravity-feed option – Elevated tanks provide pressure without a booster pump

Cons of Above Ground Tanks

  • Visible – Affects property aesthetics (screening helps)
  • Temperature fluctuation – Water heats in summer, may need insulation in mountain areas
  • Freeze risk – Julian, Palomar, and other mountain areas may need tank heaters
  • UV degradation – Plastic tanks eventually degrade (black tanks last longest)
  • Fire vulnerability – Plastic tanks can melt in wildfire; steel with liner is safer
  • Uses surface space – A 5,000-gallon tank has a significant footprint

San Diego County-Specific Considerations

Fire Zones (Most of Inland San Diego)

If you're in a high fire severity zone (Ramona, Julian, Valley Center, Fallbrook, etc.), consider:

  • Steel tanks with internal liner – Won't melt in fire
  • Underground tanks – Completely protected from fire
  • Fire department connection – 2.5" NST fitting so fire trucks can draft water
  • Minimum 2,500 gallons – CalFire recommendation for defensible space

Mountain Communities (Julian, Palomar, Pine Valley)

Where winter temperatures drop below freezing:

  • Underground tanks are ideal – Natural freeze protection
  • Above ground tanks need – Insulation, tank heaters, buried supply lines below frost line
  • Exposed plumbing – Heat tape on all above-ground pipes

Coastal Areas (Oceanside, Encinitas, Carlsbad)

Salt air accelerates corrosion:

  • Fiberglass or polyethylene preferred – No rust issues
  • Steel tanks – Require marine-grade coating or galvanization
  • Stainless steel fittings – Brass and standard steel corrode faster

HOA Communities

Many HOAs restrict visible tanks. Options include:

  • Underground installation – Usually the only approved option
  • Screened enclosures – May require architectural approval
  • Below-grade placement – Partially buried with access hatch

Maintenance Requirements Compared

Underground Tank Maintenance

  • Annual inspection – Check access ports, vents, and water quality
  • Every 3-5 years – Consider professional interior inspection (camera or entry)
  • Sediment removal – Pump out accumulated sediment every 5-10 years
  • Vent screening – Ensure vent screens prevent insects and debris entry

The challenge: Problems develop invisibly. Cracks, contamination, and leaks may go unnoticed until water quality suffers or water bills spike.

Above Ground Tank Maintenance

  • Monthly visual check – Look for leaks, cracks, algae, and proper water level
  • Annual cleaning – Drain, scrub interior, remove sediment
  • UV inspection – Check plastic for sun damage, cracks, or brittleness
  • Winter prep – Insulate or drain if in freezing area
  • Screen and vent check – Prevent mosquito breeding and contamination

The advantage: You can see exactly what's happening. A slow leak becomes obvious. Algae growth is visible. Water level changes are immediately apparent.

Which Should You Choose?

Choose Underground If:

  • HOA restrictions prohibit visible tanks
  • Aesthetics are critical (front of property, high visibility)
  • You're in a high fire zone and want maximum protection
  • You're in a mountain freeze zone and don't want to manage winterization
  • You have the budget and want maximum property value retention
  • You're building new and can include excavation in construction

Choose Above Ground If:

  • Budget is a primary concern
  • You want easy access for maintenance and monitoring
  • The tank location is screened from view naturally
  • You may relocate the tank in the future
  • You want to see problems before they become expensive
  • Quick installation is important (emergency water storage)

Frequently Asked Questions

Which lasts longer, buried or above ground water tanks?

Above ground tanks typically last 20-30 years since they're easier to inspect and maintain. Underground tanks can last 30-50 years (concrete even longer), but hidden problems may go undetected, potentially shortening effective lifespan.

Do buried tanks freeze in San Diego County?

Generally no—underground tanks benefit from earth's stable temperature (55-60°F year-round). However, in mountain communities like Julian or Palomar where temperatures drop below 25°F, shallow burial or exposed pipes can still freeze.

How much does it cost to install an underground water tank?

Underground tank installation runs $8,000-$25,000+ including excavation, the tank, backfill, and connections. Above ground tanks of the same capacity cost $3,000-$10,000 installed—roughly 40-60% less.

Can I install a water tank myself?

Small above-ground tanks (under 500 gallons) can be DIY projects. Larger tanks and any underground installation should be professionally installed to ensure proper connections, permits, and safety.

Do I need a permit for a water storage tank in San Diego County?

San Diego County typically requires permits for tanks over 500 gallons and any underground installation. Above-ground tanks under 5,000 gallons may be exempt in some areas—check with your local building department.

What size tank do I need?

General guideline: Store at least one day's water usage. Average households use 50-100 gallons per person per day. Add irrigation, livestock, and fire suppression needs. Low-yield wells (under 5 GPM) often need 1,500-2,500 gallon tanks; very low-yield wells may need 5,000+ gallons.
