What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Pinon Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Pinon Hills wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Boosting Hard-Rock Well Yield in Pinon Hills

Pinon Hills is one of the most well-dependent communities in San Bernardino County's high desert. Tucked along the northern foot of the San Gabriel Mountains between Wrightwood and Phelan, this rural stretch of unincorporated desert has almost no municipal water service. Homes along Pinon Hills Road, Largo Vista, and the parcels climbing toward Mountain Top draw their water from private wells punched into decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock. When one of those wells starts to falter, the choices come down to two: spend big on a brand-new well, or try to restore the one you already have. For many Pinon Hills properties, hydrofracturing is the smarter first move, and the local geology is unusually well suited to it.

Below, we walk through what hydrofracturing is, why hard-rock desert wells like those in Pinon Hills tend to respond well, the realistic results you can expect, and the honest cases where a different repair makes more sense.

What Hydrofracturing Does

Think of a fractured-rock well as a straw plugged into a web of underground cracks. The water in Pinon Hills' aquifer does not sit in an open pool; it moves through fissures and seams in the granite. Your well only produces as much as those cracks can deliver to the borehole. Over the years, the narrowest of those cracks clog with mineral scale, fine grit, and the decomposed-granite sediment that washes in everywhere across this part of the desert. Production slowly drops, even though plenty of water still moves through the formation nearby.

Hydrofracturing clears and widens that pathway. A technician lowers an inflatable packer into the well casing, inflates it to isolate a zone, and then pumps clean water in above it at high pressure, commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes debris out of the existing fractures and gently extends hairline cracks into broader, more connected channels. Once the well is developed and cleared, it draws from a far larger slice of the surrounding rock. The only material used is water; there are no chemicals, which matters when the same well supplies your kitchen tap.

Why Pinon Hills Wells Respond Well

Hydrofracturing only works where there is rigid rock to fracture, and Pinon Hills delivers that in abundance. The community sits on the granitic flank of the San Gabriels, with decomposed granite near the surface giving way to harder fractured crystalline bedrock at depth. Local wells run a wide range, from roughly 300 feet to over 600 or even 1,000 feet in places, and the productive zones are typically in fractured rock rather than loose sediment. That is exactly the formation type that responds to high-pressure injection.

This is a genuine strength of the area compared with, say, a deep desert sand basin or a coastal town on municipal water, where hydrofracturing usually disappoints. In Pinon Hills, the question is rarely whether the geology supports the technique; it is whether your specific well is clogged enough, and productive enough at depth, to be worth treating. A small number of shallower Pinon Hills wells do tap looser alluvium near canyon mouths, and those are poor candidates. We confirm which type you have before recommending anything, because spending on hydrofrac for a sand well wastes your money.

Signs a Pinon Hills Well Is Worth Treating

  • Yield has slipped over several years while neighbors at similar depths still produce well.
  • Long recovery times — after a load of laundry or a fill-up of a livestock trough, the well takes hours to recover.
  • Air in the lines or pump short-cycling during summer when demand peaks.
  • A disappointing new well — drilled into rock but never producing the gallons-per-minute you were promised.
  • Pressure drops in the dry, windy season when the desert water table sits lower.

These point to a yield problem in the formation. If instead the pump is old, the pressure switch is chattering, or the bladder tank is waterlogged, the fix is cheaper and has nothing to do with the rock. A diagnostic visit sorts that out first.

How the Job Runs, Start to Finish

  1. Diagnostic and baseline flow test. We measure current yield and recovery and pull your well log to verify the completion is in fractured rock. The visit is $125 and is credited toward the work.
  2. Downhole camera survey. Video confirms casing condition and shows us where the productive fracture zones are.
  3. Rig setup at the wellhead. The high-pressure pump, water tank, and packer assembly are positioned and connected.
  4. Zone isolation. Packers seal the target interval so pressure concentrates on the rock instead of bleeding up the casing.
  5. High-pressure injection. Clean water at 1,500 to 3,000 PSI opens and flushes the fractures. Deeper Pinon Hills wells may get more than one isolated zone treated.
  6. Develop and re-test. We purge the loosened sediment, then run a fresh flow test and hand you the before-and-after numbers. Most jobs take four to eight hours on site.

Realistic Results

On the fractured-rock wells we treat across the high desert, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, with a typical gain of two to five times the starting flow rate. Some badly clogged wells jump more; a well in genuinely tight, water-poor rock may gain little. We are upfront about that range because it is honest. Hydrofracturing improves your well's connection to the water the formation holds, but it cannot create groundwater where the rock is simply dry. That is why we baseline before and verify after, and why we will tell you if your odds look marginal before you commit.

What to Have Ready

  • Your well completion report. It lists total depth, casing, and whether the well bottoms in rock or sediment.
  • The well's drilling date and any history of past yield or repairs.
  • How the decline showed up — slow over years (formation) versus sudden overnight (usually equipment).
  • Pump specs — horsepower and set depth, which help us separate a rock problem from a pump problem.

When to Bring in a Professional

Pumping water into a cased well at over 3,000 PSI is not a homeowner project. Done wrong, it can crack casing or breach the annular seal, and on a drinking-water well that is a serious problem. Just as important, a licensed contractor's first job is the diagnosis: confirming your Pinon Hills well is a fractured-rock candidate, and steering you away from hydrofrac if it is not. If your well draws from loose alluvium, or the trouble is really the pump, we will say so and point you to deepening, screen rehabilitation, or a pump replacement instead.

Cost Ranges for Pinon Hills

Hydrofracturing here generally runs $3,000 to $8,000, set mostly by well depth, the number of zones treated, and site access on these often-steep desert lots. A new turnkey well, by contrast, runs $18,000 to $42,000, so a successful hydrofrac can save you the better part of a new-well budget. If the diagnosis points to equipment, the numbers drop sharply: a pump replacement is $2,500 to $5,500, a pressure tank $600 to $1,500, and a pressure switch $150 to $350. Well deepening is priced by the foot and varies with depth. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward whatever you choose to do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Pinon Hills well a good candidate for hydrofracturing?

Most likely yes if it bottoms in fractured granite or crystalline rock, which is the norm for deeper Pinon Hills wells. The exception is the small number of shallow wells in loose alluvium near canyon mouths. We verify from your well log before recommending the work.

Does hydrofracturing use any chemicals?

No. It uses only clean water under pressure. It shares a name with oil-and-gas fracking but is a completely different, chemical-free process safe for drinking-water wells.

How long until I have water again?

The on-site work takes about four to eight hours. After we develop and re-test the well, normal household use typically resumes within a day or two as the well clears loosened sediment.

What kind of yield increase is realistic?

On suitable wells we usually see two to five times the prior flow rate, succeeding in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases. We measure before and after so you get documented results, not just a promise.

What happens if it does not work on my well?

If the well is not a strong candidate or the gain is too small, we will lay out alternatives such as deepening the well or drilling a new one, and help you weigh cost against your water needs.

Is hydrofracturing cheaper than a new well?

Almost always. At roughly $3,000 to $8,000 versus $18,000 to $42,000 for a new well, it is usually worth trying first whenever the geology supports it, as it generally does in Pinon Hills.
