What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Wildomar Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Restoring Well Yield in Wildomar's Granite Foothills

Wildomar sits in southwest Riverside County between Lake Elsinore and Murrieta, where the rolling hills along the eastern edge of the Santa Ana Mountains are built from the decomposed granite and granodiorite of the Peninsular Ranges. Most private wells in and around town — from the rural lots out toward The Farm and Sycamore Ranch to the parcels climbing the slopes off Palomar Street and Bundy Canyon — draw their water from fractures in that hard rock rather than from a deep sandy aquifer. When those fractures slowly clog with mineral scale and fine sediment, the well's yield drops, and that is exactly the situation hydrofracturing is built to reverse.

If your Wildomar well used to keep up with the house and the irrigation but now sputters by late afternoon, the problem usually is not the pump. It is that water can no longer move freely through the rock into the borehole. Hydrofracturing reopens and extends those natural fracture pathways using nothing but clean, high-pressure water.

How Hydrofracturing Works on Fractured-Rock Wells

The technique is straightforward in concept. We lower an inflatable packer into your cased well and seal off a target zone, then pump water into that isolated section at pressures that typically run between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. That pressure flushes out the clay, iron, and carbonate scale choking the existing fractures and nudges those cracks open a little wider and a little deeper into the surrounding granite. The result is a larger, cleaner network of channels feeding the well. On a good candidate, the whole job takes a single working day, and the only thing going down the hole is water.

Because Wildomar wells are completed in hard rock, they tend to respond well. The hard fractured granite that makes drilling slow and expensive here is the same rock that holds an open fracture once we have created it. Wells finished in loose alluvium down on the valley floor are a different story, and we will tell you honestly if yours is not a candidate.

Signs a Wildomar Well Is Ready for Treatment

Homeowners usually call us after they notice a pattern: the well that once filled the storage tank in twenty minutes now takes an hour, recovery after heavy use has slowed to a crawl, or the pump short-cycles during the dry stretch from June through October when demand peaks and the water table sits at its seasonal low. A brand-new well that came in with a disappointing flow rate is also a strong candidate — rather than abandon it, hydrofracturing often unlocks the yield the driller was hoping for.

A Quick Self-Check Before You Call

Before assuming the worst, it helps to separate a well problem from a pump problem. If your pressure tank is short-cycling or your water sputters with air, the issue may simply be the pump or the pressure switch rather than the well's yield. A genuine yield decline usually looks different: the system holds normal pressure for a while, then the well draws down and recovery takes longer than it used to. In Wildomar, where summer temperatures push irrigation demand from June into October, that draw-down most often shows up in the late afternoon when the house, the landscaping, and any livestock are all pulling at once. Note how long your well takes to refill the storage tank after heavy use and compare it to a year or two ago. That single observation tells us a great deal about whether hydrofracturing is the right tool.

What a Treatment Day Looks Like

On the day of the job, our crew arrives early and starts with the pre-treatment flow test so we have a documented baseline. Next comes the downhole camera, which lets us see the casing, the static water level, and the fracture zones along the open hole. We then pull the pump if needed, set the inflatable packer at the chosen depth, and begin injecting clean water in stages, watching the pressure response as fractures accept water and open. A Wildomar granite well usually takes the bulk of the working day to treat across its productive zones. We reinstall the pump, run the well to clear loosened sediment, and finish with a post-treatment flow test so you can see the before-and-after numbers side by side. Most owners have normal water service back by that evening.

Why the Local Geology Drives the Result

The Peninsular Ranges batholith that underlies Wildomar is competent, crystalline rock, and that is good news for hydrofracturing. Competent rock holds an open fracture rather than collapsing back on itself, so the pathways we create tend to stay productive for years. The flip side is that yields in crystalline rock are inherently fracture-dependent: two wells a few hundred feet apart can produce very differently depending on whether they intersect a well-connected fracture set. That variability is exactly why we measure rather than guess, and it is why a well that seems to be failing is so often a candidate for restoration rather than replacement.

Protecting Your Investment After Treatment

Once your yield is restored, a few habits keep it that way. Periodic well chlorination controls the iron bacteria that contribute to fracture clogging, and an annual flow check catches any gradual decline early, while it is still easy and inexpensive to address. If your water is hard or high in iron, simple sediment filtration ahead of the pressure tank reduces the scale that works its way back into the system over time. We are happy to set you up with a maintenance schedule that fits your Wildomar property so the gains from hydrofracturing last as long as possible. When you are ready for a straight assessment, our team is a phone call away.

Hydrofracturing Compared With Other Yield Solutions

Hydrofracturing is the right answer when the limiting factor is the rock's ability to feed water into the well, but it is not the only tool, and an honest contractor will tell you when something simpler fits. If your Wildomar well actually produces enough water and the problem is weak pressure in the house, the fix may be a new pressure switch ($150-$350), a properly sized pressure tank ($600-$1,500), or a constant-pressure booster system ($2,000-$4,500) rather than any work on the well itself. If the pump is the failing component, a replacement submersible pump runs $2,500-$5,500. Hydrofracturing, at $3,000-$8,000, is specifically for the case where the well's recovery and yield have fallen because the bedrock fractures have clogged. Our $125 diagnostic exists precisely to tell these situations apart before you spend money on the wrong solution.

What Determines Whether Treatment Succeeds

Three things drive the outcome on a Wildomar well. First is fracture connectivity: the more the borehole intersects an interlinked network of cracks, the more dramatic the improvement, because high-pressure water can propagate the treatment outward. Second is casing condition, which we verify with the camera; a sound casing lets us isolate and pressurize the open-hole section cleanly. Third is the depth and character of the water-bearing zones, which the well log and our flow testing reveal. When all three line up — competent granite, intact casing, identifiable fracture zones — the typical two- to five-fold yield gain is well within reach. When they do not, we will tell you plainly rather than proceed with a low-probability treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing damage my Wildomar well casing?

No. The high-pressure water is directed below the casing into the open-hole bedrock section using packers that isolate the treatment zone. A camera inspection beforehand confirms your casing is sound, so the pressure works on the rock rather than the steel.

How much can hydrofracturing improve my well's yield?

In Wildomar's fractured granite, most treated wells see a two- to five-fold increase in flow, and some improve more. Results depend on how the rock is fractured around your borehole, which is why we flow-test before and after.

Is this the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. Water-well hydrofracturing uses only clean water at far lower pressures, with no sand, gels, or chemicals. It simply reopens the natural fractures that already feed your well.

How long does the process take in Wildomar?

Most residential jobs are completed in a single day, including setup, treatment, and a post-treatment flow test. You typically have water back the same evening.

My well is brand new but low-yield. Can it still be treated?

Yes. A disappointing new well is one of the best candidates. Rather than drill again, hydrofracturing frequently opens additional fractures and brings the yield up to a usable level.

Do I need a permit for hydrofracturing in Riverside County?

Well rehabilitation generally does not require the same permit as a new well, but we handle any local requirements and keep documentation of the work for your records.
