How Water Pressure Is Created and Lost

On a well system, pressure is built and stored, then delivered on demand — and it can be lost at several points. The pump lifts water and pushes it into a pressure tank, which holds water under an air charge. A pressure switch tells the pump when to run and stop, setting the cut-in and cut-out pressures. From the tank, water travels through your service line and household plumbing to each fixture. A restriction or failure anywhere along that path shows up as weak flow at the tap.

The pressure tank

The tank is the most common source of pressure complaints. When its bladder fails and it becomes waterlogged, it can no longer cushion delivery, so pressure swings sharply and the pump short-cycles. Restoring the air charge or replacing the tank often fixes the problem outright.

The pressure switch

A switch set too low, knocked out of calibration, or fed by a sediment-clogged sensing tube makes the entire system operate at reduced pressure. It's an inexpensive component, and adjusting or replacing it is one of the quickest pressure wins available.

The pump

A pump that's worn, undersized for the home's demand, or losing efficiency to mineral buildup produces steady but inadequate pressure that sags further when multiple fixtures run. We test the pump's output before concluding it needs replacement.

Clogged pipes and fixtures

This is where University Heights's age matters. Decades-old galvanized supply lines corrode internally, narrowing the bore and strangling flow. Mineral scale builds in aerators, valves, and the home's pipes. Sometimes the well delivers fine pressure and the bottleneck is entirely in the house.

The well and water level

On a private well, a falling water level or a low-yielding well can limit how much water the pump can deliver, especially during peak summer demand. Measuring static and pumping levels tells us whether the well itself is the limiting factor.

Common Scenarios in University Heights

Given the neighborhood's housing stock, corroded galvanized plumbing is a recurring theme — water arrives at the meter or tank fine but loses pressure on the way to the upstairs bathroom. On well properties, we often find waterlogged pressure tanks on systems that have run a decade or more, and pressure switches drifting out of calibration. Summer demand spikes also expose marginal pumps and weak wells that coped fine in cooler months. Identifying which scenario fits usually points straight to the fix.

What You Can Check First

  • Pressure gauge: if you're on a well, read the gauge at the tank — pressure should cycle between roughly 40 and 60 psi.
  • One fixture vs. many: note whether pressure is weak everywhere or only when several taps run together.
  • Hot vs. cold: low pressure on the hot side alone points to a clogged water heater or its supply line.
  • Aerators: unscrew a faucet aerator and check for mineral debris — a cheap, common culprit.
  • Pressure tank: tap it top to bottom; a heavy, solid tank suggests a failed bladder.

When to Call a Professional

Call us if pressure is low throughout the house, if it swings or the pump short-cycles, if you suspect old galvanized lines, or if you're on a well and unsure where the loss is. Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my water pressure low only in University Heights's older homes?

University Heights is one of San Diego's historic streetcar neighborhoods, and many homes here date to the early 1900s. Aging galvanized supply lines corrode and narrow on the inside over the decades, choking flow long before anything at the source fails. On properties served by a private well, that old plumbing compounds with pressure-tank or pressure-switch wear. We diagnose both the well side and the house side so you fix the real bottleneck.

Is low pressure a pump problem or a pressure tank problem?

Both are common culprits and they behave differently. A waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air charge makes pressure swing wildly and the pump cycle on and off. A worn or undersized pump produces steady but weak pressure that sags further when several fixtures run at once. A quick gauge reading and a tank air-charge check usually tell us which one you have.

Can a pressure switch cause low water pressure?

Yes. The pressure switch controls the cut-in and cut-out points at which your pump starts and stops. If it's set too low, miscalibrated, or has a sediment-clogged sensing line, your whole system runs at lower pressure than it should. Adjusting or replacing the switch is one of the most affordable pressure fixes, typically $150–$350.

Will a constant-pressure system help my University Heights home?

If your pressure drops noticeably whenever the shower and a faucet run together, a constant-pressure or booster system is often the best answer. Instead of cycling between a low and high setpoint, it varies pump speed to hold steady pressure on demand. Installed cost generally runs $2,000–$4,500 depending on your setup.

How much does it cost to fix low water pressure?

It depends on the cause. A pressure switch is $150–$350, a new pressure tank $600–$1,500, and sediment filtration to clear clogged lines $300–$900. A constant-pressure or booster system runs $2,000–$4,500, and a full pump replacement, only if the pump has failed, is $2,500–$5,500. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward whatever repair you choose.
