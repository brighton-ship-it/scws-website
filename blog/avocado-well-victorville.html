Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Victorville well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How Victor Valley Wells Work

Wells in the Victorville area tap the Mojave River groundwater basin and surrounding alluvial aquifers — layers of sand and gravel deposited over geologic time. A submersible pump set well below the static water level lifts water to a pressure tank or to storage for irrigation. Because groundwater levels in parts of the basin have dropped over the decades, pump setting depth and well design matter a great deal. A pump set too shallow will lose its water in a dry stretch; set correctly, it delivers reliably for years.

The Hard-Water Challenge

Mojave groundwater is mineral-rich. That means hard water that leaves scale in pipes, water heaters, and fixtures, often accompanied by iron or manganese that stains sinks and laundry. Left untreated, scale shortens the life of every appliance the water touches and can choke down flow over time. We test desert wells for hardness, iron, and manganese, then recommend right-sized treatment — a softener, an iron/manganese filter, or sediment filtration — so your water is both reliable and easy on your plumbing.

Common Well Problems in the High Desert

  • Declining water levels: Basin drawdown can leave a pump drawing air during peak demand.
  • Hard-water scale: Mineral buildup on pumps, heaters, and fixtures.
  • Iron and manganese staining: Orange and brown-black marks on porcelain and laundry.
  • Pump and motor wear: Heat and heavy summer use stress equipment.
  • Pressure-tank and switch failures: The most frequent service call anywhere, desert included.

What to Check Before You Call

If your Victorville well loses water, note whether the loss is total or just weak pressure, and whether it worsens during the hottest part of the day — a clue that a declining water level is the cause. Confirm the pump breaker is on, listen for rapid cycling at the pressure tank, and check the gauge. If you irrigate, note whether the problem appears only when you run a heavy zone. These details help us diagnose quickly in the field.

When to Call a Professional

Call a licensed contractor when you suspect a failed pump, a dropping water level that needs the pump lowered, or treatment to tame hard, staining water. Pulling a pump from a deep desert borehole and re-setting it at the correct depth is precise, equipment-heavy work, and getting it wrong in the high desert can mean repeat dry-outs. A C-57 contractor brings the rig, the testing, and the experience to solve it once.

Sizing a System for Desert Demand

Water demand in the Victor Valley peaks exactly when the well is most stressed: triple-digit summer afternoons, when landscape, livestock, and household use all spike at once. A system that runs fine in spring can stutter in July if the pump, pressure tank, and storage are not matched to peak demand. We size each component to the property's real summer load, and where a well cannot keep up instantaneously, we add storage so the well can fill a tank steadily while a booster delivers the pressure you feel at the tap. Getting this balance right is the difference between a well that copes with the desert and one that fails the first heat wave.

Stretching a Well in a Drought-Stressed Basin

Parts of the Mojave River basin have seen long-term groundwater decline, so making the most of every gallon matters. Low-water cutoffs protect the pump from running dry, efficient drip and micro-irrigation cut waste, and scheduling heavy watering for cooler hours reduces evaporation loss. Where an older well has lost capacity, options like lowering the pump, deepening, or hydrofracturing ($3,000 to $8,000) can sometimes restore production for far less than a new well. We help Victorville owners weigh these choices honestly against the cost of starting over.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can avocados be grown in Victorville?

Not practically. Victorville sits in the high desert of the Victor Valley at roughly 2,700 feet, with hot summers, cold winters, hard freezes, and very low humidity. Avocados are frost-sensitive and would not survive a typical Victorville winter. Our work here centers on residential, ranch, and small-agriculture well service suited to high-desert conditions.

How deep are wells in the Victorville area?

Victor Valley wells draw from the Mojave River groundwater basin and alluvial desert aquifers. Depths vary considerably, often from about 200 to over 500 feet, and groundwater levels in parts of the basin have declined over the years, which affects pump setting and yield.

Why is my desert well water so hard and scaly?

High-desert groundwater in the Mojave is typically high in dissolved minerals, producing hard water that scales pipes, water heaters, and fixtures, and sometimes carries iron or manganese staining. A softener or iron/manganese filter ($1,500 to $3,500) and sediment filtration ($300 to $900) address these issues.

What does a new well cost in the high desert?

A turnkey well in the Victorville area generally falls between $18,000 and $42,000 depending on depth, casing, and pump system. Deeper static water levels in parts of the basin can push costs upward. We quote after reviewing your parcel and target depth.

Do you handle large pumps for ranch and small-farm properties?

Yes. Many Victor Valley properties run alfalfa, pasture, livestock, or small orchards on agricultural wells. We size and service submersible pumps ($2,500 to $5,500), pressure systems, and storage to match irrigated acreage and desert demand.

Do you offer same-day emergency service to Victorville?

Yes. We provide same-day emergency response for no-water situations across the Victor Valley. Summer heat makes a failed well urgent, so we prioritize getting desert properties back online quickly.
