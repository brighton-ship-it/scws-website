Understanding Well Yield

Well yield is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Typical requirements:

  • Single family home: 3-5 GPM minimum
  • Large family/multiple bathrooms: 6-12 GPM
  • Small farm/livestock: 10-20 GPM
  • Agricultural irrigation: 50-500+ GPM

Many San Bernardino County wells produce 1-3 GPM—technically "low flow" but manageable with proper system design.

Solutions for Low Flow Wells

Storage Tank Systems

Collect water 24/7 into a large tank, then use a booster pump for household pressure. This multiplies your effective supply.

Hydrofracturing

High-pressure treatment to open fractures in bedrock, often doubling or tripling yield.

Well Deepening

Drilling deeper may reach better-producing zones.

Second Well

Adding a second well to supplement your primary supply.

Living with a Low Flow Well

While improving yield is ideal, you can also adapt:

  • Install low-flow fixtures
  • Spread water use throughout the day
  • Add storage capacity for peak demand
  • Use water-efficient appliances

Low-Flow Well Help for Wrightwood Mountain Properties

Living at 6,000 feet in the San Gabriel Mountains has its rewards, but a stubborn, low-producing well is not one of them. If your Wrightwood well struggles to fill the bathtub, drops off when the washing machine runs, or runs low after a dry winter, you have a low-yield well — a familiar story in this rugged corner of San Bernardino County. The terrain that makes Wrightwood beautiful, all that hard granite and steep relief, is also what makes water hard to come by. Fortunately, there are proven ways to coax more, steadier water from a mountain well.

Wrightwood sits astride the San Andreas Fault zone on fractured crystalline bedrock. Unlike valley communities that tap thick alluvial aquifers, mountain wells here depend on water stored and moved through cracks in the granite. When those fractures are sparse or poorly connected, even a deep, expensive well can disappoint. Understanding that geology is the first step to fixing the flow.

Understanding Well Yield and GPM Testing

Yield is the rate, in gallons per minute, that your well can sustain without running dry — a very different thing from momentary faucet pressure. We measure it with a flow test paired with drawdown and recovery monitoring: we pump steadily, track how far the water level drops, and time how fast it rebounds. A mountain well that draws down quickly and recovers slowly is fracture-limited; one that holds its level but delivers little is usually plugged or under-pumped. For a Wrightwood home, 4 to 8 GPM is comfortable, while 1 to 4 GPM is common and manageable with storage.

Why Wrightwood Wells Run Low

  • Fracture-limited granite. The defining issue in the San Gabriels. Water enters only where the bit crosses an open fracture, so a well can be deep and still slow.
  • Dry winters and shrinking snowpack. Mountain aquifers recharge from snowmelt. A string of low-snow years drops the water table and the fractures' saturation.
  • Mineral scale and biofouling. Over years, deposits and bacterial slime plug the screen and fracture faces, throttling inflow.
  • A pump set above the best fractures. Repositioning the pump can recapture flow that is already there.
  • Sediment from steep, decomposing granite. Fine material can clog the intake and reduce effective yield.

Solutions That Work in the San Gabriels

Rehabilitation and redevelopment clean a fouled screen and fracture network with surging, brushing, and chemical treatment — the first, lowest-cost step. Lowering the pump helps when the water table has fallen or the intake sits above productive fractures. Hydrofracturing is frequently the standout option here: injecting water under high pressure reopens and links the granite's fractures, and yields often double or more. Deepening or a new well makes sense when the borehole bottomed out in tight rock. And a storage tank with a booster or constant-pressure pump lets a slow-but-steady mountain well bank its 24-hour output and still deliver strong household pressure.

When to Call a Pro

Reach out if your flow has dropped, you are pulling air or sediment, pressure fails when two fixtures run, or you are about to add demand the well cannot meet. A flow and drawdown test removes the guesswork and identifies the most economical fix — before a marginal well becomes a dry one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal well yield for a home in Wrightwood?

In the Wrightwood area of the San Gabriel Mountains, 4 to 8 GPM is comfortable for a household. Many mountain wells drilled into fractured granite produce 1 to 4 GPM, which works for a home when combined with adequate storage, but high water use or a steep, rocky lot can push a well into true low-flow territory.

Why do Wrightwood mountain wells often produce so little water?

Wrightwood sits on hard granite bedrock near the San Andreas Fault, and mountain wells rely on water collected in fracture systems rather than a deep sandy aquifer. If a well intersects only a few fractures, natural yield is low regardless of depth. Snowpack-dependent recharge and dry winters make it worse.

Can hydrofracturing increase yield on a Wrightwood well?

Yes — fractured granite bedrock is the ideal setting for hydrofracturing. We inject water under high pressure to reopen and connect the rock's natural fractures, and mountain wells in this geology commonly see yield double or more afterward.

Should I add a storage tank for my low-flow mountain well?

For most Wrightwood properties, yes. A storage tank lets a slow well fill it steadily over 24 hours, and a booster or constant-pressure pump then supplies the house. Even a 2 GPM well banks nearly 2,900 gallons a day, which covers ordinary household demand and snowmelt-season irrigation.

How deep are wells around Wrightwood?

Mountain wells in this part of San Bernardino County commonly run 200 to 400 feet through granite, though depths vary widely with terrain and fracture location. Deeper is not always better in hard rock; intersecting productive fractures matters more than total depth.

What will it cost to fix a low-yield Wrightwood well?

Well rehabilitation generally runs $1,500 to $6,000, hydrofracturing $3,000 to $8,000, and a storage-tank-and-booster system $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey mountain well ranges from $18,000 to $42,000. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward approved work.
