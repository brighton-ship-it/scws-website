What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Perris Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Perris, Riverside County

Perris sits in the heart of inland western Riverside County, a fast-growing city on a broad, level valley floor between the San Jacinto Mountains to the east and the low hills that ring the basin. Its long agricultural past left behind thousands of private wells serving homes, ranchitos, and farms across the Perris Valley. Understanding that valley is the key to understanding hydrofracturing in Perris: the ground beneath the city is a deep alluvial basin — sand, gravel, silt, and clay washed down from the surrounding highlands — not the fractured hard rock that hydrofracturing is built to treat.

For that reason we want to be candid with Perris well owners from the outset. Hydrofracturing is a valuable tool in the right geology, but the alluvial valley fill under Perris is not that geology. If your Perris well is producing less than it used to, the real solutions are almost always rehabilitating or replacing the well screen, deepening the well, lowering or replacing the pump, or drilling a new well — not high-pressure rock fracturing.

How Hydrofracturing Works — and Why Rock Matters

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates wells drilled into solid bedrock. A technician lowers an inflatable packer to seal off a section of the borehole, then injects clean water into that isolated zone at high pressure, commonly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. In granite or other crystalline rock, that pressure clears silt from the natural fractures and widens them, reconnecting the well to water-bearing seams and often multiplying yield several times over. It uses clean water only, with no chemicals, and is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking.

The catch is that fracturing needs fractures. In the loose alluvial sand and gravel that fills the Perris Valley, there is no rock to crack open. Pressurizing that material does not create durable flow paths; the sediment simply shifts and re-packs around the borehole. That is why hydrofracturing is rarely appropriate for valley-floor wells in Perris, and why an honest contractor will point you toward methods that actually fit alluvial ground.

Understanding the Perris Valley Aquifer

Perris draws its groundwater from the alluvial basin of the San Jacinto and Perris Valley groundwater system, where water-bearing sediments fill the valley to depths ranging from tens to hundreds of feet. Most local wells run roughly 200 to 600 feet deep: older wells from the 1960s through the 1980s tend to be shallower, around 200 to 350 feet, while wells drilled in recent decades often reach 400 to 600 feet or more. The younger alluvium in parts of the basin is coarse and permeable and can yield well, while older, finer sediments transmit water more slowly.

This matters for well owners in two ways. First, there is generally substantial groundwater stored in the basin, so a properly built and maintained well can tap it reliably — the issue is usually the well's connection to the aquifer, not a lack of water. Second, the aquifer's nature points to alluvial-well methods, not bedrock fracturing, as the way to restore yield. Perris groundwater is also frequently hard and can carry elevated total dissolved solids, nitrates from the area's farming history, and in some formations naturally occurring arsenic, which is why water testing and the right treatment often go hand in hand with yield work here.

What Actually Causes Low Yield in Perris Wells

  • A clogged or encrusted well screen — mineral scale and iron bacteria plug the slots that admit water to the casing.
  • A sanded-in or collapsed screen interval — older wells fill with fine sediment, shortening the effective intake.
  • A declining water table — valley-wide pumping and drought can drop the static level below an old, shallow pump setting.
  • A worn or undersized pump — impellers wear out and lose capacity, which feels exactly like a failing well.
  • A waterlogged pressure tank or failing pressure switch — inexpensive fixes often mistaken for a dry well.

Better Alternatives to Hydrofracturing Here

  1. Well rehabilitation — mechanical brushing, surging, and chemical treatment of the screen and gravel pack to restore intake capacity. This is the alluvial-aquifer counterpart to hydrofracturing and is frequently very effective in Perris.
  2. Deepening or lowering the pump — if the water table has dropped, setting the pump deeper or extending the well can recover lost production.
  3. Pump and pressure-system repair — replacing a tired pump, pressure tank, or switch often restores flow at a fraction of the cost of major work.
  4. A new well — when an old well is structurally failing, a properly screened and gravel-packed new well in the basin is the durable answer.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Note when flow drops. A well that runs strong then fades after several minutes points to a screen or water-level problem; one weak all the time points to the pump.
  2. Listen for rapid pump cycling. That usually signals a waterlogged tank or pressure switch, not the aquifer.
  3. Watch for sand or grit at the taps. Sand pumping suggests a screen or gravel-pack issue worth inspecting.
  4. Find your well completion report. Depth, screen interval, and static water level tell us exactly what is happening.
  5. Check the breaker and wiring. Sometimes "no water" is simply lost power to the pump.

Where Hydrofracturing Genuinely Helps

To be fair to the technique, it is worth noting where it does shine, because the contrast explains our advice for Perris. East of the valley floor, as the land climbs into the granite of the San Jacinto foothills and the back country toward Anza, wells are commonly drilled directly into fractured crystalline rock. There, when yield slips, hydrofracturing is frequently the most cost-effective fix — reopening and extending the natural fractures for $3,000–$8,000 instead of spending far more on a new well. Those hillside wells share none of the alluvial character of the Perris Valley floor, which is exactly why the right method depends on where your property sits. For valley-floor Perris wells, alluvial rehabilitation, deepening, or replacement remains the honest, effective path, and that is what we will recommend.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing right for my Perris well?

Usually not. Perris sits on a deep alluvial basin — sand and gravel, not fractured rock. Hydrofracturing needs bedrock fractures to widen, so it rarely helps valley-floor wells here.

What works instead on an alluvial well?

Screen rehabilitation, deepening or lowering the pump, pump and pressure-system repair, or a new well. These match the geology and are typically what restore yield in Perris.

Why is my Perris well producing less water?

Most often a clogged or sanded-in screen, a dropped water table, or a worn pump — not the basin running dry. The Perris Valley aquifer generally holds ample water.

How much does well work cost in this area?

Pump replacement runs $2,500–$5,500 and a new well $18,000–$42,000; many problems are solved far more cheaply with a pump or pressure-tank repair.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. It uses only clean water under pressure and is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking. It simply is not the right tool for Perris valley ground.
