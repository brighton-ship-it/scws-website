What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Mission Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Mission Hills

In Mission Hills, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Mission Hills wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Mission Hills

Mission Hills is one of San Diego's oldest and most established neighborhoods, perched on the bluffs just north of Old Town and west of Hillcrest, looking out over San Diego Bay and the San Diego River valley. It is a dense, fully urbanized community of historic Craftsman and Spanish Revival homes, tree-lined streets like Fort Stockton Drive and Sunset Boulevard, and city blocks served by the City of San Diego's municipal water system. That urban character is the single most important fact for anyone here researching hydrofracturing, so let us be direct about it.

Almost no homes in Mission Hills are on private wells, and hydrofracturing is rarely relevant here. If you are connected to city water — as the overwhelming majority of Mission Hills properties are — a low-pressure or low-flow problem at your faucet is a plumbing, pressure, or service-line issue, not a well issue, and hydrofracturing has nothing to do with it. We want to save you time: the right call may be a plumber or the water utility rather than a well contractor. Where we genuinely help Mission Hills residents is with the small number of older estate parcels, canyon-edge lots, or inherited rural properties elsewhere in the county that still rely on a private well.

How Hydrofracturing Works (and Where It Applies)

Hydrofracturing is a well-rehabilitation technique for private wells drilled into fractured bedrock. We seal off a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI against that isolated zone. In hard rock, the pressure clears debris and widens the natural fractures that carry groundwater into the well, often boosting yield two to fivefold over four to eight hours. It uses only clean water — no chemicals. The key requirement is fractured rock; the technique does nothing for a home on a municipal connection and nothing for a well sunk into loose sand or gravel.

Common Water Problems in Mission Hills

Because this is a city-water neighborhood, the issues residents actually run into are usually these:

  • Low household pressure — often a partially closed main valve, an old galvanized service line, or a failing pressure regulator at the meter rather than anything underground.
  • Hard-water scale — San Diego's imported water is mineral-rich; a softener ($1,500–$3,500) addresses scale and spotting.
  • Inherited rural property elsewhere — many Mission Hills families own a second home or land in the East County backcountry where an actual well does need attention.

If You Own a Private Well Outside the City

For the Mission Hills resident who owns acreage in Ramona, Alpine, Jamul, or the county's granite backcountry, that is where hydrofracturing can shine. San Diego County's mountains and foothills are built on the Peninsular Ranges batholith — granitic and metamorphic bedrock laced with natural fractures. Wells drilled into that rock are exactly the candidates that respond well to hydrofracturing, with success rates in the 70–80% range in our experience. If that describes a property you own, we are glad to evaluate it and give you a frank assessment of whether the well will respond.

What to Check Before You Call

A few quick checks tell us whether you even have a well problem:

  • Are you on city water or a private well? Your monthly bill from the City of San Diego is the giveaway.
  • Is the low pressure at every fixture or just one? A single slow faucet is a fixture problem, not a supply problem.
  • If you do have a well, how fast does it recover after heavy use, and have you noticed sand or air spitting from the taps?
  • Do you have the original well log showing depth and the formation drilled? That document tells us immediately whether the well is in rock or alluvium.

When to Call a Professional

If you are a Mission Hills homeowner on city water with a pressure complaint, start with a plumber or the utility. If you own a private well — here or on county acreage — and it is losing yield, pumping sand, or running dry, that is our specialty. Our $125 diagnostic (credited toward any work) includes a flow test and, where helpful, a downhole camera inspection so you know exactly what you are dealing with before spending a dollar. Frequently Asked Questions

Do homes in Mission Hills have private wells?

Very few. Mission Hills is a fully urbanized San Diego neighborhood served by city water, so hydrofracturing is rarely relevant here. Most pressure or flow complaints are plumbing or service-line issues.

I'm on city water but have low pressure — can hydrofracturing help?

No. Hydrofracturing only improves private wells in fractured rock. On a municipal connection, low pressure is usually a valve, regulator, or old service-line problem — a plumber or the utility is the right call.

I inherited a well on rural land — is it a candidate?

Quite possibly. If it is drilled into San Diego County's granitic backcountry bedrock, it is exactly the kind of well that responds well to hydrofracturing. We are happy to evaluate it.
