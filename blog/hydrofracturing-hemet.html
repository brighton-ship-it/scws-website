What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Hemet Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Hemet Wells in the San Jacinto Valley

Hemet sits on the floor of the San Jacinto Valley in Riverside County, surrounded by San Jacinto, Valle Vista, and Winchester, with the ground climbing toward Sage, Anza, and the San Jacinto Mountains to the east and south. That geography matters a great deal when your well starts running short, because the rock beneath your property is not the same everywhere in this valley. Down on the valley floor, wells generally draw from deep alluvial deposits, which are layers of loose sand, gravel, and clay washed down over thousands of years. Up in the foothills and along the rising ground toward Anza and Sage, wells are usually drilled into fractured granitic bedrock, part of the Peninsular Ranges that form the mountains.

This single geological fact decides whether hydrofracturing is the right answer for your Hemet well. Hydrofracturing works by opening and cleaning natural cracks in solid rock, so it shines in the fractured granite of the foothills. It does very little for a well sitting in loose sand or gravel, because there are no rock fractures to open. A big part of an honest assessment, then, is simply figuring out which kind of well you have before anyone quotes you a price.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing, sometimes spelled hydrofracking, is a well-rehabilitation method that uses nothing but clean water under pressure. A technician lowers a set of inflatable seals called packers into your well casing and inflates them to isolate a target section of the borehole. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated zone at high pressure, commonly in the range of about 1,500 to 3,000 or more PSI. That pressure pushes into the existing hairline fractures in the surrounding bedrock, opening them wider, flushing out the silt, mineral scale, and fine debris that have plugged them over the years, and connecting your borehole to water-bearing cracks it could not reach before.

The work is methodical rather than dramatic. The packers are repositioned to treat the borehole zone by zone, so each productive interval gets a fair chance. From setup to the final development pumping, a typical Hemet job takes about four to eight hours on site. Importantly, no chemicals are added at any point. This is not the oil-and-gas style of fracking that uses chemical-laden fluids and proppants. Water-well hydrofracturing relies on clean water alone, which keeps your drinking supply exactly that, clean.

The Local Nuance: Foothill Bedrock Wells vs. Valley-Floor Wells

Here is the part most generic articles skip, and it is the most important thing for a Hemet property owner to understand. Hydrofracturing is a rock technique. It needs fractures to open, which means it needs hard, fractured stone around the borehole.

Foothill and bedrock wells are good candidates. If your property sits on the higher ground around Hemet, climbing toward Valle Vista, Sage, the Anza area, or the flanks of the San Jacinto Mountains, your well most likely draws from fractured granitic bedrock. These are precisely the wells that respond to hydrofracturing. When the natural cracks that feed the well have clogged or the borehole simply did not intersect enough of them, pressurized water can reopen and extend those pathways and meaningfully boost flow.

Deep valley-floor alluvial wells usually are not candidates. If your well is out on the flat San Jacinto Valley floor and pulls from sand and gravel aquifers, there is no solid rock to fracture. Pumping high-pressure water into loose material does not create durable new pathways, and it can stir up sediment without producing a lasting gain. For these wells, the better answers lie elsewhere, and a reputable contractor will tell you so rather than sell you a treatment that cannot work.

How to tell which kind you have. Two clues usually settle it. First, location: foothill and rising-ground properties lean toward bedrock, while flat valley-floor properties lean toward alluvium. Second, and more reliable, your well driller's log, which is the report filed when the well was drilled. It records what the drillers passed through, and words like granite, decomposed granite, or fractured rock point to a bedrock well, while sand, gravel, and clay point to an alluvial one. If you cannot find the log, a flow test and a brief downhole assessment let us infer the formation and judge candidacy honestly.

Signs a Bedrock Hemet Well May Need Hydrofracturing

Assuming you have a foothill bedrock well, several patterns suggest hydrofracturing could help:

  • Declining yield over time. The well steadily produces less than it did a few years ago, even though your usage has not changed.
  • Slow recovery after pumping. Once you draw the well down, it takes a long time to refill, so heavy use leaves you waiting.
  • Dry-season and drought shortfalls. The well keeps up in winter but falls behind during Hemet's hot, dry months when the water table drops.
  • An under-producing new well. A recently drilled bedrock well that simply never delivered the flow you expected may have missed the better fractures, and hydrofracturing can help connect to them.

None of these signs alone proves you need the treatment, but together they make a strong case for a professional flow test in a bedrock setting.

Better-Fit Solutions for Valley-Floor Alluvial Wells

If your well turns out to be a deep alluvial well on the valley floor, do not despair. Hydrofracturing is the wrong tool, but several other approaches often restore production:

  • Well redevelopment. Surging, jetting, and cleaning can pull fine sediment out of the gravel pack and screen, reopening the spaces water flows through.
  • Screen or casing work. Corroded or encrusted screens and casing can be cleaned, repaired, or replaced to restore intake.
  • Deepening the well. Extending the borehole into a more productive zone can reach reliable water in an alluvial aquifer.
  • Pump upgrade or reset. Sometimes the well is fine and the pump is undersized, worn, or set at the wrong depth, and the real fix is in the equipment.

Matching the method to the formation is the whole game. A treatment that works wonders in foothill granite can be a waste of money on the valley floor, and the reverse is true as well.

Safety First: What to Check Before Anything Else

Before you assume the well itself is the problem, rule out the simpler and far cheaper causes. A surprising number of low-water calls turn out to be equipment, not geology. With the power off where appropriate, or by simply observing the system, check the basics:

  • The pump. Confirm it is actually running and not tripped at the breaker.
  • The pressure tank. A waterlogged or failed tank causes rapid pressure swings and short cycling that mimic a weak well.
  • The pressure switch. A dirty, corroded, or misadjusted switch can stop the pump from coming on properly.
  • Leaks. A leaking line, fitting, or fixture can drain pressure and waste water that you assume the well is failing to deliver.

If those check out and you still have a shortfall, a professional flow test measures what the well truly produces and recovers. That single test usually points clearly to whether the issue is the well, the formation, or the equipment.

Realistic Success in Fractured Rock

It is fair to ask what hydrofracturing actually delivers. In hard, fractured granite like the foothill formations around Hemet, the treatment improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of wells. Typical gains run about two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate. Those are honest numbers, not guarantees, because every borehole and fracture network is different. To keep the results transparent, we run a flow test before treatment to set a baseline and another afterward to confirm the change, usually within 24 to 48 hours, and we document the before-and-after comparison so you can see exactly what you got for your money.
