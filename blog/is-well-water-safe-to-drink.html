You turn on your tap, fill a glass, and pause. Is this water actually safe to drink? For the nearly 2 million Californians who rely on private wells—and the 43 million Americans nationwide—this isn't an idle question. Unlike city water that's tested daily and regulated by strict federal standards, your well water is entirely your responsibility. There's no government agency testing it monthly. No utility company ensuring it meets safety standards. No automated alerts if dangerous bacteria or chemicals appear. Private wells fall into a regulatory blind spot: the EPA's Safe Drinking Water Act explicitly excludes wells serving fewer than 25 people, leaving homeowners to navigate water safety largely on their own. The answer to "Is well water safe to drink?" isn't a simple yes or no—it's "it depends on your specific well, your local geology, nearby contamination sources, well construction quality, and whether you're actually testing and treating your water." This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know to answer that question for your well: regulations (or lack thereof), common contaminants, testing requirements, treatment options, and the critical factors that determine whether your well water is safe for your family.

⚠️ Critical Fact: Private wells serving individual homes are NOT regulated by the EPA or required to meet Safe Drinking Water Act standards. Well owners are 100% responsible for their own water quality and safety.

Is Well Water Safe to Drink?

The short answer: well water can be safe to drink, but it's not automatically safe. Many private wells provide excellent, clean drinking water that meets or exceeds municipal water quality standards. However, many others contain bacteria, nitrates, arsenic, or other contaminants at levels that pose health risks.

Well water safety depends on multiple factors:

  • Local geology: Groundwater naturally contains minerals and elements from surrounding rock—some beneficial, some harmful
  • Well construction quality: Proper depth, casing, sealing, and location prevent contamination
  • Nearby contamination sources: Septic systems, agriculture, industry, mining, and landfills can affect groundwater
  • Well age and maintenance: Older wells may have deteriorated seals or components that allow contamination
  • Testing and treatment: Regular testing identifies problems; appropriate treatment removes contaminants
  • Seasonal and environmental changes: Flooding, drought, and nearby land use changes can introduce new risks

The only way to know if your well water is safe is through regular professional testing. Water that looks, tastes, and smells fine can still contain dangerous levels of bacteria, nitrates, arsenic, or other contaminants that have no warning signs.

💡 Key Point: Clear, good-tasting water does NOT mean safe water. Many dangerous contaminants—including arsenic, lead, nitrates, and bacteria—are colorless, tasteless, and odorless. Only testing reveals what's actually in your water.

EPA Regulations vs State Regulations for Private Wells

Understanding who regulates your well water—or more accurately, who doesn't—is critical to recognizing your responsibilities as a well owner.

Federal Regulations: The EPA Doesn't Cover Private Wells

The EPA's Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) regulates public water systems—utilities serving at least 25 people or 15 service connections. Private wells serving individual homes are completely exempt from federal drinking water regulations.

What this means:

  • No required water quality testing
  • No maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) that must be met
  • No mandatory treatment or disinfection
  • No government monitoring or oversight
  • No requirement to notify anyone if contamination is found
  • Full responsibility rests with the well owner

The EPA provides recommendations for private well testing and publishes maximum contaminant levels as guidance, but these aren't legally enforceable for private wells. Compliance is entirely voluntary.

California State Regulations

California has more oversight than most states but still doesn't require routine testing for existing private wells:

What California DOES Require:

  • Well construction standards: Wells must be drilled by licensed contractors following state bulletins (Bulletin 74 guidelines)
  • Minimum construction specifications: Proper casing depth, annular seals, sanitary seals
  • Well completion reports: Must be filed with the state documenting construction details
  • Testing at property transfer: Since 2022, well water must be tested for bacteria and nitrates when property changes hands (Health and Safety Code Section 116275)
  • Disclosure requirements: Sellers must disclose known water quality problems

What California DOESN'T Require:

  • Annual or routine water testing for existing wells
  • Testing for contaminants beyond bacteria and nitrates (except at property transfer)
  • Treatment or remediation if contaminants are found
  • Notification to neighbors or health departments about contamination
  • Ongoing monitoring for chemical contaminants like arsenic, lead, or PFAS

Bottom line: California provides construction standards and requires minimal testing when properties change hands, but ongoing water safety is almost entirely the homeowner's responsibility. You must voluntarily test and treat your water to ensure it's safe.

Common Contaminants in Well Water

Well water can contain dozens of potential contaminants, but certain ones are particularly common and concerning. Here's what you need to know about the most frequent threats to well water safety:

1. Coliform Bacteria and E. coli

What it is: Coliform bacteria are microorganisms found in soil, surface water, and the digestive systems of humans and animals. Total coliform indicates general contamination; E. coli specifically indicates fecal contamination.

Health risks: E. coli and other pathogenic bacteria cause diarrhea, cramps, nausea, vomiting, and fever. Vulnerable populations (infants, elderly, immunocompromised) face severe risks including kidney failure and death.

How common: Found in 15-20% of private wells nationwide. Particularly common after flooding, in shallow wells, or near septic systems.

Safe levels: Zero. Drinking water should have no detectable coliform bacteria.

Treatment: Shock chlorination (temporary), UV disinfection, chlorination systems, well rehabilitation. See our complete guide to coliform bacteria in well water.

2. Nitrates (NO₃)

What it is: Nitrogen compounds from fertilizers, animal waste, septic systems, and decomposing organic matter. Highly soluble and mobile in groundwater.

Health risks: Causes "blue baby syndrome" (methemoglobinemia) in infants under 6 months—nitrates prevent blood from carrying oxygen, potentially fatal. Long-term exposure may increase cancer risk and cause thyroid problems.

How common: Widespread in agricultural areas. About 22% of private wells in agricultural regions exceed safe levels. Particularly prevalent in California's Central Valley and other farming areas.

Safe levels: EPA MCL is 10 mg/L (10 ppm). California matches this standard. Levels above 10 mg/L are unsafe, especially for infants.

Treatment: Reverse osmosis, ion exchange, distillation. Boiling water concentrates nitrates and makes it MORE dangerous—never boil water with high nitrates for infant formula.

3. Arsenic

What it is: A naturally occurring element that leaches from rock formations into groundwater. Particularly common in volcanic and sedimentary geology.

Health risks: Known human carcinogen causing bladder, lung, and skin cancer with long-term exposure. Also causes cardiovascular disease, diabetes, skin lesions, and neurological damage. No immediate symptoms—effects accumulate over years.

How common: Present in groundwater across California, especially in parts of San Diego County, Central Valley, Owens Valley, and areas with volcanic geology. An estimated 350,000+ Californians have arsenic above 10 ppb in their well water.

Safe levels: EPA/California MCL is 10 ppb (parts per billion or μg/L). However, California's Public Health Goal is 0.004 ppb—there's no truly safe level for arsenic.

Treatment: Reverse osmosis (most effective for point-of-use), anion exchange, activated alumina, oxidation/filtration systems. Requires NSF-certified arsenic-specific treatment.

4. Lead

What it is: Heavy metal that enters water from plumbing components—brass faucets, lead solder, old galvanized pipes, submersible pump components, and well-related plumbing. Rarely occurs naturally in groundwater.

Health risks: Causes irreversible brain damage and developmental delays in children. Adults experience kidney damage, high blood pressure, and reproductive problems. No safe level for children.

How common: Uncommon in groundwater itself but present in 10-20% of household tap water due to plumbing. More common in acidic water (low pH) which dissolves lead from pipes and fixtures.

Safe levels: EPA "action level" is 15 ppb, but there's no safe level for children. California requires action if levels exceed 15 ppb.

Treatment: Replace lead-containing plumbing (permanent solution), reverse osmosis, activated carbon filters, distillation. Flush faucets before use if water sits in pipes overnight. See our California lead testing guide.

5. Iron and Manganese

What it is: Naturally occurring metals dissolved in groundwater, particularly common in California wells.

Health risks: Generally not harmful at typical well water concentrations, though very high manganese may affect infants and children neurologically.

How common: Extremely common—found in 25-30% of private wells. Often occurs together.

Aesthetic impacts: Iron causes red/brown staining, metallic taste, and orange slime (iron bacteria). Manganese causes black staining and bitter taste. Both stain laundry, fixtures, and appliances.

Safe levels: EPA secondary standards (aesthetic, not health-based): Iron 0.3 mg/L, Manganese 0.05 mg/L. California infant advisory for manganese: 0.3 mg/L.

Treatment: Iron/manganese filters, oxidation systems, water softeners (for low levels), greensand filters, aeration + filtration.

6. PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances)

What it is: Synthetic "forever chemicals" used in firefighting foam, non-stick cookware, water-repellent fabrics, and industrial processes. They don't break down in the environment or human body.

Health risks: Linked to cancer, liver damage, thyroid disease, immune system suppression, developmental effects in children, and reproductive problems. Accumulates in the body over time.

How common: Emerging contamination issue. Found near military bases, airports, fire training facilities, industrial sites, and landfills. Testing is increasing—contamination more widespread than previously known.

Safe levels: EPA proposed maximum contaminant level goals (MCLGs) are near zero for the most common PFAS. California interim notification levels: 5-10 ppt (parts per trillion) for PFOA and PFOS.

Treatment: Reverse osmosis (most effective for point-of-use), granular activated carbon (GAC) filters, anion exchange resins. Requires specialized PFAS-specific media.

7. Hard Water (Calcium and Magnesium)

What it is: Dissolved calcium and magnesium minerals from limestone, dolomite, and gypsum rock formations.

Health risks: None—hard water minerals are beneficial nutrients.

How common: Extremely common, especially in San Diego County where hardness typically ranges from 10-30 grains per gallon (very hard to extremely hard).

Problems: Causes scale buildup in pipes and appliances, reduces soap effectiveness, leaves spots on dishes, makes skin/hair feel dry, shortens water heater life, increases energy costs.

Treatment: Water softeners (ion exchange), salt-free conditioners, reverse osmosis (drinking water only). See our complete hard water solutions guide.

California Testing Requirements and Standards

While California doesn't mandate routine testing for existing private wells, understanding what is required and what standards apply helps you make informed testing decisions.

When Testing IS Required in California

  • Property transfers (since 2022): Well water must be tested for total coliform bacteria and nitrates when property changes ownership
  • New well construction: Some counties require initial testing before the well is approved for use
  • Specific local ordinances: Certain counties or municipalities may have additional requirements
  • When applying for certain permits: Building permits or septic system permits may trigger testing requirements

Recommended Testing Beyond Legal Requirements

What California requires and what's actually necessary for safety are very different. Health experts recommend testing for:

Annual Testing (Minimum):

  • Total coliform bacteria
  • E. coli bacteria
  • Nitrates

Every 3-5 Years:

  • Arsenic (especially in known arsenic areas)
  • Lead (particularly if you have children or pregnant women in the home)
  • pH, hardness, TDS (total dissolved solids)
  • Iron and manganese (if staining or taste issues)
  • Sulfate, chloride (if taste/odor issues)

When Warranted by Location/Risk:

  • PFAS (near military bases, airports, industrial sites, fire training facilities)
  • Pesticides/herbicides (agricultural areas)
  • Volatile organic compounds/VOCs (near gas stations, dry cleaners, industrial sites)
  • Uranium, radium (areas with naturally occurring radioactivity)
  • Fluoride (natural occurrence in some areas can exceed safe levels)

How to Test Your Well Water

Testing well water correctly ensures accurate results that you can trust to make treatment decisions. Here's what you need to know:

DIY Test Kits vs Professional Laboratory Testing

DIY Home Test Kits ($15-$80)

Best for: Quick screening, hardness testing, pH checks, iron/manganese presence

Pros: Immediate results, inexpensive, convenient, good for monitoring known issues

Cons: Less accurate than lab testing, limited contaminants tested, not suitable for bacteria testing, results not accepted for real estate transactions or regulatory purposes

Common types: Test strips (hardness, pH, iron, nitrates), colorimetric kits (color-change chemical tests)

Professional Laboratory Testing ($30-$300+)

Best for: Complete water analysis, bacteria testing, arsenic/lead/nitrates, property transfers, treatment system design

Pros: Highly accurate, certified results, comprehensive panels available, legally valid, detects trace contaminants

Cons: More expensive, wait time for results (2-7 days), must follow strict sampling procedures

Who uses: Certified environmental labs, county health departments, private testing companies

How to Collect a Proper Water Sample

Proper sample collection is critical—contamination during sampling leads to false results. Follow these steps:

For Bacteria Testing (Most Critical):

  1. Use only sterile sample bottles provided by the lab (don't substitute your own container)
  2. Remove faucet aerator, hose attachments, and any filters
  3. Disinfect faucet spout with bleach or alcohol wipe
  4. Run cold water for 2-3 minutes at full flow
  5. Reduce flow to a steady stream (not splashing)
  6. Open sterile bottle only at the moment of collection—don't touch inside of bottle or cap
  7. Fill to designated line (usually leaving 1" of air space)
  8. Cap immediately, label with date/time/location
  9. Keep sample cold (below 50°F) and deliver to lab within 24-48 hours

For Chemical Testing (Arsenic, Nitrates, Lead, etc.):

  1. Use lab-provided bottles (some tests require preservatives pre-added to bottles)
  2. For lead testing: Collect "first draw" sample (water that sat in pipes overnight) and "flushed" sample (after running water 2-3 minutes)
  3. For other contaminants: Run water 2-3 minutes before collecting
  4. Fill bottle completely (no air space for chemical tests)
  5. Label with required information
  6. Deliver according to lab instructions (refrigeration usually not required for chemical tests)

Signs Your Well Water May Be Unsafe

While many dangerous contaminants are invisible, some water quality problems do produce warning signs. Watch for these indicators:

Visual Signs

  • Cloudy or turbid water: Could indicate bacteria, sediment, or suspended particles
  • Brown, red, or orange water: Usually iron or manganese; could also indicate corroding pipes
  • Blue-green staining: High copper levels (acidic water corroding copper pipes)
  • Black staining: Manganese contamination
  • Oily film or sheen: Could indicate petroleum contamination or iron bacteria
  • White/chalky residue: Hard water minerals (not harmful, but indicates high mineral content)
  • Pink/red slime: Airborne bacteria (Serratia) thriving in moist environments—not from water itself but indicates conditions bacteria like

Taste and Odor Issues

  • Rotten egg smell: Hydrogen sulfide gas—usually harmless but indicates sulfur bacteria or chemical reactions
  • Metallic taste: Iron, manganese, copper, zinc, or low pH (acidic water)
  • Salty taste: High sodium, chloride, or total dissolved solids; possible seawater intrusion in coastal areas
  • Earthy/musty smell: Decaying organic matter; possible surface water intrusion
  • Chemical/fuel smell: Petroleum products, industrial chemicals, or pesticides—test immediately
  • Chlorine smell: If you don't chlorinate your well, this could indicate contamination from nearby chlorinated water source

Health-Related Red Flags

  • Recurring gastrointestinal illness: Diarrhea, nausea, cramps that come and go—possible bacteria or parasites
  • Illness after heavy rain or flooding: Surface water contamination
  • Only household members affected: If neighbors on different water sources are fine, suspect your well
  • Infants with breathing difficulties: Could indicate high nitrate levels (blue baby syndrome)—seek immediate medical attention and test water
  • Skin rashes or irritation after showering: Could indicate high chlorine (if treating well), bacteria, or chemical contamination

External Conditions That Increase Risk

  • Recent flooding: Can introduce bacteria and surface contaminants
  • Nearby construction or land disturbance: Can mobilize contaminants or damage aquifer
  • Changes in nearby land use: New agricultural operations, septic systems, or industrial activity
  • Known contamination in area: If neighbors' wells test positive for contaminants, yours may too
  • Drought conditions: Can concentrate contaminants or lower water table, drawing in different water
  • Aging well: Wells over 20 years old may have deteriorating seals or casing

🚨 When to Test Immediately

Test your well water immediately if you notice: sudden changes in taste, odor, or appearance; recurring unexplained illness in household members; after flooding or ground disturbance near your well; if a new baby will be drinking the water; if neighbors report well contamination; or if you haven't tested in over a year. Don't wait—some contaminants pose serious immediate risks.

Treatment Options for Common Contaminants

Once you've identified contaminants in your well water, appropriate treatment can make it safe to drink. Here are the most effective treatment methods for common well water contaminants:

Bacteria Treatment

Ultraviolet (UV) Disinfection

How it works: UV light destroys bacteria, viruses, and parasites by damaging their DNA

Effectiveness: 99.99% kill rate for bacteria and viruses when properly sized and maintained

Best for: Clear water with consistent bacteria presence; requires pre-filtration if water is turbid

Pros: No chemicals, no taste/odor impact, low maintenance, treats whole house

Cons: Requires electricity, lamp replacement annually, doesn't provide residual protection, won't work with cloudy water

Cost: $500-$1,500 installed; $50-$150/year for lamp replacement

Chlorination Systems

How it works: Injects chlorine into water, holds for contact time, then removes excess chlorine with carbon filter

Effectiveness: Kills bacteria and oxidizes iron/manganese

Best for: Persistent bacteria issues, high iron/manganese, situations requiring residual disinfection

Pros: Provides residual protection, treats iron/manganese simultaneously, works with turbid water

Cons: Requires chemical handling, regular maintenance, can affect taste if carbon filter fails, more complex

Cost: $1,500-$3,000 installed; $100-$300/year for chemicals and filters

Shock Chlorination (Temporary)

How it works: One-time heavy dose of chlorine to disinfect entire well and plumbing system

Best for: Temporary contamination (after flooding, repairs), initial treatment before installing continuous system

Not a permanent solution: Bacteria often return within weeks to months if source isn't eliminated

Cost: $200-$500 professional service; $50-$100 DIY

Nitrate Treatment

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

Effectiveness: Removes 85-95% of nitrates

Application: Point-of-use (kitchen sink) only—not whole-house

Best for: Drinking and cooking water when whole-house treatment isn't needed

Cost: $300-$800 installed; $100-$200/year filters

Ion Exchange (Nitrate-Specific Resin)

Effectiveness: 90-95% nitrate removal

Application: Whole-house or point-of-use

Pros: Can treat entire house, removes sulfate as well

Cons: Requires regeneration with salt brine, produces wastewater, may increase sodium levels

Cost: $1,500-$3,000 whole-house; $150-$300/year operating costs

Arsenic Treatment

Reverse Osmosis

Effectiveness: 95-99% arsenic removal (both As III and As V forms)

Application: Point-of-use (under-sink)

Best choice for: Drinking/cooking water arsenic removal

Cost: $300-$1,200 installed; $150-$250/year filters

Anion Exchange

Effectiveness: 95%+ removal of arsenic V (requires oxidation for arsenic III)

Application: Whole-house possible

Considerations: Affected by sulfate and TDS levels; requires periodic regeneration

Cost: $1,500-$4,000 whole-house system

Activated Alumina or Iron-Based Media

Effectiveness: 90-95% arsenic removal

Application: Point-of-use or whole-house

Considerations: Media requires periodic replacement; pH sensitivity

Cost: $800-$2,500; media replacement $200-$400 every 2-5 years

Lead Treatment

Source Removal (Best Solution)

Replace lead-containing plumbing, fixtures, solder, and pump components

Cost: Varies widely; $500-$5,000+ depending on extent

Reverse Osmosis

Effectiveness: 95-99% lead removal

Application: Point-of-use drinking water

Cost: $300-$800 installed

NSF-Certified Carbon Filters

Effectiveness: 95%+ when properly certified for lead removal (must be NSF Standard 53)

Application: Point-of-use or point-of-entry

Important: Not all carbon filters remove lead—must be specifically certified

Cost: $150-$500; filter replacement $50-$150/year

Iron and Manganese Treatment

Oxidation + Filtration

Air injection or chemical oxidation converts dissolved iron/manganese to particles, then filters them out

Effectiveness: Handles high iron/manganese levels (5+ mg/L)

Cost: $1,500-$3,500 installed

Greensand or Catalytic Media Filters

Specialized media oxidizes and filters iron/manganese in one step

Best for: Moderate iron/manganese (up to 10-15 mg/L iron)

Cost: $1,200-$2,500 installed

Water Softeners (Low Levels Only)

Can remove dissolved iron up to 2-3 mg/L as side benefit of softening

Not recommended: As primary iron treatment—will foul resin if levels too high

PFAS Treatment

Reverse Osmosis

Effectiveness: 90-95%+ PFAS removal

Best for: Point-of-use drinking water treatment

Cost: $300-$1,200 installed

Granular Activated Carbon (GAC)

Effectiveness: 70-90% PFAS removal with proper media

Application: Point-of-use or whole-house

Important: Must use PFAS-specific GAC; regular carbon is less effective

Cost: $800-$2,500 whole-house; frequent media replacement needed

💡 Treatment System Selection Tips

  • Choose NSF-certified systems for the specific contaminant you need to remove
  • Size systems based on your water usage and contaminant levels
  • Consider whole-house vs point-of-use based on where you need protection
  • Budget for ongoing maintenance—filters need replacement, systems need servicing
  • Retest water after installation to verify treatment effectiveness
  • If you have multiple contaminants, you may need combination treatment (e.g., iron filter + softener + UV)

How Often to Test Your Well Water

Regular testing is the cornerstone of well water safety. Without routine testing, you won't know when contamination appears or when treatment systems stop working effectively.

Recommended Testing Schedule

Every Year (Minimum):

  • Total coliform bacteria
  • E. coli
  • Nitrates (especially in agricultural areas or if you have infants)
  • pH and total dissolved solids (good indicators of water chemistry changes)

Every 3-5 Years:

  • Arsenic
  • Lead and copper
  • Iron and manganese
  • Hardness
  • Sulfate, chloride, fluoride
  • Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) if near potential sources

Test More Frequently If:

  • You have a new baby: Test before use and annually for nitrates, bacteria, arsenic
  • Someone in the household is pregnant: Test for lead, nitrates, bacteria
  • Household members have compromised immune systems: Test bacteria quarterly
  • There's been flooding or ground disturbance near your well: Test within 2 weeks
  • You notice changes in water taste, odor, or appearance: Test immediately
  • Nearby wells test positive for contamination: Test for the same contaminants
  • Your area has known contamination issues: Test annually for those specific contaminants
  • You've done well work or plumbing repairs: Test before resuming normal use

One-Time or As-Needed Testing:

  • PFAS: If you're within 5 miles of military bases, airports, fire training facilities, or known PFAS sites
  • Pesticides/herbicides: If you're in agricultural areas or notice spray drift
  • Uranium and radium: If you're in areas with known radioactivity
  • Comprehensive inorganic analysis: Once for baseline, then as needed

Keep Good Records

Maintain a water testing logbook or folder with:

  • All test results with dates
  • Lab names and contact information
  • Notes about any water quality changes you observed
  • Treatment system maintenance records
  • Well maintenance and repair records

This documentation helps you:

  • Track trends over time
  • Prove treatment system effectiveness
  • Demonstrate due diligence if selling your property
  • Provide history to well contractors or water treatment professionals
  • Identify seasonal or environmental patterns

Well Construction Factors Affecting Safety

Even the cleanest aquifer can't keep your water safe if your well construction is faulty. Proper well construction is your first line of defense against contamination.

Critical Well Construction Elements

1. Sanitary Well Seal

A tight-fitting, vermin-proof cap that prevents surface water, insects, rodents, and debris from entering your well. Should be raised above ground level and slope away from the casing. Many older wells have inadequate seals—this is the #1 source of bacterial contamination.

2. Proper Casing Depth and Seal

Well casing should extend at least 20 feet below ground (California typically requires 50+ feet) and be sealed with grout in the annular space between the casing and borehole. This prevents shallow contaminated groundwater from entering the well. The casing should also extend 12-18 inches above ground or the concrete pad.

3. Adequate Well Depth

Wells should reach below the seasonal low water table and into aquifer zones that provide adequate water quality and quantity. Shallow wells (less than 50 feet) are more susceptible to surface contamination, seasonal variations, and drought impacts.

4. Proper Location and Setbacks

Wells must be located away from contamination sources. California minimum setbacks: 50 feet from septic tanks, 100 feet from leach fields and animal enclosures, 150 feet from sewage treatment areas. Uphill from contamination sources when possible. In reality, greater distances are better—contaminants can travel hundreds of feet in some soil conditions.

5. Surface Grading and Drainage

Ground around the wellhead should slope away in all directions to prevent ponding and surface water infiltration. No low spots or depressions that collect water near the well.

6. Vent and Cap Design

Well caps must be vented to prevent vacuum or pressure issues but screened to keep out insects and debris. Vent openings should point downward and have fine mesh screens.

Signs Your Well Construction May Be Compromised

  • Recurring bacteria in water tests despite treatment
  • Water quality that worsens after rain
  • Visible cracks in casing or concrete pad
  • Gaps around the well cap or missing seal
  • Evidence of rodents, insects, or debris in wellhead area
  • Pooling water around the well
  • Well is more than 20-30 years old (may not meet modern standards)
  • No visible grout seal between casing and ground

Well Inspection and Rehabilitation

If your well is older or showing signs of construction issues, have it inspected by a licensed well contractor. They can:

  • Video-inspect the casing for cracks or holes
  • Check seal integrity
  • Upgrade the well cap and seal
  • Add or repair annular grout seal
  • Extend casing if needed
  • Improve surface grading and drainage

Cost: Well inspection $200-$500; seal/cap upgrades $300-$800; grouting $500-$2,000; major rehabilitation $2,000-$10,000 depending on scope.

⚠️ Important

If you have recurring bacteria contamination despite shock chlorination and treatment, the problem is almost always well construction—not just the water itself. Don't keep treating symptoms; fix the structural issue allowing contamination to enter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is well water safe to drink?

Well water can be safe to drink, but it's not automatically safe. Unlike municipal water systems regulated by the EPA's Safe Drinking Water Act, private wells are the homeowner's responsibility. Well water safety depends on proper well construction, regular testing, local geology, nearby contamination sources, and appropriate treatment when needed. In California, approximately 2 million people rely on private wells. While many wells provide excellent drinking water, others contain bacteria, nitrates, arsenic, or other contaminants that require treatment. The only way to know if your well water is safe is through regular professional testing.

Are private wells regulated by the EPA?

No, the EPA does not regulate private wells serving fewer than 25 people or 15 service connections. The Safe Drinking Water Act only covers public water systems. Private well owners are entirely responsible for their water quality—testing, treatment, and maintenance. However, states can impose their own regulations. California requires testing at the time of property transfer and sets construction standards through well standards bulletins, but does not mandate routine testing for existing wells. This means most California well owners must voluntarily test and treat their water to ensure safety.

What are the most common contaminants in well water?

The most common well water contaminants are: (1) Coliform bacteria - found in 15-20% of private wells, indicating possible fecal contamination; (2) Nitrates - from fertilizers and septic systems, especially dangerous for infants; (3) Arsenic - naturally occurring in California geology, long-term exposure causes cancer; (4) Lead - from plumbing fixtures and well components; (5) Iron and manganese - cause staining and taste issues; (6) PFAS (forever chemicals) - from industrial sources and firefighting foam; (7) Hard water minerals (calcium/magnesium) - not harmful but cause scale buildup. Testing is the only way to identify which contaminants are present in your specific well.

How often should I test my well water?

Test your well water annually for bacteria (total coliform and E. coli) at minimum. Additionally, test every 3-5 years for nitrates, arsenic, lead, and other chemical contaminants. Test more frequently if: you have a new baby (before use and annually), there's been flooding or ground disturbance near your well, you notice changes in taste/odor/appearance, nearby wells have contamination issues, your area has known contamination risks (agricultural runoff, mining, industrial sites), or someone in your household has recurring gastrointestinal illness. California requires testing at property transfer but not routine testing, making voluntary annual testing critical for ongoing safety.

What is the California maximum contaminant level for arsenic?

California's maximum contaminant level (MCL) for arsenic in drinking water is 10 parts per billion (ppb or μg/L), matching the federal EPA standard. However, California also sets a Public Health Goal (PHG) of 0.004 ppb for arsenic, recognizing that no level is truly safe for long-term consumption. Many areas of California—including parts of San Diego County, the Central Valley, and Owens Valley—have naturally occurring arsenic in groundwater due to geology. Wells in these areas frequently exceed the 10 ppb MCL and require treatment. Even levels below 10 ppb pose long-term cancer risks, making treatment advisable for any detectable arsenic.

Can well water make you sick?

Yes, contaminated well water can cause illness ranging from acute gastrointestinal problems to serious long-term health effects. Bacteria (E. coli, coliform) cause diarrhea, cramps, nausea, and vomiting, with symptoms appearing within hours to days. High nitrate levels cause 'blue baby syndrome' in infants, reducing blood oxygen. Arsenic, lead, and PFAS don't cause immediate symptoms but lead to cancer, organ damage, and developmental problems with long-term exposure. Viruses and parasites (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) cause severe digestive illness. If household members experience recurring unexplained illness, especially gastrointestinal symptoms, test your well water immediately. Vulnerable populations (infants, elderly, immunocompromised) face higher risks.

What should I do if my well water tests positive for bacteria?

If your well water tests positive for total coliform or E. coli bacteria: (1) Stop drinking the water immediately—use bottled water for drinking, cooking, ice, and brushing teeth; (2) Boil water for 1 minute before use as a temporary measure; (3) Identify the contamination source—inspect well cap/seal, check for surface water intrusion, evaluate nearby septic systems or animal waste; (4) Shock chlorinate the well to kill bacteria; (5) Wait 1-2 weeks, flush the system thoroughly, then retest; (6) If bacteria return, investigate deeper issues—well construction defects, cracked casing, inadequate depth, or persistent contamination source; (7) Install continuous disinfection (UV or chlorination) if bacteria persist; (8) Consider well rehabilitation or reconstruction for structural issues.

How do I know if my well construction is safe?

Safe well construction includes: (1) Sanitary well seal/cap preventing surface water and pest entry; (2) Proper casing extending at least 20 feet deep (50+ feet in California) and sealed with grout; (3) Adequate depth reaching below seasonal water table fluctuations; (4) Minimum 50-foot separation from septic systems, 100+ feet from animal areas; (5) Grading directing surface water away from wellhead; (6) Vented cap preventing vacuum/pressure issues; (7) No cracks, holes, or gaps in casing; (8) Proper annular seal between casing and borehole. If your well is more than 20 years old, have it inspected by a licensed well contractor. Many older wells lack proper seals and need upgrading to meet modern safety standards.
