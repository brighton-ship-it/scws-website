What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your San Pasqual Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Wells in San Pasqual

San Pasqual is a historic rural area in central San Diego County, east of Escondido, known for the San Pasqual Battlefield, the surrounding farms, and the San Diego Zoo Safari Park that sits within the broader valley. It is true back country — large lots, working agricultural parcels, and homes scattered up the canyons and onto the granitic hills that frame the basin. Many of these properties have no municipal water main running to them at all; they depend entirely on private wells. That makes well performance a real, practical concern, and it makes hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) a technique worth understanding for the well owners who qualify.

The San Pasqual area is a mix of geology. Down on the valley floor, wells generally tap the alluvial sand-and-gravel aquifer, while up on the canyon slopes and rocky margins, wells are drilled into the fractured granitic bedrock of the Peninsular Ranges. Hydrofracturing works only on that bedrock — the crystalline rock with natural fractures — so the first question for any San Pasqual well owner is which kind of well you have.

The granite that frames San Pasqual is part of the Peninsular Ranges Batholith, the same Cretaceous plutonic belt that builds the foothills and mountains across inland San Diego County. Near the surface it often weathers into crumbly decomposed granite, but at well depth it firms into harder, fractured crystalline rock — and it is those deeper fractures that carry groundwater. How many fractures a borehole happens to intersect during drilling largely determines its yield, which is why two wells on similar parcels can perform very differently. A well that simply came up short on fractures is often a prime candidate to be opened up rather than abandoned.

How Hydrofracturing Works

A bedrock well produces water through a network of thin natural fractures threading through solid granite. Over the years those fractures fill with fine sediment and a coating of iron, manganese, and calcium scale, and the flow into the borehole drops. Hydrofracturing clears and reopens them. We isolate a section of the cased well with inflatable packers and inject clean water — no chemicals — at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes the fractures and opens hairline cracks, linking the well to a larger volume of water-bearing rock. The job typically runs four to eight hours. On a sand-and-gravel well there are no rock fractures to open, so the technique does not apply.

When It Helps in San Pasqual — and When It Doesn't

A strong candidate is a well drilled into fractured granite on the canyon slopes or rocky margins — often a few hundred feet deep — that has declined over time or always ran a little short. For those wells, hydrofracturing is exactly the right tool, and at a few thousand dollars it is far cheaper than drilling a new well.

It is the wrong tool for a well screened in the valley's alluvial sand and gravel, where the better options are screen rehabilitation, deepening, a new well, or treatment. Because San Pasqual contains both kinds of wells side by side, we never assume; we confirm your formation from the well log and a camera survey before recommending anything. If you fall on the alluvial side, we will tell you honestly and point you toward the repair that will actually move the needle.

The Process Step by Step

  1. Diagnostic and baseline flow: We determine whether the well is in bedrock or alluvium and measure current yield. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward any work.
  2. Downhole camera survey: We assess casing condition and, in a rock well, locate fracture zones to target.
  3. Packer isolation: Inflatable packers seal the treatment interval.
  4. High-pressure injection: Clean water flushes and opens fractures over four to eight hours.
  5. Development and re-test: We develop the well, re-measure, and provide a documented before-and-after comparison.

Realistic Results and Expectations

On genuine fractured-bedrock wells in this part of San Diego County, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, commonly two to five times the starting flow. Results are never guaranteed before testing — a well that missed the productive fractures may gain only a little — and we never quote a specific gallon figure up front. We measure your real before-and-after numbers so you know exactly what the treatment achieved.

Living on Well Water in San Pasqual

Because so many San Pasqual properties have no city connection, a struggling well is more than an inconvenience — it can affect a household's whole water supply or an operation's irrigation. Groundwater levels in the area rise and fall with the weather, drawing down in drought years and recovering after wet winters, so a slow well is not always a failed well. Sometimes it reflects a temporary dip in the regional table; sometimes it is a clogged fracture network that hydrofracturing can reopen; and sometimes it is a pump or screen problem entirely. Sorting out which is the first job of a good diagnosis.

If Hydrofracturing Isn't the Right Fix

We are deliberate about not recommending fracturing when something else is the real problem. If your water quantity is fine but the taste, odor, hardness, or iron content is poor, a treatment system is the answer. If the pump or pressure tank is failing, replacing that part may solve the issue for far less than any well work. And if the regional water table has dropped, deepening the borehole or drilling a new, properly sited well may be the durable solution. The $125 diagnostic exists to tell these cases apart so your money goes toward the repair your well genuinely needs.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Well location: Canyon slope or rocky margin (possible bedrock) versus valley floor (likely alluvium)?
  • Formation on the log: Granite or "hard rock" is a fracturing candidate; sand and gravel generally are not.
  • Yield trend: Has output declined over years, or after a dry spell?
  • Recovery time: A slow recovery after pumping is a telling clue.
  • Pump symptoms: Short-cycling or sputtering taps can mean low yield or a pump issue.

When to Call a Professional

Call when your well runs short during peak demand, when recovery has slowed, or when you are deciding between hydrofracturing and a new well. The high pressures involved make hydrofracturing unsuitable for do-it-yourself attempts — poor isolation can crack casing or breach the grout seal — and choosing the right repair for your particular well is something a licensed contractor should help you settle first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my San Pasqual well a candidate for hydrofracturing?

If it is drilled into fractured granite on the canyon slopes or rocky margins, yes. If it draws from valley-floor sand and gravel, usually not. Our diagnostic confirms which you have.

Is well hydrofracturing the same as oil-and-gas fracking?

No. We use only clean water to clean and open existing fractures in a water well — no chemicals and no new deep drilling.

How long does a treatment take?

Most jobs run four to eight hours, with flow verification over the next day or two.

How much more water can I expect?

Two to five times the starting flow is typical on qualifying rock wells, though results vary and are never guaranteed before testing.

My well is in the valley sediment — what should I do instead?

Screen rehabilitation, deepening, a new well, or water treatment is usually the better path. We'll recommend the right one after the diagnostic.

Do you charge for estimates?

No. Estimates are free, and the $125 diagnostic is credited toward any work you authorize.
