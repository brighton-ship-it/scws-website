Avocado Water Demands

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Bay Ho well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Well and Water Services in Bay Ho: An Honest Look at a City-Water Neighborhood

Bay Ho is a quiet, mostly mid-century residential neighborhood of the City of San Diego, tucked into the Clairemont area on the central coastal mesa. Sitting between Interstate 5 and Interstate 805, a short drive from Mission Bay and the coast, it is a built-out suburb of single-family homes on tidy streets — not farm country. So let us be straight with you from the start: if you searched for “avocado well services in Bay Ho,” the honest answer is that almost nobody here irrigates an avocado grove from a private well. The overwhelming majority of Bay Ho homes are connected to municipal water supplied by the City of San Diego Public Utilities Department, and private agricultural wells — avocado-irrigation wells in particular — are essentially nonexistent in this neighborhood.

That does not mean a well company has nothing to offer Bay Ho. Southern California Well Service has been working across San Diego County for more than 30 years, and we would rather give you accurate guidance than pretend your block is the avocado belt. Below we explain why city water dominates here, what the coastal-mesa groundwater picture actually looks like, the rare situations where a Bay Ho property might involve a well, and where the genuine avocado-growing opportunities — and our agricultural well work — really live in this county.

Why Nearly Every Bay Ho Home Is on Municipal Water

Bay Ho developed primarily in the post-war decades as a planned residential extension of Clairemont. By the time these homes were built, the City of San Diego had already extended treated, pressurized water mains throughout the mesa. When a reliable municipal connection is sitting at the curb, there is rarely any reason for a homeowner to drill, permit, equip, and maintain a private well. City water arrives filtered, disinfected, and pressure-regulated, and it is billed monthly rather than demanding a five-figure capital investment up front.

Lot sizes reinforce this. Bay Ho is a neighborhood of standard suburban parcels — comfortable yards for a family, room for a few fruit trees or a vegetable bed, but not the multi-acre acreage that makes an agricultural well pencil out. A private well only begins to make economic sense when you are irrigating large plantings or living somewhere the municipal main simply does not reach. Neither condition describes the typical Bay Ho address.

The San Diego Coastal-Mesa Groundwater Picture

There is also a geological reason private wells never took hold along this stretch of coast. Bay Ho sits on the coastal mesa above the San Diego Formation, a sedimentary aquifer system that underlies much of the central and southern part of the county. Compared with the deep, productive fractured-rock and alluvial aquifers found inland, groundwater near the coast here has real limitations:

  • Shallow and variable yield. Coastal-mesa groundwater can be relatively shallow, and the quantity a well can produce is often modest — not the steady high-volume flow an irrigated grove would require.
  • Brackish or saline conditions. Near the coast, groundwater is frequently affected by salinity and, in places, seawater intrusion. Elevated chlorides and total dissolved solids are common, which is a problem for drinking water and a serious problem for salt-sensitive landscaping.
  • Mineral content. Iron, manganese, hardness, and sediment can all show up in coastal-mesa water, requiring treatment before the water is genuinely usable.

Put simply, a clean, high-volume private water supply is uncommon on this part of the mesa. That combination of an easy city connection and a marginal aquifer is exactly why Bay Ho is a municipal-water neighborhood, and why we will never tell a homeowner here to drill a well they do not need.

The Rare Bay Ho Well: Legacy Wells and Landscape Irrigation

Occasionally we do hear from a Bay Ho or greater Clairemont property owner with a genuine well question, and it usually falls into one of two categories.

Legacy and Abandoned Wells

A handful of older or larger parcels carry a legacy well — a hole drilled decades ago, sometimes long before the surrounding neighborhood filled in, and often unused for years. An abandoned or improperly sealed well is not just dead weight on a property; it is a safety and groundwater-contamination liability, and California requires that wells no longer in service be properly destroyed by a licensed contractor. If you have an old well casing, a mysterious capped pipe, or paperwork referencing a well on your deed, the responsible step is professional decommissioning and abandonment to current state and county standards.

Landscape and Irrigation Wells

A small number of owners with larger lots ask about a well purely for landscape irrigation — watering ornamentals, lawns, or a backyard orchard without running up a city water bill. This can occasionally be worthwhile, but it deserves an honest feasibility conversation first. Given the shallow, often-brackish coastal groundwater described above, the water you reach may need treatment before it is suitable even for sensitive plants, and yield may disappoint. We would rather test and assess before anyone spends money drilling.

What to Check If You Have or Suspect a Well

If your Bay Ho property does have a well, or you are buying a home and want to understand one, here is a practical starting checklist:

  • Locate and inspect the wellhead. Confirm whether it is active, capped, or abandoned, and whether the casing is intact and properly sealed.
  • Test the water. A laboratory analysis for chlorides, total dissolved solids, iron, manganese, hardness, bacteria, and nitrates tells you what you are actually dealing with.
  • Check the equipment. Pumps, pressure tanks, and pressure switches all have finite lifespans. Cycling problems, sputtering, or sediment in the line are early warning signs.
  • Review the paperwork. Old well completion reports and permits clarify depth, construction, and history.

You should call a licensed professional when you notice a sudden drop in yield or pressure, discolored or foul-smelling water, a pump that short-cycles or runs constantly, or any sign that an unused well needs to be sealed. These are not DIY situations — a C-57 licensed well contractor has the equipment and the legal standing to do the work correctly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my Bay Ho home run on a private well?

Almost certainly not. Bay Ho is a built-out residential neighborhood of the City of San Diego, and nearly every home here is connected to municipal water through the City of San Diego Public Utilities Department. Private wells are very rare in this part of the coastal mesa.

Can I grow avocados in Bay Ho using well water?

Realistically, no. Bay Ho is a small-lot residential suburb without agricultural wells, and the coastal groundwater here tends to be shallow and often brackish. Serious avocado growing belongs inland in places like Fallbrook, Bonsall, and Valley Center, where the climate, soils, and groundwater support it.

Why is groundwater near the San Diego coast often unusable?

Coastal-mesa groundwater above the San Diego Formation is frequently affected by salinity and seawater influence, with elevated chlorides and dissolved solids, plus iron, hardness, and sediment. It can also be low-yielding. Treatment is usually required before such water is suitable for drinking or sensitive landscaping.

I found an old well on my property. What should I do?

Have it assessed by a licensed C-57 contractor. An unused well that is not properly sealed is a safety and contamination risk, and California requires abandoned wells to be professionally destroyed. Decommissioning typically runs $1,500–$5,000 depending on depth and access.

Is it worth drilling a well just for landscape irrigation?

Sometimes, but only after testing. On the coastal mesa the water you reach may be brackish or low-yielding, requiring treatment that erodes the savings. We recommend a feasibility assessment and water test before anyone invests in drilling for irrigation.

Do you serve Bay Ho even though it is not avocado country?

Yes. We serve all of San Diego County from our Ramona and Anza offices, including water testing, treatment, pump and pressure service, and old-well decommissioning for residential clients in Bay Ho and the wider Clairemont and coastal communities.
