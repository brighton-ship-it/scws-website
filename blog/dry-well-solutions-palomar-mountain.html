Why Wells Go Dry

  • Drought Conditions: Extended dry periods lower the water table
  • Increased Demand: More wells in the area drawing from the same aquifer
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: Water table naturally drops in summer/fall
  • Pump Position: Pump may be positioned above current water level
  • Well Age: Mineral buildup reducing water entry points
  • Aquifer Depletion: Long-term overdrawing of groundwater

Solutions for Dry Wells in Palomar Mountain

1. Well Deepening

If the water table has dropped, deepening your existing well may reach water again. This is often less expensive than drilling a new well.

2. Hydrofracturing

High-pressure water treatment can open new fractures in bedrock, allowing more water to flow into your well.

3. Pump Lowering

Sometimes simply lowering your pump deeper in the well can restore water production.

4. New Well Drilling

When rehabilitation isn't viable, we can drill a new well in a better location on your property.

Dry Wells on Palomar Mountain: High-Elevation Geology and Real Solutions

Palomar Mountain rises to more than 6,100 feet in the northeastern corner of San Diego County, a forested island of pines, cedars, and meadows above the surrounding chaparral. Famous for its observatory and its cool, wet microclimate — the mountain can receive several times the rainfall of the valleys far below — Palomar is also home to rural residents who depend entirely on private wells and springs. Yet even a mountain that catches the clouds can have wells run dry, because elevation and rainfall do not guarantee that water sits where a well can reach it. When a Palomar Mountain well stops producing, the fix starts with understanding the mountain's hard-rock geology.

Southern California Well Service has more than 30 years of backcountry experience, and our Anza office sits close to the Palomar region. Here is why mountain wells go dry and what your options are.

Why Palomar Mountain Wells Go Dry

Palomar is built of hard granitic and metamorphic bedrock. Despite the mountain's generous rainfall, that water does not collect in a deep sandy aquifer; it runs off quickly, feeds springs and seeps, and stores underground only in the cracks and fracture networks of the rock. A well on Palomar lives or dies by the fractures it intersects:

  • Fracture-fed wells. A well that taps a productive fracture can do well; one that misses the good fractures may be marginal even on a wet mountain. Two nearby wells can perform completely differently.
  • Seasonal swing. The mountain's water levels rise with winter and spring precipitation and fall through the dry summer and autumn, so a fracture-fed well can go marginal late in the dry season.
  • Drought years. Even Palomar feels multi-year droughts; reduced recharge lowers the level feeding the fractures.
  • Wells drilled too shallow or into weak fractures. Older wells that never hit strong fractures struggle as levels fall.
  • Pump set above the water. A pump hanging above the current level pulls air before the well is truly dry.

Confirming the Well Is Really Dry

On a fracture-dependent mountain, measurement is decisive. Our technician sounds the well for the static and pumping water levels, tests the pump's amperage, and measures recovery after a controlled drawdown. A well that draws down hard and recovers slowly is yield-limited; one with standing water but no output has an equipment or placement problem. We review the driller's report for fracture intervals and inspect the pressure tank and controls. The $125 diagnostic is credited toward any repair.

Solutions for a Dry Palomar Mountain Well

Lower the Pump

If the well still holds water but the pump sits above it, resetting the pump deeper in the casing is the fastest, cheapest fix — $800 to $2,500 — when there is depth and water column to spare.

Hydrofracturing

Hydrofracturing is well suited to Palomar's hard-rock wells. High-pressure injection clears and opens fractures, often substantially improving a marginal mountain well. Cost runs $3,000 to $8,000, and on a fracture-fed mountain it is frequently the best value short of a new well.

Deepen the Existing Well

Where the bore no longer reaches productive fractures, deepening to intersect new water-bearing zones can revive the well for $5,000 to $15,000, far below the cost of starting over.

Storage Tank and Booster Pump

Low, steady yield from a fracture is common on Palomar, and a storage tank with a booster pump is an ideal match: the well fills a cistern around the clock while the booster delivers strong pressure on demand. Budget roughly $3,000 to $7,000. This approach pairs naturally with the spring-and-cistern setups many mountain properties already use.

Flow-Management Controls

Low-water cutoffs, timers, and flow controllers protect the pump and let a modest fracture yield serve a household reliably through the dry season.

Drill a New, Deeper Well

For a worn-out or poorly producing well, a new well drilled to better fractures is the durable answer, at $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey. Access and terrain on the mountain can affect the work, and we treat a new well as a last resort after rehabilitation is ruled out.

When to Call a Professional

Air in the lines, sand, a short-cycling pump, or whole-house pressure loss mean you should shut the pump down and call a licensed C-57 contractor. Running a pump dry destroys motors quickly. We are licensed and equipped to work inside the well itself, which a general plumber is not, and our crews are experienced with mountain access.

Frequently Asked Questions

If Palomar Mountain gets so much rain, why do wells go dry?

Because the mountain is built of hard granitic and metamorphic bedrock. Rainfall runs off quickly and stores underground only in the cracks and fractures of the rock, not in a deep sandy aquifer. A well that doesn't intersect productive fractures can be marginal even on a wet mountain.

Why do two nearby wells on the mountain perform so differently?

Palomar wells depend on the specific fracture network each one taps. One well can hit a strong, productive fracture while a neighbor's misses it. That is why hydrofracturing — which opens and clears fractures — is often so effective up here.

Is hydrofracturing a good option on Palomar Mountain?

Usually yes. Hydrofracturing suits hard-rock, fracture-fed wells like Palomar's, injecting high-pressure water to clear and open fractures and boost yield. At $3,000 to $8,000 it is frequently the best value short of drilling a new well.

My mountain well drops every fall. What can I do?

That seasonal swing is normal on Palomar, where levels rise with winter and spring precipitation and fall through the dry months. Lowering the pump, adding a storage tank and booster, or hydrofracturing can keep a fracture-fed well producing through the dry season.

Can a storage tank work alongside my spring?

Yes. Many Palomar properties already use spring-and-cistern setups, and a storage tank with a booster pump integrates naturally. The well or spring fills the cistern continuously while the booster delivers strong household pressure on demand, typically for $3,000 to $7,000.
