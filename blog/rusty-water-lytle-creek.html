What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

Rusty, Orange, or Red Well Water in Lytle Creek

Lytle Creek sits in a narrow canyon at the mouth of the San Gabriel Mountains, northwest of San Bernardino and Fontana, where the creek that gives the community its name spills out of the high country toward the Cajon corridor. Homes here are almost entirely on private wells, and the water those wells draw is shaped by the canyon itself: coarse, boulder-strewn creek-wash alluvium near the streambed and hard, fractured granitic and metamorphic bedrock in the surrounding slopes. When that water starts coming out of the tap rusty, orange, brown, or red-tinted, it is almost always an iron problem somewhere between the aquifer and your faucet. The good news for Lytle Creek homeowners is that rusty water is one of the most diagnosable and fixable well problems there is, once you know where the iron is coming from.

Why Lytle Creek Wells Are Prone to Iron and Rust

Iron is one of the most abundant elements in the rocks of this part of San Bernardino County, and the geology around Lytle Creek practically guarantees that it ends up in groundwater. The fractured bedrock of the San Gabriel range carries iron-bearing minerals that slowly dissolve into water moving through the cracks. The thick, coarse alluvium washed down the canyon over thousands of years is full of rock fragments that do the same thing. Water that sits in low-oxygen conditions deep in a well dissolves this iron in its clear, "ferrous" form, the kind you cannot see in a freshly drawn glass but that turns orange the moment it hits air and oxidizes.

Lytle Creek adds a second wrinkle that flatter, desert communities do not have: storm and flood dynamics. The canyon is a flashy watershed, and heavy winter storms or debris flows can push a surge of sediment, organic material, and surface water through shallow aquifers and into the gravel beds that some wells tap. After a big storm, it is common to see a temporary spike in cloudiness, sediment, and rust color as that disturbed water works through the system. A well that runs clear most of the year but turns orange every February is telling you something specific about how surface water is interacting with the aquifer.

The Five Common Sources of Rust

When we trace rusty water for a Lytle Creek household, the cause almost always falls into one of five categories:

  • Dissolved (ferrous) iron in the groundwater itself. Naturally occurring, dissolved out of the canyon's rock and gravel. Water looks clear at the tap, then turns orange in a glass or stains fixtures over hours.
  • Iron bacteria. Harmless-to-drink but nuisance organisms that feed on iron and leave behind a slimy, reddish-brown sludge. They clog screens, coat toilet tanks, and produce a swampy or oily film. Common in wells that draw shallow, oxygen-touched water near a creek.
  • A corroding steel well casing. Older Lytle Creek wells were often cased in steel, and decades of contact with mineralized water rusts the casing from the inside, shedding flakes of rust into the water column.
  • Corroding galvanized household plumbing. Many of the canyon's older cabins and homes still have galvanized iron supply lines. As the zinc coating wears away, the pipe rusts internally and releases orange water, especially first thing in the morning after water has sat overnight.
  • A failing water heater. A rusting anode rod or a tank corroding from the inside produces rusty water that shows up only on the hot side.

How to Pinpoint Where Your Rust Is Coming From

Before spending a dollar on treatment, you can narrow the cause with a few simple observations at home:

  1. Hot only, or hot and cold? Run the cold tap at a fixture that bypasses any treatment. If only the hot water is rusty, your water heater is the prime suspect. If both are rusty, the problem is upstream, in the well or the main supply line.
  2. Sudden or gradual? A gradual worsening over months points to a corroding casing or slowly rising iron. A sudden change after a storm points to surface-water intrusion or disturbed sediment in the aquifer.
  3. Worse after the water sits, or worse during heavy use? Rust that is strongest first thing in the morning suggests household plumbing corrosion. Rust that worsens when the pump runs hard, or when the well is drawn down during heavy irrigation, points to the well and casing.
  4. Clear-then-orange, or orange-from-the-tap? Water that is clear when drawn and turns orange in the glass is classic dissolved iron. Water that comes out already orange and cloudy is carrying rust particles or sediment.
  5. Any slime or odor? A slimy reddish coating in the toilet tank or a swampy smell strongly suggests iron bacteria rather than simple dissolved iron.

Whatever you find, the definitive answer comes from a water test that measures total and dissolved iron, manganese, hardness, pH, and bacteria. We include a thorough water analysis as part of a diagnostic visit so the treatment is matched to the actual chemistry rather than a guess.

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

At the iron concentrations typical of Lytle Creek wells, iron itself is considered a secondary, aesthetic contaminant rather than a health hazard. It will not poison you, but it tastes metallic, stains laundry and fixtures, ruins the flavor of coffee and tea, and can harbor the bacteria and biofilm that cause odors. More importantly, rusty water is often a messenger: it can signal a deteriorating casing, surface-water intrusion that may carry actual pathogens after storms, or other changes in water quality. That is why we always pair iron treatment with a broader water-quality check, especially for canyon wells exposed to flood events.

Solutions That Fit a Lytle Creek Well

The right fix depends entirely on the source, which is why diagnosis comes first. Common solutions include:

  • Oxidizing / greensand iron filters. The workhorse for dissolved iron. The filter oxidizes ferrous iron and traps it, then backwashes the rust away. Installed systems typically run $1,500-$3,500 depending on flow rate and iron load.
  • Aeration and chemical injection. For higher iron levels or where manganese and sulfur are also present, an aeration or chlorine/peroxide injection stage ahead of filtration gives more complete removal.
  • Water softeners. For modest iron alongside the hard water common in this granitic terrain, a softener (roughly $1,500-$3,500) can handle low iron levels while also tackling scale.
  • Shock chlorination and well rehabilitation. The targeted fix for iron bacteria. Disinfecting and cleaning the well removes the bacterial colony and its slime; this often runs a few hundred dollars and may need periodic repetition.
  • Casing repair or replacement. A severely corroded steel casing can be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination at the source. Sediment filtration ($300-$900) helps with particulate rust in the meantime.
  • Replacing galvanized plumbing. If the rust is from old galvanized supply lines, switching to PEX or copper eliminates the household source for good.

What You Can Check Yourself, and When to Call a Pro

It is reasonable for a homeowner to run the hot-versus-cold test, flush the water heater, check whether the problem follows storms, and replace point-of-use cartridge filters to see if the color clears. If flushing the heater fixes a hot-only problem, you may be done. But once rusty water is present on both hot and cold sides, comes and goes with the pump or the seasons, or brings slime and odor with it, the diagnosis moves into the well itself, and that is where professional testing and equipment matter. Pulling a pump, inspecting a casing with a downhole camera, or shock-chlorinating a well are not DIY jobs in a steep canyon setting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Lytle Creek well water turn orange only after it sits in a glass?

That is the signature of dissolved ferrous iron. Deep in the well, with little oxygen, iron stays dissolved and invisible. When the water hits the air, it oxidizes into the orange rust particles you can see. An oxidizing iron filter is usually the most effective fix.

My water turns rusty every winter after big storms. Why?

Lytle Creek is a flashy canyon watershed, and heavy storms can push sediment and surface water into shallow aquifers and gravel beds that some wells tap. That can cause a seasonal spike in cloudiness and rust color. It is worth testing for bacteria after storm events, since surface-water intrusion can carry more than just iron.

Is the rusty water making my family sick?

Iron at typical well concentrations is an aesthetic problem, not a health hazard, so it is generally safe to drink even though it tastes metallic and stains. The bigger reason to address it is that rust can signal a corroding casing or storm-driven contamination, which is why we recommend a full water test alongside treatment.

How do I know if it is my well or my home's pipes?

Run a cold tap that bypasses any treatment. If only the hot water is rusty, suspect the water heater. If rust is worst in the morning after water has sat, suspect old galvanized household plumbing. If both hot and cold are rusty and it tracks with the pump or the seasons, the problem is in the well or casing.

What does it cost to fix rusty water?

It depends on the cause. Shock chlorination for iron bacteria can run a few hundred dollars; an oxidizing iron filter typically runs $1,500-$3,500 installed; a water softener for lower iron runs $1,500-$3,500; sediment filtration runs $300-$900.
