What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lake Arrowhead Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Lake Arrowhead

Lake Arrowhead sits at roughly 5,200 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, a forested resort community surrounded by the San Bernardino National Forest and centered on its private alpine lake. The broader Lake Arrowhead area takes in Blue Jay, Twin Peaks, Cedar Glen, Sky Forest, Rim Forest, Crest Park, and the villas and tracts scattered along the Rim of the World. Many homes here, especially those off the central water system, rely on private wells drilled into the mountain's granite. When one of those wells begins producing less than it used to, hydrofracturing (also called hydrofracking) is frequently the most cost-effective way to restore flow without drilling a new well on a steep, tree-covered lot.

Granite Wells in the Lake Arrowhead Area

There is little soft sediment up here. Wells are drilled into the hard crystalline granite of the San Bernardino range, and water collects in the cracks, joints, and fracture zones within that rock. A Lake Arrowhead well does not draw from a broad underground reservoir the way a valley well does; it depends on the particular network of water-bearing seams it happens to intersect. That makes the area a strong fit for hydrofracturing. The treatment opens and cleans those seams, and the solid granite holds the new openings apart afterward. In loose, sandy ground a fracture would collapse shut, but mountain granite keeps it propped open, so the gains tend to last.

Why Lake Arrowhead Wells Lose Water

Over the years the seams feeding a granite well gradually narrow as fine mineral scale, mountain silt, and iron and manganese deposits build up along the fracture faces. Many homeowners first notice the change during the dry stretch from late summer into fall, when a well that used to run the household comfortably now sputters by evening, recovers slowly overnight, and leaves the pump short-cycling. A well drilled a few hundred feet into Lake Arrowhead granite usually still has water in the rock around it; the problem is that the pathways into the borehole have tightened. Snowmelt and winter storms recharge the mountain slowly through fractures, so in a string of dry years the seams feeding a marginal well are the first to draw down, and a well that was adequate when it was drilled in a wetter decade can fall behind.

How Hydrofracturing Opens Up Granite

Hydrofracturing uses nothing but clean water under high pressure. We lower inflatable packers into the cased and open-hole sections of your well to seal off a target zone, then pump water into that isolated interval at pressures that commonly run from about 1,000 up to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes accumulated sediment out of existing fractures and gently widens and extends the natural cracks, reconnecting your borehole to seams that had silted shut or were never fully open. Because the Lake Arrowhead area's bedrock is genuine fractured granite, it responds to this treatment far better than soft ground would. The whole job is a closed water process, with no chemicals and nothing added to your aquifer.

Signs Your Lake Arrowhead Well Could Benefit

  • Steady decline: the well produces noticeably less than it did a few years ago, even after a normal-snow winter.
  • Afternoon slowdowns: pressure and volume fade under heavy use and take hours to recover.
  • A new well that disappointed: a recently drilled hole hit granite but came in with weak flow.
  • Frequent pump cycling: the pump kicks on and off because the well cannot keep up.
  • Seasonal shortfalls: you ration water through September and October every year.

What to Check Before You Assume the Well Is Failing

Not every drop in water at the tap is a yield problem, and at this elevation some causes are seasonal. Look at the pressure tank gauge first; a low reading or a waterlogged tank often points to a failed pressure tank or a worn pressure switch rather than a dry well. Check whether the issue affects the whole property or just one fixture, since a single weak faucet usually means a clogged aerator. Lake Arrowhead winters are cold enough that frozen lines and tanks can mimic a yield loss until they thaw. And if the pump draws air or the water turns sandy, the trouble may be a dropping water level reaching the pump, or a worn pump, rather than the aquifer. When the well genuinely recovers more slowly than it used to and the decline has crept in over seasons, that is the classic signature of a tightening fracture network, which is what hydrofracturing addresses.

When to Call a Professional

If your well has clearly lost ground year over year, if a newer borehole came in weak, or if you are weighing a new well against rehabilitating the one you have, that is the time for a licensed contractor to run a flow test and camera inspection. A new well in the San Bernardino high country is a five-figure undertaking, so a proper diagnosis is well worth its modest cost. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward the work if you move forward.

The Step-by-Step Process

  1. Baseline flow test: we measure current yield and check static and pumping water levels for a real before-number.
  2. Downhole camera survey: a video inspection shows casing condition and the fracture zones worth targeting.
  3. Packer isolation: we seal the chosen interval so the pressure concentrates on that zone.
  4. High-pressure injection: clean water clears and extends the fractures, usually four to eight hours on site.
  5. Post-treatment flow test: we re-measure yield after the well settles 24 to 48 hours and provide a documented before-and-after comparison.

What Hydrofracturing Will and Will Not Do

Clear expectations make for satisfied customers. Hydrofracturing does not create new groundwater and it cannot undo a dry winter; it reconnects your borehole to the water already moving through the Lake Arrowhead area's granite by clearing and widening the seams that feed it. On a genuine fractured-rock well that has slowly lost production, that is usually enough to restore a comfortable household supply. It will not rescue a well whose water level has truly dropped below the pump in a deep drought, where lowering the pump may be the better answer, and it is not the right tool for a well drawing from a pocket of loose decomposed-granite sand. Because nearly all wells in the area sit in solid fractured granite, most are good candidates, but we confirm that with a flow test and camera survey first.

After Treatment: Water Quality and Winter Care

For the first hours to a day after treatment, expect cloudy water as the opened fractures flush silt and fine mineral material; it clears as the well develops, and we run the well to move that sediment through. Mountain granite can carry naturally elevated iron and manganese, and opening new seams may briefly shift the mineral mix until the well settles, so service any iron filter or softener afterward. Because the Lake Arrowhead area sits above 5,000 feet, protecting your wellhead, pressure tank, and exposed lines from freezing keeps your restored flow reliable through winter. A periodic flow check lets you catch any renewed decline early, when a simple re-treatment is far easier than an emergency during a January storm.

Cost Compared to a New Well in Lake Arrowhead

Drilling a new well in the San Bernardino high country is expensive and uncertain, given the depth needed to reach reliable granite seams and the difficulty of getting a rig onto a steep forest lot above the lake. A turnkey new well generally runs from about $18,000 to $42,000. Hydrofracturing an existing well usually costs between $3,000 and $8,000, and it improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of the fractured-rock wells we treat, with typical gains of two to five times the starting flow. For most Lake Arrowhead homeowners, it is simply the smart first move before committing to a new borehole.
