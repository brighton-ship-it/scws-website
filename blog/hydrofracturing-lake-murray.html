What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lake Murray Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in the Lake Murray Area

Lake Murray is a reservoir and residential neighborhood in eastern San Diego, bordered by La Mesa, San Carlos, and Del Cerro along the lower San Diego River corridor. This is established, city-served suburbia, so private water wells inside the Lake Murray neighborhood itself are rare. The well owners who land on a page like this are usually responsible for a private well farther out—on a rural lot in the inland canyons and ridges east of the city. We have written this guide for them, and we are candid about when hydrofracturing is the right tool and when it is not.

Hydrofracturing increases the yield of wells drilled into hard, fractured bedrock by using high-pressure water to reopen and extend the natural cracks that supply them. In the granitic and crystalline rock of San Diego County's interior, it is one of the most cost-effective ways to revive a tired well. In a well producing from deep, loose valley-floor sediment, however, there are no fractures to enhance—so we are upfront that such wells are weak candidates.

The Mechanics of Hydrofracturing

An inflatable packer seals off a chosen section of your well, and we then inject clean water at pressures of about 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. The pressure flushes silt and scale out of the existing rock fractures and widens them so groundwater can move toward the borehole more freely. Treating multiple zones in a single well is common; we simply reposition the packer. The job typically runs four to eight hours and uses only water—never chemicals.

Matching the Method to Your Well

The decisive question is what your well is drilled into. A rural well in the inland hills completed in fractured rock is a promising candidate. A well drawing from deep alluvium is not, and for those we more often recommend adjusting pump depth, redeveloping the well, or deepening it. The only way to know your well's category is a diagnostic that includes a look at your well log and a downhole camera survey. We would rather give you that clarity than book a treatment unlikely to pay off.

Signs Your Well May Be Failing

  • Yield trending downward — less water than the well once made
  • Drawing down quickly under load — the well cannot keep pace with demand
  • Slow recovery — long waits for the water level to rebuild after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off — a sign the level is dropping below the intake
  • Dry-season pressure loss — a familiar late-summer decline inland

Realistic Outcomes

For fractured-rock wells in San Diego County, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, with typical gains of two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate. Some wells improve dramatically; a few barely move. We do not promise a number, but we do document your flow before and after so the result is measurable. Wells in genuinely hard rock respond best, which is exactly the kind of honest expectation-setting we bring to every job.

Step-by-Step Service

  1. Diagnostic and baseline flow test — we establish current yield; the $125 diagnostic fee is credited toward the work if you proceed
  2. Well log and casing review — we determine whether your well reaches fracturable rock and confirm casing integrity
  3. Video inspection — a camera survey identifies fracture zones and sediment
  4. Hydrofracturing — targeted high-pressure injection, four to eight hours

Cost vs. a New Well

Hydrofracturing usually costs $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000 including drilling, casing, pump, and hookup, and a replacement pump on its own is $2,500 to $5,500. Whenever your existing well is rock-fed and the casing is sound, trying hydrofracturing first is the financially sensible path. It costs a small fraction of what a new well would, and it preserves the investment you already have in the existing borehole.

When Deepening or Redrilling Is Smarter

If a camera shows a collapsed or corroded casing, if the borehole has filled with debris, or if the well was drilled too shallow, deepening or drilling new may be the better long-term choice. And when an alluvial well is simply low because the regional water table has fallen, the answer is reaching deeper water, not fracturing rock that is not there. We always present the trade-offs plainly so your decision is fully informed.

Why a Bigger Pump Is Rarely the Answer

A common first instinct when a well slows down is to install a more powerful pump, but that approach often backfires. If the formation around the borehole can only deliver so much water, a stronger pump simply draws the well down faster, pulls in air, and can run the pump dry—shortening its life and leaving you no better off. The real constraint is how much water the rock can supply, and that is what hydrofracturing addresses. By reopening the fractures, the treatment raises the well's sustainable output so a properly sized pump can actually use it. In practice, the most reliable results come from pairing a hydrofracturing treatment with a pump that is matched to the newly restored flow rate, rather than oversizing one component and starving the other. For rural well owners east of Lake Murray, understanding that distinction prevents an expensive detour that does not fix the underlying problem.

Caring for Your Well Over the Long Run

After a successful treatment, a little maintenance keeps your well dependable. We recommend an annual flow and water-level check, periodic inspection of the pressure tank and pump switch, and attention to water quality. The hard water common throughout San Diego County deposits scale that slowly re-narrows fractures, so monitoring and modest treatment where needed protect your improved yield for years. On a rural property where the well is the sole water source, an annual checkup is inexpensive insurance, and it lets us catch a gradual decline long before it becomes a no-water emergency.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hydrofracturing in Lake Murray

Does the Lake Murray neighborhood have private wells?

Very few. Lake Murray, San Carlos, and Del Cerro are city-served suburban areas. Most well owners researching this topic have a private well on a rural lot in the surrounding inland country, where hydrofracturing can be relevant if the well is in fractured rock.

How do I know if my well is a good hydrofracturing candidate?

It comes down to geology. Wells in the hard, fractured granitic rock of San Diego County's interior are good candidates about 70 to 80 percent improve. Wells in deep loose sediment are weak candidates. We use a well log review and downhole camera to determine yours.

What does hydrofracturing cost?

Typically $3,000 to $8,000. A new turnkey well is $18,000 to $42,000 and a replacement pump $2,500 to $5,500, so for a sound rock-fed well, hydrofracturing is usually the more economical first option.

How much will my yield improve?

Typical gains are two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate for fractured-rock wells. Because every well differs, we measure and document your flow before and after rather than guaranteeing a specific number.

Will the process contaminate my water?

No. Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under high pressure, with no chemicals or additives. It is fundamentally different from oil and gas fracking and poses no contamination risk.

What if hydrofracturing won't help my well?

If your well is alluvial and low because the water table dropped, or its casing is damaged, we recommend the right alternative pump adjustment, redevelopment, deepening, or a new well instead of a treatment that will not solve your problem.
