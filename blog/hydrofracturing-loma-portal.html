What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Loma Portal Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing and Loma Portal: Guidance for Rural Well Owners

Loma Portal is a leafy, established neighborhood on the Point Loma peninsula, near Liberty Station and the airport, with mature streets and Spanish-revival homes well within the City of San Diego's water system. There are no private wells in Loma Portal itself, and we will not suggest otherwise. The reason this page exists is that Point Loma and coastal San Diego residents frequently own property elsewhere in the county, rural acreage in the East County hills and back-country valleys where a private well, not a municipal main, supplies the home. For those wells, hydrofracturing is often the most economical way to recover lost yield before considering a costly new well.

Hydrofracturing, also spelled hydrofracking, drives high-pressure water into a low-yield bedrock well to reopen the natural rock fractures that feed it. On inland San Diego County's granitic terrain, it reliably restores production in most of the wells we treat, and it does so at a small fraction of what a replacement well would cost.

What Happens Down the Borehole

A well in hard rock does not draw from an open aquifer; it draws from a lattice of cracks in the granite and crystalline rock. Those cracks are the well's plumbing, and over the years the ones nearest the borehole silt up with sediment and mineral scale, slowly choking the flow. Hydrofracturing reopens them. We isolate a section of the well with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes the clogged fractures and forces open tight, marginally connected cracks nearby, expanding the network of water-bearing seams that feed the well. It is purely water and pressure, no chemicals, and the field work is typically done within four to eight hours.

Geology Determines Whether It Works

The deciding factor is what your well is completed in. Inland San Diego County rests on the granitic and metamorphic basement of the Peninsular Ranges, weathered to decomposed granite near the surface and hard at depth. Wells in that rock are prime candidates. Wells finished in deep alluvial valley fill, the unconsolidated sand and gravel along streambeds, are poor candidates, since there are no fractures for pressure to open. We examine your well log and, when appropriate, run a downhole camera to confirm you are in rock, so we never recommend a treatment that cannot physically succeed.

Signs Your Well Could Benefit

  • Year-over-year decline in production.
  • Drawing down to air during irrigation or peak household use.
  • Slow recovery, with levels taking hours to rebound.
  • A short-cycling pump the borehole cannot sustain.
  • A new well that underdelivered despite reaching rock.

What You Can Realistically Expect

Across San Diego County's decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of treated bedrock wells, with successful jobs commonly producing a two-to-fivefold increase over baseline. We speak in ranges deliberately, because subsurface rock is never fully predictable. The safeguard is measurement: we baseline your flow before treatment and re-test afterward, so you receive documented results, not assurances.

How We Handle the Job

  1. Diagnostic and flow test ($125, credited) measuring current yield.
  2. Camera survey to confirm casing and identify productive fracture zones.
  3. Hydrofracturing staged high-pressure injection, generally one day on site.
  4. Post-treatment test after the well settles over 24 to 48 hours.
  5. Written report a clear before-and-after.

Cost Compared With Drilling New

Hydrofracturing usually costs $3,000-$8,000. A new turnkey well in rural San Diego County typically runs $18,000-$42,000 depending on depth and equipment, and a replacement pump, if needed, is about $2,500-$5,500. With that kind of spread, treating a sound bedrock well first is almost always the prudent first step.

When We Steer You Toward a New Well

We decline hydrofracture work that will not help. If a camera reveals failed casing, a collapsing borehole, or a well that never reached a producing fracture, deepening or redrilling is the honest recommendation, and a shallow alluvial well has nothing for the process to open. We would rather give you that truth than charge for a treatment doomed to disappoint.

Coastal Owners, Inland Wells

It is worth noting that the well challenges facing a Point Loma resident's inland parcel are very different from anything along the coast. The productive groundwater is in fractured rock tens or hundreds of feet down, not in the shallow coastal sediments near the bay. That is good news, because fractured-rock wells are exactly the kind hydrofracturing was built to help. When you call us about a back-country well, we will ask about the well log, the completion depth, and the symptoms, so we can tell you over the phone whether the property is likely a strong candidate before anyone drives out.

Recovery Rate Versus Raw Flow

Owners often fixate on gallons per minute, but how quickly a well recovers between draws frequently matters more for daily comfort. A well can post a decent instantaneous flow yet still leave a household short if it draws down fast and refills slowly. By broadening the fracture network feeding the borehole, hydrofracturing tends to improve both the peak flow and the recovery rate, and that faster rebound is often what makes a marginal well feel dependable again through a dry San Diego County summer.

Doing the Job the Right Way

A hydrofracture is only as good as the crew performing it. We target the productive intervals our camera identifies, isolate them properly with packers, stage the pressure rather than blasting the whole borehole, and verify casing integrity before pressurizing, all of which protects your well and improves the odds of a lasting result. That care, honed over three decades of well work across San Diego County, is what distinguishes a treatment that holds for years from one that fades fast.

Extending the Life of an Older Inland Well

Plenty of the inland parcels connected to Point Loma owners were drilled decades ago, and the assumption when one slows down is that a costly replacement is due. Frequently that is not so. A well completed in good fractured rock that remains structurally sound can often be revived with a single hydrofracture, pushing the need for a new well out by years. The catch is timing: rehabilitation works best before the casing or borehole degrades past the point of saving. That is why we urge owners to call at the first sign of declining yield rather than waiting for the well to fail outright, an early treatment on a sound well is both less expensive and far more likely to succeed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does anyone in Loma Portal use a private well?

No, Loma Portal is on city water. People searching this topic usually own rural San Diego County property where wells are common and hydrofracturing can extend a well's productive life.

Will hydrofracturing affect my water quality?

No. It uses only clean water under pressure, with no chemicals, so it does not alter water quality the way oil-and-gas fracking can.

How much more water will I get?

Successful bedrock jobs often deliver a two-to-fivefold increase, though the rock dictates the outcome. We document your before-and-after flow so the gain is verifiable.

How does it compare in cost to a new well?

Hydrofracturing is roughly $3,000-$8,000 versus $18,000-$42,000 for a new turnkey well, which is why we recommend trying it first on a sound bedrock well.

Might my well need treating again later?

Possibly, years down the road as minerals re-accumulate, but many wells run strong for the better part of a decade after a single treatment.

Can you respond quickly to a dry well?

Yes. We offer same-day emergency service for non-producing wells and schedule diagnostics promptly.
