Why Decommission a Well?

  • Legal Requirement: California law requires proper abandonment of unused wells
  • Groundwater Protection: Open wells can contaminate aquifers
  • Safety Hazard: Old wells pose falling risks for children and animals
  • Property Sale: Real estate transactions often require well documentation
  • New Construction: Building over an old well requires proper abandonment

Well Decommissioning Process

  1. Permit Application: We obtain required permits from San Diego County
  2. Well Assessment: Inspect well depth, casing condition, and construction
  3. Equipment Removal: Remove pump, piping, and electrical components
  4. Sealing: Fill well with approved grout/cement from bottom to top
  5. Surface Seal: Concrete cap installed below grade
  6. Documentation: File completion report with the county

  • California Well Standards (Bulletin 74-90)

  • State Water Resources Control Board regulations

When Is Decommissioning Required in Lakeside?

  • Switching to district water. When a Lakeside property connects to the Lakeside Water District or Padre Dam service, the now-redundant private well must be retired properly.
  • Selling the property. Title and lending requirements commonly call for proof that abandoned wells have been sealed under permit.
  • Replacing a failed well. Drilling a new well means the old one must be decommissioned.
  • Inactive wells. A well left unused without proper maintenance and sealing is legally abandoned.
  • Contamination and safety. Wells near the river, septic systems, or former ranch operations can channel pollutants into the aquifer, and open casings are a physical hazard.

The San Diego County Permit Process

Lakeside well destruction is regulated by the San Diego County Department of Environmental Health and Quality (DEHQ), Land and Water Quality Division. A destruction permit must be issued before sealing begins, and only a licensed C-57 contractor may do the work. We complete the application, supply the required destruction method and cross-section, retrieve the original completion report when available, and pay the fee. After approval we schedule the field work and the county inspection, then file the DWR destruction report to close out the permit.

The Physical Destruction Procedure

We follow DWR Bulletin 74-81 and 74-90 step by step:

  1. Verify the well. Sound the borehole to confirm depth, casing diameter and material, and water level.
  2. Clear it. Remove the pump, drop pipe, wiring, and any debris so the hole is open to the bottom.
  3. Disinfect. Chlorinate before sealing.
  4. Seal bottom-up. Pump neat cement, sand-cement, or bentonite grout through a tremie pipe from the bottom up to prevent voids and bridging.
  5. Finish the surface. Cut the casing below grade, set a concrete plug, and restore the ground.

Inspection, Documentation, and Timeline

The county may witness the work or inspect afterward. A typical residential Lakeside well is sealed in one day; from permit application to closed file, plan on one to three weeks. We provide you copies of the destruction report for your records and disclosures.

What Happens If You Don't Decommission an Old Well

An abandoned well is not a passive hole in the ground. It is an open vertical pathway that connects the surface directly to the aquifer, bypassing the natural soil layers that normally filter contaminants. In Lakeside, leaving a well unsealed creates several distinct problems. Along the San Diego River corridor, a shallow water table makes Lakeside groundwater especially vulnerable to surface pollutants. Surface runoff, fertilizer, fuel, and bacteria can travel straight down a corroded or open casing and degrade the groundwater that neighboring wells still draw from. Old wells also collapse over time, and a hidden, partially filled shaft is a genuine physical hazard to children, pets, livestock, and equipment. From a financial standpoint, an undocumented well is a recorded liability that can surface during a title search and stop a sale cold. Decommissioning resolves all of these at once: it removes the contamination pathway, eliminates the safety hazard, and produces a recorded document proving the well was properly retired.

A Closer Look at the Sealing Materials

The heart of a proper decommissioning is the sealant, and San Diego County follows the California Department of Water Resources standards for what may be used. The acceptable materials are neat cement grout (cement and water, sometimes with a small bentonite addition), sand-cement grout, and bentonite grout. Each has a place: neat cement is the workhorse for sealing the casing itself, while bentonite is often used where a flexible, low-permeability seal is preferred. The material must be placed by tremie pipe from the bottom of the well upward so that the grout displaces water and fills every foot of the bore without trapping voids or bridging partway down. Simply dumping dry sacks or concrete from the top is not compliant and is the most common reason a do-it-yourself "abandonment" fails inspection. Where a well is gravel-packed or has perforated sections, the grout is placed under pressure so it penetrates the annular space and the surrounding formation, not just the inside of the casing.

The San Diego County Department of Environmental Health Permit, Step by Step

In San Diego County, the San Diego County Department of Environmental Health and Quality (DEHQ), Land and Water Quality Division administers the well program. If a well is to be destroyed, the applicant must provide a description and cross-section detailing the destruction method, which the county reviews before issuing the permit. We prepare that cross-section, pull any archived completion report for your Lakeside well from the county's document library, and submit everything on your behalf so the review goes smoothly.

Once the permit is in hand, the field sequence is straightforward but exacting. We mobilize our rig to the wellhead, sound the well one more time to confirm conditions match the permit, pull the pump and any down-hole equipment, disinfect, and then grout from the bottom up. A county inspector may be present to witness the seal or may inspect the completed surface. When the work is done, we complete the Department of Water Resources Well Completion Report documenting the destruction, including the materials used and the volumes placed, and we file it with the county and the state. You receive copies for your permanent records, your insurance, and any future buyer's due diligence.
