How a Pressure Tank Works

Think of your pressure tank as a spring made of air. Inside the steel shell, a rubber bladder holds water while a sealed pocket of compressed air presses against it. Your submersible pump fills the tank and compresses that air; when you open a tap, the air pushes the water back out through your plumbing without the pump running. The pump only restarts when pressure falls to the cut-in point — commonly 30 or 40 PSI — and shuts off again at cut-out, usually 50 or 60 PSI. The usable water delivered between those two points is the tank’s drawdown. A tank sized with enough drawdown means your pump runs in long, efficient cycles instead of rapid bursts.

The air side is set with a pre-charge about 2 PSI under cut-in. Lose that pre-charge — through a slow leak or a torn bladder — and the tank can no longer store energy. That is when the trouble starts.

Waterlogged Tanks and the Pass Climate

When the air pocket leaks away or the bladder ruptures, water floods the space that should hold air and the tank becomes waterlogged. A waterlogged tank holds almost no usable water, so the pump short-cycles — snapping on and off many times a minute. In Banning that is doubly costly: the Pass is hot and windy, pumps already work hard, and the extra starts drive up both wear and your Southern California Edison bill. We have replaced Banning-area pumps that failed years early solely because a dead tank hammered them.

To check yours, cut the pump breaker, open a faucet to bleed pressure, and press the Schrader valve on top of the tank. Air hissing out is normal. Water spraying out means the bladder is torn and the tank has to be replaced. You can also tap the shell — a solid ring all the way up instead of a hollow top confirms the air is gone.

Warning Signs in Banning Homes

  • The pump cycles on and off rapidly whenever water is running
  • Pressure swings from strong to weak within the same shower
  • Banging pipes (water hammer) when valves close
  • Spitting, sputtering faucets that cough air
  • Visible corrosion at the tank base or seams — common where mineral water sits
  • A noticeably higher electric bill with no change in usage

Sizing a Tank for San Gorgonio Pass Properties

Correct sizing keeps your pump alive. The goal is a run time of at least a minute per cycle, which means matching the tank’s drawdown to the pump’s gallons-per-minute. General guidance for Banning-area homes:

  • 1-2 bathrooms: a 20-32 gallon tank pairs well with 5-10 GPM pumps
  • 3-4 bathrooms: a 44-86 gallon tank supports simultaneous demand and 10-20 GPM pumps
  • Larger lots on the Banning Bench or toward Cabazon: 86-120 gallon tanks or dual tanks for irrigation loads

Local well data backs up careful sizing: DWR records show Banning with 112 wells on record and an average depth near 405 feet (ranging from about 15 to over 1,150 feet). Deeper wells mean stronger, pricier pumps — and every reason to protect them with the right tank.

Checking Your Air Pre-Charge (PSI)

Once a year, check the pre-charge. Shut off the pump, open the lowest faucet until water stops, and gauge the Schrader valve with a tire gauge. It should read 2 PSI below cut-in — about 28 PSI on a 30/50 system or 38 PSI on a 40/60 system. Add air in short bursts if it is low. If it will not hold, the tank is failing. In the Pass, where mineral scale is common, we also recommend flushing sediment from the tank periodically so grit does not chew up the bladder and switch.

Diagnosis and When to Call a Professional

Short-cycling has more than one cause. Before condemning a tank, we verify the pressure switch, test cut-in and cut-out, read the pre-charge, inspect the bladder, and confirm the pump still makes rated flow — a tired pump can mimic a bad tank. Homeowners can safely test the pre-charge and tap the shell. Call a licensed C-57 contractor when water comes out of the air valve, when pressure never builds, when the switch contacts arc or smell hot, or when rust is weeping from the tank. Repair or Replace? What Makes Sense in Banning

Not every pressure problem means a new tank. A worn pressure switch that chatters or fails to cut out is a $150-350 repair, and it is often the real culprit behind pressure that will not build. A leaking Schrader valve core can sometimes be replaced for the cost of a service call. But once the internal bladder has torn, the tank itself is done — there is no patch, and adding air only buys a few days before it floods again. As a rule of thumb, if a bladder tank is past ten years and the bladder has failed, replacement is the smart money, because a fresh tank protects the far more expensive submersible pump below it. When we quote a Banning job, we tell you plainly which parts can be repaired and which need replacing, and we never upsell a tank you do not need.

We also see homeowners in the Pass try to nurse along an old galvanized tank — the pre-bladder style with no internal barrier. Those tanks lose their air charge constantly because the air dissolves directly into the water, and they are notorious for waterlogging. If you still have one, switching to a modern bladder tank almost always ends the short-cycling for good.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my well pump short-cycle in the summer?

Rapid on-off cycling is nearly always a pressure-tank problem — the air charge has leaked out or the bladder has torn, leaving almost no drawdown. Summer heat in the Pass just makes the extra pump starts more punishing. A quick tank and switch check will pin it down.

What is the right air pre-charge for my tank?

Set it 2 PSI below your pump’s cut-in: about 28 PSI on a 30/50 switch or 38 PSI on a 40/60. Check it with the pump off and the tank drained. If it will not hold the charge, the tank needs service.

How can I tell if my pressure tank is waterlogged?

Tap the shell — the top should sound hollow. If it rings solid all the way up, or if water sprays from the air valve when you press it, the air cushion is gone and the tank should be replaced.

How long should a pressure tank last in Banning?

Ten to fifteen years is typical for a quality bladder tank. The Pass’s mineral-rich water can shorten that, so an annual pre-charge check and occasional sediment flush pay off.

Will a bigger tank lower my electric bill?

Often yes. A properly sized tank lets the pump run longer, gentler cycles instead of constant restarts, which cuts the inrush current that drives up both wear and your bill.
