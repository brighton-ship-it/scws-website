Well Running Dry? Signs, Causes & What You Can Do About It

Published February 1, 2026 | Well Problems | 14 min read

Few things are more alarming for a homeowner than realizing their well is running dry. Whether you've noticed sputtering faucets, muddy water, or a complete loss of water after heavy use, a well losing water is a serious problem that requires immediate attention. The good news? In most cases, there are solutions—from simple pump adjustments to well deepening or hydrofracturing. This comprehensive guide will help you understand what's happening, what to do right now, and how to restore your water supply.

Warning Signs Your Well Is Running Dry

A well doesn't usually go dry overnight. In most cases, there are warning signs days, weeks, or even months before complete water loss. Recognizing these signs early gives you more options and time to plan. Here are the key indicators that your well water level is dropping:

1. Sputtering Faucets and Air Bursts

One of the earliest and most obvious signs of a well going dry is air coming through your faucets. When the water level drops near the pump intake, the pump starts drawing a mixture of air and water. You'll notice:

  • Faucets spitting and sputtering before water flows
  • Irregular water flow that surges and stops
  • Air "burps" from the faucet, sometimes spraying water
  • Loud banging in pipes when water first comes on

If this happens consistently—not just occasionally—it's a strong indication your water level is critically low. This is different from air in the lines after plumbing work, which resolves quickly.

2. Muddy, Sandy, or Discolored Water

When a well's water level drops, the pump may begin drawing water from closer to the bottom of the well, where sediment accumulates. Signs include:

  • Water that appears brown, tan, or cloudy
  • Fine sand or grit in the water
  • Sediment collecting in toilet tanks or water heaters
  • Filters clogging more frequently than usual

This sediment can also damage your pump and plumbing if the condition persists. If you notice sudden changes in water quality, have your well inspected promptly.

3. Decreased Water Pressure

A gradual decline in water pressure often signals a dropping water level. You may notice:

  • Showers feel weaker than they used to
  • Multiple fixtures running reduces pressure dramatically
  • Pressure drops during irrigation or heavy water use
  • The pressure tank takes longer to refill

While pressure problems can have other causes (like a failing pressure tank or pump issues), low water level should always be considered, especially during drought conditions.

4. Water Runs Out After Heavy Use

This is a telltale sign of a low well water level. You might notice:

  • Running out of water after running irrigation
  • No water after filling a swimming pool or hot tub
  • Multiple showers or loads of laundry depleting supply
  • Water returns after letting the well "rest" for several hours

If your well recovers after resting, the good news is water is still entering the well—just not as fast as you're using it. This situation often worsens over time if not addressed.

5. Pump Running Longer or More Frequently

Pay attention to how long and how often your well pump runs:

  • Pump runs for extended periods to fill the pressure tank
  • Pump cycles on and off more frequently than before
  • You hear the pump running even when water use is minimal
  • Higher electricity bills from increased pump operation

A pump working harder to produce the same amount of water is often compensating for lower water levels.

6. Neighbors Experiencing Similar Problems

If neighbors with wells are also reporting low water or dry wells, it suggests a regional water table decline rather than a problem specific to your well. This is common during:

  • Extended drought periods
  • Increased development drawing from shared aquifers
  • Agricultural irrigation seasons

Why Wells Run Dry: Understanding the Causes

Understanding why your well is losing water helps determine the best solution. Here are the most common causes of wells going dry in Southern California:

Drought and Reduced Rainfall

Southern California's Mediterranean climate means most rain falls between November and March. During drought years—which have become increasingly common—aquifer recharge is significantly reduced. The water table drops as pumping continues without adequate replenishment.

Impact: During California's 2012-2016 drought, thousands of residential wells went dry as water tables dropped 50-100+ feet in some areas. Many wells in San Diego, Riverside, and Orange counties required deepening or replacement.

Learn more about protecting your well during drought conditions.

Aquifer Depletion

An aquifer is the underground geological formation that holds and transmits groundwater to your well. Aquifer depletion occurs when water is withdrawn faster than it's naturally replenished. Causes include:

  • Increased residential development: More homes with wells means more demand on the same water source
  • Agricultural pumping: Large-scale irrigation can significantly lower regional water tables
  • Municipal well fields: Cities pumping groundwater affects private wells in the vicinity
  • Climate change: Reduced snowpack and altered precipitation patterns affect long-term recharge

In some areas of Southern California, aquifer levels have been declining for decades. If you're in an area of known groundwater decline, planning ahead is essential.

Increased Household Demand

Sometimes the problem isn't the aquifer—it's increased demand from your own household. Consider whether any of these apply:

  • New irrigation system or expanded landscaping
  • Addition of a pool, spa, or water feature
  • More people living in the home
  • New water-intensive appliances
  • A leak you haven't noticed (check your meter if you have one)

Your well has a specific yield—the rate at which it can sustainably produce water. Exceeding that yield causes temporary shortages even if the aquifer is healthy.

Seasonal Fluctuations

Water tables naturally rise and fall with the seasons. In Southern California, levels are typically:

  • Highest: Late winter/early spring (after rainy season)
  • Lowest: Late summer/early fall (after dry season and peak irrigation)

A well that runs marginally during dry months may be fine the rest of the year. However, if seasonal low points are getting worse each year, that's a concerning trend.

Well Construction Issues

Sometimes the problem isn't water supply but well construction:

  • Shallow well: Well wasn't drilled deep enough for current conditions
  • Clogged screen: Mineral buildup or sediment reducing water entry
  • Collapsed casing: Structural failure blocking water flow
  • Pump set too high: Pump above the current water level

A professional well inspection can identify these issues.

What to Do Immediately When Your Well Runs Dry

If you find yourself with no water or severely reduced flow, take these steps right away:

⚠️ Critical: Stop Using Water Immediately

Running a pump dry can cause severe and expensive damage. The pump motor relies on water for cooling—without water, it can overheat and burn out within minutes. If you suspect your well is dry, turn off the pump at the breaker until a professional can assess the situation.

Step 1: Turn Off the Pump

Locate your well pump breaker in the electrical panel and switch it off. This prevents pump damage and potential fire hazards from an overheating motor. Don't turn it back on until you've either confirmed water has returned or had the system professionally checked.

Step 2: Let the Well Rest

If your well ran dry due to heavy usage (irrigation, filling a pool, etc.), the water level may recover after resting. Wait 4-8 hours minimum—overnight is better. This allows groundwater to flow back into the well.

Step 3: Arrange Emergency Water

For immediate needs:

  • Drinking water: Purchase bottled water or fill containers at a neighbor's
  • Bulk water delivery: Many companies deliver water to fill tanks (search "water delivery" in your area)
  • Storage tanks: Consider installing a holding tank that can be refilled
  • Reduce usage: Conserve what you have—no irrigation, minimal flushing, quick showers

Step 4: Check If Water Returns

After resting, turn the pump back on and test carefully. If water returns:

  • Use water sparingly—don't immediately run irrigation
  • Monitor for sputtering or sediment
  • Schedule a professional assessment soon

If water doesn't return or the problem recurs, it's time for professional intervention.

Step 5: Call a Well Professional

A qualified well contractor can:

  • Measure your static water level accurately
  • Assess your well's condition and production capacity
  • Recommend appropriate solutions based on your specific situation
  • Provide cost estimates for different options

Solutions for a Well Running Dry

Depending on your situation, several solutions exist—from relatively simple and affordable to more extensive. Here are your options, from least to most invasive:

1. Lower the Pump

Best for: Modest water table declines where adequate water still exists below the current pump depth.

If the water table has dropped but there's still water in the well, simply lowering the pump to a deeper position may restore production. This works when:

  • The well is deep enough to accommodate a lower pump position
  • Water level has dropped 20-50 feet from original levels
  • Existing pump and pipes are in good condition

Cost: $1,000-$2,500 depending on well depth and access.

Pros: Least expensive option, quick turnaround, uses existing infrastructure.

Cons: Only works if there's water deeper in the existing well; may be a temporary fix if water levels continue dropping.

2. Hydrofracturing (Hydrofracking)

Best for: Wells in rock formations where water-bearing fractures have become clogged or where additional fractures could increase yield.

Hydrofracturing involves pumping water into the well under high pressure (typically 500-2,000 PSI) to clean existing fractures and open new pathways for water to enter the well. This technique can dramatically increase well yield in the right conditions.

Cost: $2,000-$5,000.

Success rate: About 70-80% of wells show improvement, with many doubling or tripling production. Results are typically permanent.

Pros: Can significantly increase production without drilling; works on many types of wells; relatively affordable.

Cons: Doesn't work in all geological formations; results can vary; not effective if aquifer itself is depleted.

3. Well Deepening

Best for: Wells where the water table has dropped below the current well depth, but water exists at greater depths.

Well deepening extends your existing well further into the ground to reach the current water table. The existing casing may be extended, and a new pump suitable for the greater depth is installed.

Cost: $3,000-$15,000+ depending on how much deeper (typically $30-$75 per foot of additional depth, plus new pump if needed).

Pros: Uses existing well location; less expensive than a new well; effective when water exists at greater depth.

Cons: Limited by existing well diameter and construction; may not be possible for very old wells; doesn't help if aquifer is depleted at all depths.

Learn more about proper well depths for San Diego County.

4. Drilling a New Well

Best for: Situations where the existing well cannot be salvaged, a different location may have better water, or significantly deeper drilling is needed.

Sometimes the best solution is a completely new well. This allows for:

  • Optimal placement based on current geological knowledge
  • Drilling to whatever depth is needed for your aquifer
  • Modern construction techniques and materials
  • Proper sizing for current household needs

Cost: $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth, geology, and access. See our guide to well drilling costs in San Diego and Riverside County.

Pros: Long-term solution; can be designed for current conditions; modern equipment and warranties.

Cons: Most expensive option; requires permits; longer timeline; may still face same aquifer issues if regional depletion.

5. Storage and Conservation Systems

Best for: Wells with limited yield but adequate recovery time; supplementing any of the above solutions.

Sometimes the solution isn't getting more water faster—it's storing what you get:

  • Storage tanks: Large tanks (1,000-5,000+ gallons) store water pumped slowly over time
  • Pump timers: Run the pump during optimal hours when water table is highest
  • Recovery systems: Automatically manage pumping to match well recovery rate
  • Rainwater harvesting: Supplement well water with captured rain (for non-potable uses)

Cost: $2,000-$10,000 for storage systems.

Pros: Works with existing well; provides reserve capacity; reduces stress on aquifer.

Cons: Requires space for tanks; may limit water availability during heavy use; doesn't increase actual water supply.

Preventing Your Well From Running Dry

Prevention is always better than emergency repairs. Here's how to protect your water supply:

🛡️ Well Protection Checklist

  • ✓ Monitor water levels: Have static water levels measured annually to track trends.
  • ✓ Conserve water: Fix leaks promptly, use efficient fixtures, and don't over-irrigate.
  • ✓ Spread out water use: Avoid running irrigation while doing laundry or during showers.
  • ✓ Use drought-tolerant landscaping: Reduce irrigation demand with native and adapted plants.
  • ✓ Schedule annual inspections: Catch problems early before they become emergencies.
  • ✓ Know your yield: Understand how much water your well can sustainably produce.
  • ✓ Consider storage: A backup tank provides cushion during high-demand periods.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well is running dry?

Common signs your well is running dry include: sputtering faucets with air bursts, muddy or sandy water, decreased water pressure especially during peak usage, water that runs out after heavy use but returns after resting, and the pump running longer than usual to fill the pressure tank. If you notice multiple signs, have your well's static water level professionally measured.

Can a dry well recover on its own?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. If your well ran dry due to temporary overuse or a short drought, it may recover after resting for several hours or after seasonal rains recharge the aquifer. However, if the water table has permanently dropped due to long-term drought, aquifer depletion, or increased regional pumping, the well may not recover without intervention like deepening or hydrofracturing.

How much does it cost to fix a dry well?

Costs vary significantly based on the solution: lowering the pump costs $1,000-$2,500, well deepening ranges from $3,000-$15,000 depending on depth and geology, hydrofracturing costs $2,000-$5,000, and drilling a new well typically runs $15,000-$50,000 or more. A professional assessment can determine the most cost-effective solution for your situation.

What should I do immediately if my well runs dry?

First, stop using water completely to prevent pump damage from running dry. Turn off the pump at the breaker if possible. Arrange for emergency water delivery or fill containers from another source. Let the well rest for at least 4-8 hours, then check if water returns. If it does, use water sparingly and call a professional to assess the situation. Running a pump dry can cause expensive damage.

Is my well going dry permanently or temporarily?

A professional water level test can determine this. If your static water level (the level when the pump hasn't run) has dropped significantly below historical levels, it may indicate permanent decline. Factors suggesting temporary issues include: recent heavy usage, current drought conditions, and seasonal patterns. Factors suggesting permanent decline include: consistent drop over multiple years, regional water table decline, and nearby wells also experiencing problems.
