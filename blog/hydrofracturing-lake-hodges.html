What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Lake Hodges Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Understanding Your Well's Water Source

If you live in the hills above Lake Hodges, your well does not draw from a sandy aquifer. It draws from groundwater that has migrated into fractures within the granite. Those fractures are the entire highway system for your water and like any system, they get congested. Mineral scale, iron and manganese, and fine rock sediment slowly coat and plug the seams closest to your borehole. Add a few dry years that lower the regional water table, and a well that once kept up easily can start running short, especially during summer.

How Hydrofracturing Restores Flow

The technique is straightforward and proven. We place inflatable packers in the well to isolate a section, then inject clean water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000-plus PSI. The pressure breaks loose the buildup clogging the fractures and opens them slightly wider, reconnecting your well to seams that had stopped contributing. We reposition the packers and treat additional zones as needed. Most jobs near Lake Hodges take four to eight hours on site, and the only thing we put down the well is clean water.

It is worth repeating, because the word causes confusion: this is not oil-and-gas fracking. There are no chemicals and no sand. We are cleaning and reopening the natural fractures that already carry your water.

Will It Work on a Lake Hodges Well? An Honest Answer

Hydrofracturing performs best in fractured crystalline rock and decomposed granite, which is precisely what underlies the Del Dios hills and the slopes around the reservoir. That is good news for most well owners here. The technique does not work in loose sand, gravel, or river alluvium because those materials have no fractures to open and the injected pressure simply bleeds away. A small number of parcels closer to the valley bottom may sit on looser material, so we always confirm with a downhole camera before recommending treatment. If your well is a poor candidate, we will say so.

  • Your well near Del Dios produces less water than it did in past years
  • A well drilled into the granite hills came in lower than expected
  • The well runs low during the dry season around the lake
  • The pump cycles frequently or recovery is slow after pumping

What to Watch For and What Happens Next

Track when your well struggles. Year-round decline usually signals clogged fractures, while sharp late-summer drop-offs point to a falling water table that clean, open fractures can help you weather. Watch for rusty water and listen for a short-cycling pump. When you call us, the process runs like this:

  1. Baseline flow test to capture your current yield
  2. Downhole camera survey to confirm rock, inspect casing, and find fracture zones
  3. Hydrofracturing targeted high-pressure injection at isolated depths

Across the county's granite, suitable wells improve in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, with typical gains of two to five times the starting flow. We document results rather than make promises.

Rehabilitation Beyond the Fractures Near Lake Hodges

Not every weak well in the Del Dios hills is a fracture problem, and a responsible contractor confirms the cause before recommending a treatment. When we assess a well near Lake Hodges, we examine the complete system so we can tell whether the trouble is in the formation or in the pump and tank above it.

Several common failures imitate a low-yield well. A worn pressure switch (about $150 to $350 to replace) can cause erratic pressure. A waterlogged or failed pressure tank ($600 to $1,500) can make the pump cycle constantly. An aging submersible pump ($2,500 to $5,500) can deliver weak, inconsistent flow even when the well itself is fine. We test for these issues first, so that if we do recommend hydrofracturing, you know the fractures are truly the bottleneck.

Water quality around the reservoir is also worth attention. The granite here produces hard, mineral-rich water, and the same iron, manganese, and scale that stain fixtures are often the very deposits clogging your well's fractures. Sediment filtration generally runs $300 to $900, and a water softener runs $1,500 to $3,500. For some Lake Hodges and Del Dios homes, the best result pairs a hydrofracturing treatment with filtration or softening so that the restored flow also arrives clean. We will walk you through every option and recommend only what your situation calls for, never more.

If your home sits on a steep Del Dios lot where pressure already struggles to reach the upper floors, a constant-pressure or booster system ($2,000 to $4,500) can make a noticeable difference once the well's yield is restored. We size every recommendation to your terrain and your household's real demand.

Does the process use any chemicals?

How long does a treatment take?

How much can my well's output improve?

Is this the same as the fracking used for oil and gas?
