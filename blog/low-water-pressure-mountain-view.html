How a Well Pressure System Works

Four components keep your water flowing. The pump draws groundwater from the aquifer. A pressure tank stores it under a compressed-air cushion so the pump does not run on every tap. The pressure switch starts the pump at cut-in and stops it at cut-out (commonly a 30/50 range here), and the water delivered between those points is the drawdown. A healthy drawdown and a correctly sized pump keep pressure steady across Mountain View's larger lots, from the house to the garden or outbuildings.

Mountain View Geology and Its Pressure Challenges

The Mountain View area sits in the Upper Santa Ana Valley, where deep alluvial deposits of sand, gravel, and silt, washed down from the San Bernardino Mountains, form the aquifer. Wells here tap that alluvial groundwater, part of the larger Santa Ana River watershed that serves heavy urban and agricultural demand. Several factors shape low pressure in this setting. The alluvial nature of the basin means wells often encounter fine sediment that infiltrates screens and pipes, restricting flow. Proximity to the San Andreas and related faults can shift underground water pathways over time, and the valley's semi-arid climate means droughts stress wells further. These local conditions call for solutions matched to the valley's specific geology rather than a generic fix.

Common Causes of Low Pressure in the Mountain View Area

  • Sediment from alluvial deposits: Fine sand and silt build up in pumps, pipes, and screens, restricting flow and lowering pressure.
  • Pressure tank malfunctions: Mineral-rich water corrodes or clogs tanks, leading to waterlogging or inconsistent delivery.
  • Clogged pipes and screens: Sediment and mineral scaling create partial blockages that drop pressure at fixtures.
  • Low well yield: Droughts and regional demand can deplete the aquifer, reducing available volume and pressure.
  • Mechanical or electrical failures: Power fluctuations near the urban grid and worn check valves or impellers further diminish pressure.

What to Check Before You Call

A few checks help. Compare fixtures: weak flow everywhere points to pump or tank; one weak tap points to a clog. Watch the pump for short-cycling (tank or switch) versus constant running without pressure (mechanical failure or low yield). Inspect the water for sediment or discoloration, common with alluvial geology. Check the tank air charge with a tire gauge (pump off, tank drained), about 2 PSI below cut-in (near 28 PSI for a 30/50 switch). And note the season, since a dry-month drop usually means yield. These observations speed our diagnosis.

Preventive Maintenance for Mountain View Wells

Preventing low pressure saves time and money. Schedule a yearly inspection to catch pump or tank issues early. Monitor water quality annually for sediment and hardness, adding a softener if minerals are high. Check the tank air charge monthly with a tire gauge, keeping it about 2 PSI below cut-in. Conserve during droughts by fixing leaks and using efficient fixtures to protect aquifer levels. And clean inline filters every few months to prevent clogs from local sediment.
