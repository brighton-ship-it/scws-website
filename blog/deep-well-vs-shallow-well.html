Deep Well vs Shallow Well: Which Is Better for Your Property?

Understanding Well Depths

Shallow wells are typically less than 50 feet deep and access water from the upper water table. They can be dug, driven, or drilled. Deep wells extend 100 to 400+ feet and are always drilled to reach deeper aquifers in bedrock. In Southern California's varied terrain, well depths vary dramatically—coastal areas may only need 100 feet while mountain and desert regions often require 300+ feet.

Water Quality Comparison

Deep wells generally provide better water quality because the water has been filtered through more rock and soil layers. They're also better protected from surface contamination like pesticides, fertilizers, and septic system leakage. Shallow wells are more vulnerable to contamination and drought conditions but may have fewer minerals and less water hardness.

Cost and Installation Differences

Shallow wells cost significantly less—typically $3,000-$8,000 complete. Deep wells run $10,000-$30,000+ depending on depth and geology. Drilling costs $25-$65 per foot, plus pump equipment and casing. However, deep wells often provide better long-term value with consistent supply and superior water quality.

Making the Right Choice

The best choice depends on your local geology, water table depth, and property use. Shallow wells work well where groundwater is plentiful and protected. Deep wells are essential in areas with deeper aquifers or contamination concerns. A hydrogeological assessment can identify the optimal depth for your property.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep should a residential well be?

Residential wells should be deep enough to reach a reliable aquifer and stay below seasonal water table fluctuations—typically 100-300 feet in Southern California. Local well drillers know the productive zones in your area.

Do deep wells have better water pressure?

Not automatically. Water pressure depends on your pump and pressure system, not well depth. However, deep wells often have better flow rates from accessing larger aquifers, which can support stronger pressure systems.

Can a shallow well be converted to a deep well?

Usually not. Deepening an existing well is technically difficult and often more expensive than drilling a new deep well. The original casing diameter, materials, and construction method typically limit deepening options.
