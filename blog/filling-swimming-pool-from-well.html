Filling a Swimming Pool from Your Well

You've got a new pool—or need to refill your existing one—and you're wondering if your private well can handle the job. Good news: most San Diego County wells can fill a swimming pool, but there's a right way and a wrong way to do it. Here's everything you need to know to fill your pool safely without damaging your well or pump.

Can Your Well Handle Filling a Pool?

Before you start running water, you need to understand your well's capacity. Not all wells are created equal, and a well that handles daily household use may struggle with the continuous demand of filling a pool.

Know Your Well's Yield

Well yield (measured in gallons per minute or GPM) tells you how much water your well can produce. This is different from your pump's capacity—your pump might move 15 GPM, but if your well only recovers at 5 GPM, you'll eventually run it dry.

Typical Well Yields in San Diego County

  • Excellent: 15+ GPM (can fill pool relatively quickly)
  • Good: 8-15 GPM (can fill with moderate breaks)
  • Adequate: 3-8 GPM (needs careful management)
  • Low: Under 3 GPM (may want to consider water delivery)

Not sure of your well's yield? Check your well driller's report, or we can perform a yield test. Many backcountry San Diego properties—especially in areas like Ramona, Julian, and Alpine—have yields that vary seasonally, so timing matters too.

Understanding Recovery Rate

Recovery rate is how quickly your well replenishes after water is drawn. Even a well with modest yield can fill a pool if you give it time to recover between pumping sessions.

⚠️ The Danger of Continuous Pumping

Running your pump continuously until the well runs dry is the fastest way to burn out your pump motor. When the pump runs dry, it loses cooling (water cools the motor) and can overheat and fail within minutes. A $3,000 pump replacement is not worth saving a few days on pool filling.

How Long Does It Take to Fill a Pool from a Well?

Let's do the math for a typical backyard pool:

Pool Size Gallons At 10 GPM (continuous) Safe Fill (4-6 hrs/day)
Small pool 10,000 gal ~17 hours 3-4 days
Average pool 15,000 gal ~25 hours 4-6 days
Large pool 20,000 gal ~33 hours 5-7 days
Very large pool 30,000 gal ~50 hours 8-10 days

The key takeaway: Plan on 4-7 days for a typical pool, pumping 4-6 hours per day with breaks. Yes, it's slower than water delivery, but it's much cheaper and won't damage your well.

Step-by-Step Guide to Filling Your Pool Safely

Step 1: Test Your Well Water First

Before pumping thousands of gallons into your pool, know what's in your water. San Diego County well water commonly contains:

⚠️ Iron

Causes rust-colored staining on pool surfaces and turns water brown when chlorine is added. Common in East County wells.

⚠️ Copper

Can cause green staining and turn blonde hair green. Found in some Valley Center and Fallbrook wells.

⚠️ Calcium Hardness

High hardness causes scale buildup on pool surfaces and equipment. Very common in SoCal groundwater.

⚠️ Tannins

Organic compounds that give water a tea color. Found in wells near oak groves or agricultural areas.

Get a basic water test before filling. Your pool professional will need this information anyway to balance the chemistry properly.

Step 2: Set Up Properly

Equipment Checklist

  • Garden hose: Use a heavy-duty hose (cheap hoses may kink or restrict flow)
  • Pre-filter (optional but recommended): A hose-end sediment filter removes particles
  • Timer: Set a timer so you don't forget to take breaks
  • Pressure gauge: Monitor your well system pressure

Step 3: Establish a Safe Pumping Schedule

Here's a conservative schedule that protects your well:

✅ Recommended Schedule

  • Morning session: 6 AM - 10 AM (4 hours)
  • Break: 10 AM - 4 PM (6 hours for recovery)
  • Evening session: 4 PM - 6 PM (2 hours) - optional
  • Night: Let well fully recover

Adjust based on your well's performance. If you notice pressure dropping or the pump cycling frequently, take longer breaks.

Step 4: Monitor Throughout the Process

Watch for warning signs that your well is struggling:

  • Dropping pressure: If system pressure drops significantly, stop and let it recover
  • Pump cycling rapidly: On-off-on-off indicates low water in the well
  • Sputtering or air in water: You're pulling the water level down too far
  • Water temperature increase: Warmer water means you're getting close to the bottom
  • Sediment or discoloration: You may be pulling silt from the bottom

🚨 Stop Immediately If:

  • • Water stops flowing completely
  • • Pump makes unusual noises
  • • Significant sediment appears
  • • Pressure won't build back up after breaks

Step 5: Treat the Pool Water After Filling

Once filled, you'll likely need to address water chemistry issues:

Post-Fill Treatment Steps

  1. Test the water: Check pH, alkalinity, hardness, and metals
  2. Add metal sequestrant first: Before any chlorine (chlorine oxidizes metals and causes staining)
  3. Balance pH and alkalinity: Adjust to proper levels (pH 7.2-7.6)
  4. Add chlorine: Only after sequestrant has circulated
  5. Run filter continuously: For 24-48 hours to clear any cloudiness

Special Considerations for San Diego County

Seasonal Timing Matters

In San Diego's backcountry, well levels fluctuate seasonally:

  • Best time: Late winter/early spring (February-April) when groundwater is highest
  • Okay: Fall (October-November) after summer stress but before rainy season
  • Worst time: Late summer (August-September) when water tables are lowest

If you're filling a pool in August, be extra cautious about your well's capacity. Many wells in areas like Ramona, Santa Ysabel, and Warner Springs see significant yield reduction in late summer.

Iron in East County Wells

Wells in East San Diego County (Alpine, Descanso, Pine Valley, Julian) often have elevated iron levels. If your well water has visible iron or leaves orange stains:

Dealing with Iron

  • Use a pre-filter rated for iron removal during filling
  • Add sequestrant BEFORE any chlorine touches the water
  • Consider ascorbic acid (vitamin C) treatment if staining occurs
  • You may need to drain and refill if iron levels are very high

Hard Water Throughout the Region

Almost all San Diego County well water is hard (high calcium/magnesium). This isn't a health issue, but it affects pool maintenance:

  • Scale buildup on tile, surfaces, and equipment
  • Cloudy water that's hard to clear
  • May need calcium hardness reducer
  • Regular descaling of pool equipment

Regular Pool Top-Offs vs. Complete Fill

Once your pool is filled, ongoing top-offs from your well are much easier. Evaporation in San Diego's dry climate typically requires 1-2 inches per week in summer (about 400-800 gallons weekly for a typical pool).

✅ Easy Top-Off Strategy

Install a float valve and autofill system connected to your well water. This automatically maintains water level without impacting your household water use. Just ensure you have a backflow preventer installed (required by code and for good reason).

When to Call for Professional Help

Contact a well professional before filling your pool if:

  • You don't know your well's yield or depth
  • Your well has a history of running low
  • You've never put high demand on the well before
  • Your pump is older (10+ years) and you're concerned about stress
  • You want a yield test to know exactly what your well can handle

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fill my swimming pool from my well?

Yes, you can fill a swimming pool from your well, but you need to do it carefully. The main concerns are: your well's recovery rate (how fast it replenishes), pump capacity and duty cycle, water quality (minerals, metals, hardness), and potential to run your well dry. Most residential wells can fill a pool safely if you do it gradually over several days rather than continuously.

How long does it take to fill a pool from a well?

A typical residential well produces 5-15 gallons per minute. For a 15,000-gallon pool: at 10 GPM running continuously, it would take about 25 hours of pumping. However, you should NOT run continuously. A safe approach is 4-6 hours per day with breaks to let the well recover. This means 4-7 days to fill a pool safely. Rushing risks damaging your pump or running the well dry.

Will filling a pool damage my well pump?

It can if done improperly. The two main risks are: running the pump dry (if well water level drops too low) which burns out the motor, and overheating from continuous operation (pumps need rest periods). To protect your pump: monitor water pressure and flow, take breaks every 2-4 hours, listen for pump cycling or pressure drops, and never let the pump run if water stops flowing.

Do I need to treat well water before putting it in my pool?

Usually yes. Well water often contains minerals and metals that can cause pool problems: iron causes rust-colored staining and brown water when chlorine is added; copper can turn hair green and stain surfaces; calcium creates scale buildup; and tannins give water a tea-colored tint. Before filling, get your well water tested. After filling, use a metal sequestrant and balance chemistry carefully.

Is it cheaper to fill a pool from my well or have water delivered?

In most cases, well water is significantly cheaper. Water delivery in San Diego County typically costs $200-$400 per truckload (about 6,000 gallons), so a 15,000-gallon pool costs $500-$1,000+ delivered. Well water electricity costs about $50-$100 total. However, factor in: cost of any water treatment needed, risk to your well/pump, and your time. For wells with low yield or water quality issues, delivery might be worth the premium.
