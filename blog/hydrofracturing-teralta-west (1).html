What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Teralta West Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling in Teralta West

In Teralta West, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many Teralta West wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing in Teralta West: Honest Guidance for an Urban Neighborhood

Teralta West is a dense, walkable neighborhood within City Heights in the heart of urban San Diego, San Diego County, bordered by Interstate 15, El Cajon Boulevard, and University Avenue, near Fairmount Avenue and the Mid-City communities of Normal Heights and Kensington. It is one of San Diego's most established mid-city areas — apartments, bungalows, and small lots served entirely by the City of San Diego's municipal water system. That single fact shapes everything on this page: almost no property in Teralta West has a private water well, and hydrofracturing — a service for improving low-yield wells — is therefore a weak fit for the vast majority of addresses here. We would rather tell you that plainly than imply otherwise.

Why Hydrofracturing Rarely Applies in Teralta West

Hydrofracturing is a rehabilitation method for private wells drilled into hard, fractured bedrock. It uses high-pressure clean water to open and connect the natural cracks that feed a rural well. In an urban, fully sewered and water-mained neighborhood like Teralta West, residents receive treated water through the city distribution system. There is no private well to fracture, no aquifer being tapped on individual lots, and consequently no scenario in which hydrofracturing addresses a typical homeowner's water complaint. If your water pressure is low or your bill is high, the cause lies in plumbing or the municipal connection — not in a well.

What "Low Water" Usually Means on City Water

When a Teralta West household notices weak flow or pressure, the realistic causes are:

  • Old galvanized supply piping. Many mid-century San Diego homes still have corroded galvanized lines that gradually choke flow. Re-piping or replacing the service line restores pressure.
  • A failing pressure regulator. The pressure-reducing valve where city water enters the home can fail and drop household pressure; replacing it is a standard plumbing repair.
  • Clogged fixtures or water heater sediment. Aerators, valves, and heaters accumulate scale over time.
  • Shared or undersized service lines in older multi-unit buildings can limit flow at peak times.

None of these are well problems, and none are solved by hydrofracturing. A licensed plumber is the right call for the great majority of Teralta West water issues.

The Rare Exception: Older or Edge Properties

Occasionally a long-held parcel on the older fringes of a city neighborhood retains a legacy well — sometimes capped and forgotten, sometimes used for landscape irrigation. If you genuinely have a private well on your Teralta West property and it is producing poorly, then the underlying geology matters. Much of San Diego County's bedrock is granitic and crystalline, which is the kind of formation where hydrofracturing can help. In that uncommon case we would evaluate the well on its merits. But this is the exception, not the rule, for an inner-city neighborhood like Teralta West.

How Hydrofracturing Works (For the Rare Well That Has One)

If you do have a hard-rock well, the process is straightforward: we isolate a section of the cased borehole with inflatable packers and inject clean water at 1,500-3,000+ PSI. That pressure flushes and widens the existing fractures feeding the well and can extend them to intersect additional water-bearing cracks, often improving yield two to five times in good bedrock. No chemicals are used. The job runs about four to eight hours, and we measure flow before and after. Again, this applies only to genuine private wells — which are vanishingly rare in Teralta West.

A Note on City Heights History and Water Infrastructure

Teralta West and the wider City Heights area were largely built out in the early and mid twentieth century, and much of the original housing stock predates modern plumbing standards. That history is the real reason residents sometimes experience pressure and flow problems: aging service laterals, original galvanized branch lines, and decades-old pressure regulators are common in neighborhoods of this vintage. As San Diego has densified City Heights with apartments and infill, demand on some older shared service connections has grown as well. The upshot is that the single most valuable step a Teralta West homeowner can take is to have the home's actual plumbing and city connection inspected, rather than reaching for a rural well technique that does not match an urban, fully mained neighborhood. We mention this because we regularly field calls from city residents who have read about hydrofracturing online and assumed it might raise their household pressure; it cannot, and we would rather save you the trouble of a visit that would not help. Our team is happy to point you to the right kind of professional even when that professional is not us.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Confirm your water source. If you pay a City of San Diego water bill, you are on municipal water and do not have a producing well.
  • Test pressure at the hose bib. An inexpensive gauge tells you whether the issue is whole-house (regulator or service line) or fixture-specific.
  • Check the pressure regulator near your main shutoff; a failing one is a common, affordable fix.
  • Look for any capped pipe or old wellhead on older properties before assuming there is no well at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing improve my water pressure in Teralta West? No. If you are on City of San Diego water — as nearly everyone in Teralta West is — pressure issues come from plumbing, not a well, and hydrofracturing does not apply.

How do I know whether I even have a well? If you receive a municipal water bill, you are on city water. A private well would have a wellhead, pump, and pressure tank on your property and no metered city supply.

My pressure is low — what should I do? Have a plumber check your pressure-reducing valve, service line, and fixtures. Old galvanized piping is a frequent culprit in mid-city San Diego homes.

I found an old capped well on my lot — can it be used? Possibly. If it reaches San Diego County's granitic bedrock, hydrofracturing might help an underperforming well. We can evaluate it, though this is uncommon in urban neighborhoods.

Do you work in urban San Diego at all? Yes, anywhere in the county where there is a genuine private well, but the bulk of our hydrofracturing work is in the rural East County where wells are the norm.

Are chemicals involved in hydrofracturing? No. It uses only clean water under pressure and is unrelated to oil-and-gas fracking.
