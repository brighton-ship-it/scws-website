Understanding Coliform Testing

Coliform bacteria are a group of microorganisms found naturally in soil and digestive systems of warm-blooded animals. Testing reports two categories: total coliform (indicating potential contamination pathways) and E. coli (indicating fecal contamination). A positive total coliform result means your well may be vulnerable to contamination. A positive E. coli result is more serious and indicates definite fecal contamination requiring immediate action.

How Bacteria Enter Wells

Contamination occurs through compromised well components: cracked casings, improper seals, damaged well caps, or failing grout. Surface water carrying bacteria can enter through these openings. Nearby septic systems that are too close, improperly maintained, or failing can contaminate groundwater. Flooding events overwhelm wells with contaminated surface water. Improper well construction or old, deteriorated systems are most vulnerable.

Shock Chlorination Procedure

Shock chlorination kills existing bacteria by circulating strong chlorine solution through the entire well system. Calculate chlorine amount based on well depth and diameter—typically 1-3 gallons of household bleach. Pour into well, connect a hose to recirculate water back into the well, then run all household taps until you smell chlorine. Let sit 12-24 hours, then flush completely before retesting.

Long-Term Disinfection Options

If contamination recurs after shock chlorination, ongoing treatment is necessary. Continuous chlorination systems inject small amounts of chlorine to maintain residual protection. UV disinfection systems kill bacteria and viruses without chemicals—effective but require clear, sediment-free water. First, address the contamination source: repair casing cracks, replace well caps, improve surface drainage, and inspect nearby septic systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is coliform bacteria dangerous?

Total coliform bacteria aren't necessarily harmful themselves but indicate possible contamination pathways. E. coli, a type of coliform, specifically indicates fecal contamination and poses serious health risks including gastrointestinal illness.

How often should I test my well for coliform?

Test annually at minimum. Additional testing is recommended after flooding, well repairs, changes in water taste/odor/appearance, or if nearby septic systems have problems. Test both for total coliform and E. coli.

How long after shock chlorination can I drink the water?

Wait at least 24 hours after flushing all chlorine from the system, then have the water retested. Only resume drinking the water after test results confirm no coliform bacteria are present.
