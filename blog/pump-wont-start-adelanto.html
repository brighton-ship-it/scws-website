Well Pump Won't Start in Adelanto?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Adelanto homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

Well Pump Won't Start in Adelanto? Let's Diagnose It

Adelanto sits high in the Mojave Desert of San Bernardino County, part of the Victor Valley that spreads north and west of Victorville along the edge of the Mojave River basin. This is true High Desert country — wide open, hot in summer, and largely beyond the reach of municipal water mains on the outlying parcels. Many Adelanto homes and small ranches draw their water from private wells reaching deep into the Mojave River groundwater basin. So when the pump won't start, there's no backup tap, and the problem feels immediate. The good news, after more than 30 years of desert well work, is that the pump motor is usually fine. The fault almost always lives somewhere else in the system.

This guide explains how the system works, the failures we see most often on High Desert wells, what you can safely check yourself, and when to call a licensed C-57 contractor. Southern California Well Service serves the Victor Valley and all of San Bernardino County.

How Your Adelanto Well System Works

The most useful thing to understand is that the pump never runs on its own — it waits for a chain of components to give it the green light, and a break anywhere in that chain shows up the same way at the faucet. Diagnosis is the process of testing each link.

Power leaves your panel on a dedicated 240-volt breaker and runs to a pressure switch at the tank. The switch watches pressure and closes its contacts when pressure drops to the cut-in point, telling the pump to run, then opens at cut-out. In the deep wells common around Adelanto, the pump is a submersible set hundreds of feet down; three-wire models use an above-ground control box housing the start capacitor and relay, while two-wire models build that into the motor. The motor turns an impeller stack that lifts water up the drop pipe to a pressure tank, whose air charge stores pressurized water and keeps the pump from cycling on every tap.

The power supply

Start at the panel. A tripped double-pole well breaker is your first clue. Reset it once. If it holds, you may have only had a passing surge — not unusual on the High Desert grid. If it trips again at once, there's a real fault, and continued resets risk fire or motor damage.

The pressure switch

The switch by the tank is the part we replace most. Its contacts pit from arcing, and — a special concern in Adelanto — its sensing tube clogs with the fine sand and sediment that Mojave groundwater carries. A switch that can't close never wakes the pump.

The control box and capacitor

A pump that hums but won't turn usually has a weak start capacitor in its three-wire control box. Desert heat is brutal on capacitors, especially on a wellhead box exposed to the full Adelanto sun. Replacing it costs a fraction of a pump pull.

The pressure tank

When a tank loses its air charge it waterlogs, and the pump short-cycles — rapid on/off that wears out the switch and motor. A tank that's heavy and solid throughout has likely lost its bladder.

The motor and wiring

Deep Adelanto wells carry long drop cables, and blowing grit, temperature swings, and rodents take a toll on splices and wellhead terminations. A corroded splice can imitate a dead pump. We confirm true motor burnout with insulation-resistance and winding tests before recommending a pull.

The well and water level

Groundwater levels in parts of the Mojave basin have declined over the years, and in dry stretches the water level in an Adelanto well can fall. Low-water protection then idles the pump to keep it from running dry and cooking the motor — protective behavior that looks exactly like a no-start.

Common Scenarios in the High Desert

Adelanto's environment shapes the failures. Summer heat waves cook control-box capacitors, often during the first triple-digit stretch of the year. Blowing sand clogs sensing tubes and settles in tanks. Grid instability trips breakers and burns switch contacts. And on older desert wells that have run fifteen or twenty years, a waterlogged tank or a tired motor tends to show up. Reading the local pattern usually narrows the diagnosis before any panel is opened.

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Confirm the well breaker is on; reset once and stop if it trips again.
  2. Read the pressure gauge — zero with the pump idle means the system has drained.
  3. Open a faucet and listen at the switch for a click as pressure drops.
  4. Tap the pressure tank top to bottom for the hollow-then-solid sound of a healthy air charge.
  5. Inspect the wellhead wiring for sand-blasted, chewed, or scorched insulation — look only, never touch.

When to Call a Professional

Anything past resetting a breaker means working with 240 volts, and a deep High Desert pump is far too heavy to pull without proper rigging. Call us if the breaker keeps tripping, if you smell burning, if the pump hums but no water comes, or if your checks come up empty. We arrive with meters and the experience to test the breaker, switch, control box, and motor on site — and we never pull a pump until the readings prove it's the pump.

What Repairs Typically Cost

Most Adelanto no-start calls are affordable. A pressure switch runs about $150–$350; a pressure tank roughly $600–$1,500 installed; control-box capacitor or relay work is modest. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the High Desert climate make Adelanto pumps fail differently?

It can. Adelanto sits in the Mojave Desert of San Bernardino County, where summer heat is intense and the air is dry. Heat is hard on the start capacitors in above-ground control boxes, and blowing sand and grit work into wellhead connections. Wide day-night temperature swings also stress wiring and splices over time. None of this means your pump is doomed — it just shifts which parts tend to fail first.

How deep are wells in Adelanto and the Victor Valley?

Wells in the Adelanto area of the Victor Valley typically run anywhere from about 200 to 600 feet, drawing from the Mojave River groundwater basin. Properties nearer the river corridor often hit water shallower, while parcels out on the desert benches and mesas are drilled deeper. A deeper pump means a longer, heavier pull and more wire to inspect, which pushes a full replacement toward the upper end of the cost range.

My breaker won't stay on — should I keep resetting it?

No. Reset a tripped 240-volt well breaker only once. If it trips again right away, there's a fault drawing too much current — a grounded motor, a damaged cable, or a locked rotor. Repeated resets can start an electrical fire or finish off a motor that might have been saved. Shut it off and call a licensed contractor.

Could blowing sand or sediment be part of my pump problem?

Often, yes. The fine sand common in Mojave groundwater clogs the pressure switch's sensing tube, abrades pump components, and settles in pressure tanks. A clogged sensing tube alone can keep the pump from ever getting its run signal. We check for sediment on every visit and can recommend filtration if it's chronic.

Is a no-start pump an emergency in Adelanto?

With no city water on most well properties out here, losing your pump means losing all water — so we treat it as an emergency and offer same-day service. Acting fast also matters because some causes worsen quickly, like a humming locked motor or a repeatedly tripping breaker. Shut the breaker off after one reset and call us.
