When Your Alpine Well Needs Rehabilitation

Alpine sits in the granite foothills east of El Cajon in San Diego County, on the Peninsular Ranges batholith. This is hard-rock well country: based on hundreds of wells drilled locally, the average depth in Alpine runs around 500 feet, with many wells in the 300-to-550-foot range tapping water-bearing fractures in gneiss and decomposed granite. Static water levels often sit well over 100 feet below the surface, and typical yields run a modest 5 to 12 gallons per minute, which makes protecting every gallon of an existing well's capacity especially important.

Wells do not last forever without care, but they also rarely fail all at once. Far more often a well in the Alpine area loses ground slowly, over a span of years, as mineral scale, bacteria, and migrating sediment quietly close off the path water takes from the aquifer into your pump. Because the decline is gradual, many owners simply tolerate weaker pressure and longer pump run-times without realizing the well itself can usually be brought back to life. Well rehabilitation restores yield and water quality in an existing well, instead of spending tens of thousands of dollars to drill a replacement, and for most of the older wells we inspect around Alpine it is the right first step.

Signs Your Alpine Well Needs Attention

These warning signs usually show up well before a well stops producing entirely:

  • Declining yield: The well produces less water than it once did. The pump runs longer to refill the pressure tank, or the tank cycles more often during normal use.
  • Sand or grit in the water: Sediment in faucet aerators, grit in the tub, or cloudy water when the pump starts often points to a deteriorating screen or an over-pumped well pulling fines through the gravel pack.
  • Rising electric bills: A fouled screen forces the pump to work harder against added resistance, and the extra load shows up on your power bill long before the pump finally fails.
  • Odor, staining, or slime: Rotten-egg smells, reddish or black staining on fixtures, and slimy buildup point to iron, manganese, and the iron and sulfur bacteria common in San Diego County wells.
  • Short-cycling or air spitting: A pump that snaps on and off rapidly, or faucets that sputter air, often means water is being drawn down faster than a partially clogged well can refill.

What Causes Wells to Decline Near Alpine

Four problems account for the large majority of the declining wells we rehabilitate around Alpine:

Mineral scale and encrustation. Alpine's hard-rock groundwater is typically high in minerals, so scale plates onto screens and, critically, onto the water-bearing fractures that feed the well. Because fractured-granite wells depend on a limited number of productive fractures, even modest mineral buildup can sharply cut yield. As water passes through the screen and pressure drops, dissolved minerals precipitate out and cement themselves onto the screen slots and gravel pack. Over a decade or two that crust can seal off much of the open area the well needs to flow freely.

Iron and manganese bacteria (biofouling). Naturally occurring bacteria feed on dissolved iron and manganese and build thick, gelatinous masses across the screen. Biofouling is one of the most common and most under-diagnosed causes of lost capacity in our service area, and it usually travels alongside the rotten-egg odor and staining owners describe.

Sand and silt accumulation. Decomposed granite continuously sheds fine grit that settles into the bottom of the casing, and over years that accumulation can bury the lowest producing fractures in a deep Alpine well. A few feet of accumulated sand can bury the lowest and most productive screen sections completely.

Corrosion and casing failure. Older steel casings corrode from the inside out. Rust scale flakes into the water, and in advanced cases perforations or collapsed sections let sediment pour in. Catching corrosion early is critical, because it decides whether rehabilitation is worthwhile or whether the casing has reached the end of its service life.

Diagnosing the Problem: Camera and Capacity Testing

We never guess. Before recommending any treatment, we run two diagnostics that show exactly what is happening downhole in your Alpine well.

Downhole video inspection. A waterproof camera is lowered the full depth of the well, giving us a clear, recorded view of the casing, screen, encrustation, biofilm, sediment fill, and any corrosion or breaks. A camera survey runs about $300 to $600 and routinely pays for itself by steering us away from treatments that would not have fixed the real problem.

Specific-capacity testing. By measuring how far the water level drops for a given pumping rate, we calculate the well's specific capacity in gallons per minute per foot of drawdown. Comparing that figure to the original well completion report tells us precisely how much capacity has been lost and sets a measurable target for the work. Our diagnostic visit is $125, credited toward the rehabilitation if you proceed.

Rehabilitation Methods We Use in Alpine

The right combination depends on what the camera and capacity test reveal about your well's construction, age, and the specific problem. Most successful jobs use two or more of these in sequence:

Mechanical Brushing and Surging

A stiff wire brush sized to the casing physically scrubs scale and biofilm off the walls and screen. Surge blocks then move water in and out of the screen to break loose encrustation and pull fines out of the gravel pack, redeveloping the well.

Chemical Treatment and Acidizing

Where hard-water scale has cemented the screen, we introduce targeted acids and specialized cleaning compounds to dissolve calcium, iron, and manganese deposits. The chemicals are agitated, allowed to dwell, and then fully removed. This is frequently the most cost-effective single step and can restore 50 to 80 percent of lost yield in scale-dominated wells.

Chlorination and Disinfection for Biofouling

When iron and sulfur bacteria are the culprit, we shock the well with a strong chlorine solution, often paired with agitation and an acid pre-treatment that breaks up the bacterial slime so the disinfectant can reach the colony. This restores flow and clears the odor and staining biofouling causes.

Airlifting and Bailing Sediment

To clear accumulated sand and silt, we use air-lift pumping and bailing to bring debris to the surface, redeveloping the well and reopening buried lower screen sections.

Screen Jetting

High-pressure water jetting drives cleaning energy directly into each screen slot, dislodging stubborn encrustation that brushing alone cannot reach. Jetting paired with surging gives the most thorough redevelopment.

Rehabilitation vs. Drilling a New Well

A typical well rehabilitation in Alpine runs $1,500 to $5,000, with more involved jobs that include hydrofracturing reaching $3,000 to $8,000. Drilling and equipping a new well, by contrast, generally costs $18,000 to $42,000. Rehabilitation makes sense whenever the casing is structurally sound and the aquifer still holds water, which describes most of the aging wells we inspect around Alpine. We give every customer an honest assessment: if the camera shows a collapsed or badly corroded casing, or the static water level has dropped below a workable depth, we will tell you plainly that a new well is the better investment rather than sell you a rehabilitation that will not hold.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes Alpine wells to lose production?

Most local wells decline from mineral scale on the screen, iron and manganese bacteria biofouling, sand and silt accumulation in the casing, or corrosion of older steel casing. San Diego County's hard groundwater makes scale and biofouling especially common, and rehabilitation can usually restore flow without drilling a new well.

How much does well rehabilitation cost in Alpine?

Well rehabilitation typically runs $1,500 to $5,000 depending on the problem and well depth. Video inspection is $300 to $600, and if hydrofracturing is needed it ranges $3,000 to $8,000 - all far less than a new well at $18,000 to $42,000. Our $125 diagnostic fee is credited toward the work.

How do I know if my well needs rehabilitation or replacement?

A downhole video inspection and a specific-capacity test tell us whether the casing is sound and how much yield has been lost. If the casing is intact and the aquifer still has water, rehabilitation is almost always the better value. We only recommend a new well when the casing has failed or the water table has dropped below a workable depth.

How long does well rehabilitation take?

Most residential rehabilitations in the Alpine area take one to three days depending on the methods required. Chemical treatments need dwell time, and heavily fouled wells may need several rounds of surging, treatment, and redevelopment to reach full recovery.

How often should a Alpine well be rehabilitated?

Many wells benefit from rehabilitation every 10 to 20 years, but wells in iron-bacteria-prone or high-mineral areas may need attention sooner. Watching for declining yield, new odors, or staining and scheduling a periodic video inspection helps catch problems while they are still inexpensive to fix.

Will rehabilitation improve my water quality?

Yes. Removing bacterial slime, dissolving iron and manganese scale, and clearing accumulated sediment frequently improves clarity, reduces odor, and cuts staining, in addition to restoring flow and pressure.
