Well Casing Repair Services in San Diego County

Your well casing is the protective barrier between your drinking water and the surrounding soil and rock. When casing fails—through corrosion, cracks, or joint separation—surface contamination can enter your water supply, and your well's structural integrity becomes compromised. Southern California Well Service provides expert well casing repair throughout San Diego County, restoring wells that might otherwise require expensive replacement.

Casing damage doesn't always mean your well is beyond saving. Depending on the type, location, and extent of damage, various repair techniques can extend your well's service life by decades. Our technicians evaluate each situation to recommend the most cost-effective solution, whether that's targeted repairs, liner installation, or when necessary, well replacement.

Understanding Well Casing

Well casing is the pipe—typically steel or PVC—that lines your well bore from the surface down through unstable geological formations. It serves critical functions:

  • Structural Support: Prevents the well bore from collapsing in loose soil or fractured rock.
  • Contamination Prevention: Creates a barrier against surface water and shallow groundwater intrusion.
  • Aquifer Isolation: Separates water-bearing zones from contaminated or lower-quality zones.
  • Equipment Protection: Provides a smooth path for pump installation and service.

In San Diego County, most wells use steel casing through the upper formations, with some newer wells using schedule 40 or 80 PVC. Understanding your casing material and construction helps determine appropriate repair strategies.

Signs of Casing Damage

Casing failure often announces itself through changes in water quality or quantity. Recognizing these warning signs early allows repairs before contamination becomes severe or structural failure occurs:

  • Sudden Water Quality Changes: Discoloration, sediment, or unusual taste may indicate surface water entry.
  • Bacterial Contamination: Positive coliform tests after previously clean results suggest a new contamination pathway.
  • Sand or Sediment: Material appearing in water that wasn't present before often indicates casing breach.
  • Water Level Changes: Unusual fluctuations may result from connections to different water zones.
  • Correlation with Rainfall: Water quality declining after rain suggests surface water entry.
  • Visible Corrosion: Rust or deterioration visible at the wellhead indicates potential problems below.

Common Types of Casing Damage

Corrosion

Steel casing corrodes over time, especially in aggressive water conditions or corrosive soils. Corrosion typically appears as pitting, thinning, or complete perforation of the casing wall. San Diego County's mineral-rich groundwater and variable soil chemistry accelerate corrosion in some areas.

Joint Failure

Well casing is assembled from individual sections joined by welding, threading, or couplings. These joints are vulnerable to separation due to ground movement, corrosion at the joint, or original construction defects. Failed joints allow direct communication between zones that should be isolated.

Physical Damage

Ground settling, earthquake activity, or root intrusion can crack, crush, or deform casing. Vehicle traffic over shallow wellheads occasionally causes mechanical damage. Physical damage often creates multiple failure points requiring comprehensive repair.

Screen Deterioration

Well screens—the perforated sections that allow water to enter—can corrode, clog with minerals, or sustain physical damage. While technically part of the casing system, screen problems often require different repair approaches than solid casing failures.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Before recommending repairs, we thoroughly evaluate casing condition using multiple diagnostic tools:

  • Video Inspection: Downhole cameras reveal visual evidence of corrosion, holes, cracks, and joint problems.
  • Water Quality Analysis: Changes in water chemistry indicate contamination pathways and severity.
  • Well Record Review: Original construction details guide repair planning.
  • Packer Testing: Isolating sections of the well identifies the exact location of leaks.
  • Physical Inspection: Surface components often reveal problems extending downhole.

Casing Repair Options

Liner Installation

The most common repair for corroded or damaged steel casing is installing a liner—a new, smaller-diameter pipe inside the existing casing. Modern liners are typically PVC or fiberglass, resistant to the corrosion that damaged the original steel.

The liner is lowered into the well and sealed at top and bottom, creating a new barrier against contamination. While the liner reduces well diameter slightly, this rarely affects water production significantly and extends well life substantially.

Grouting and Sealing

For specific, localized damage, cement or specialty grouts can seal individual holes or joint failures. This approach works best for small breaches in otherwise sound casing and is often used in conjunction with other repairs.

Casing Patch

Mechanical or welded patches can repair isolated damage in accessible areas. This approach is limited to situations where the surrounding casing remains structurally sound and the damage is localized.

Partial Recasing

When damage is confined to the upper portion of the well, it may be possible to remove and replace only the affected section while preserving the deeper casing. This approach can be more economical than full liner installation for shallow damage.

The Liner Installation Process

Liner installation is a precise operation requiring specialized equipment and expertise:

  • Pump Removal: The existing pump and drop pipe are removed to access the casing.
  • Well Cleaning: Debris, scale, and obstructions are removed to ensure the liner seats properly.
  • Liner Sizing: The liner diameter is selected to fit inside the existing casing while maintaining adequate water production.
  • Installation: The liner is assembled and lowered into position, typically extending from surface to below all damaged areas.
  • Sealing: The annular space between liner and old casing is sealed, and the liner is secured at surface.
  • Pump Reinstallation: A properly sized pump is installed in the newly lined well.
  • Disinfection: The completed well is chlorinated before returning to service.

When Repair Isn't Feasible

Not every damaged well can be economically repaired. We provide honest assessments when repair costs approach or exceed replacement costs, or when damage is too severe for reliable repair:

  • Extensive Corrosion: When most of the casing is severely deteriorated.
  • Multiple Failure Points: Numerous breaches throughout the casing depth.
  • Structural Collapse: Casing that has crushed or deformed beyond lining.
  • Inadequate Diameter: Wells too narrow to accept a liner while maintaining adequate production.
  • Poor Original Construction: Wells with fundamental design flaws that repairs can't address.

Preventing Future Damage

Once repairs are complete, several measures help prevent recurrence:

  • Proper Sealing: Ensure surface seals prevent water entry around the casing.
  • Drainage: Grade the area around the wellhead to direct surface water away.
  • Protection: Install protective covers and maintain clear access.
  • Monitoring: Regular water testing catches new contamination early.
  • Cathodic Protection: For steel casing, sacrificial anodes can slow corrosion.

Cost Considerations

Casing repair costs vary widely depending on well depth, damage extent, and repair method. Liner installation typically costs significantly less than drilling a new well—often 30-50% of replacement cost—while adding 20-30 years of service life.

We provide detailed estimates before beginning work, explaining the repair scope, expected longevity, and any limitations. Our goal is helping you make an informed decision based on accurate cost comparisons.
