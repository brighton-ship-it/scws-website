What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Poway Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Poway, CA

Poway has earned its nickname, "The City in the Country," by holding onto its semi-rural character even as the rest of San Diego County has filled in around it. Tucked between Rancho Bernardo, Scripps Ranch, Escondido, and the back-country gateway of Ramona, Poway is a place of horse properties, oak-shaded lots, and homes that sit well off the municipal water grid. For many of those properties, a private water well is not a luxury or a backup—it is the entire household supply, irrigating pastures, filling troughs, and keeping landscaping alive through a long, dry Southern California summer. When that well starts to fall behind, the problem is impossible to ignore.

What makes Poway both a challenge and an opportunity for well owners is the ground beneath it. The area sits on granitic bedrock—specifically the Woodson Mountain granodiorite that forms the dramatic boulders of Mt. Woodson—along with a thick blanket of decomposed granite. Water in this kind of country does not sit in a forgiving layer of sand and gravel. Instead, it travels through cracks and fractures in hard crystalline rock. That is exactly the geology where hydrofracturing tends to shine, because the technique works by opening and connecting those existing fractures so more groundwater can reach your well.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing—often shortened to hydrofracking, though it has nothing to do with the oil-and-gas process people read about in the news—is a well rehabilitation method that uses nothing but clean water under high pressure. There are no chemicals, no sand, and no proppants injected into your aquifer. The process is straightforward in concept. A specialized rig is set up over your existing well, and an inflatable device called a packer is lowered down the borehole and expanded to seal off a section of the well. Clean water is then pumped into that isolated zone at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI or more.

That pressure does two important things. First, it flushes out the fine sediment, mineral scale, and rock debris that gradually clog the natural fractures feeding a well. Second, it widens those hairline cracks and can connect them to nearby water-bearing fractures that were not previously linked to the borehole. In Poway's fractured granite, even a small improvement in fracture connectivity can translate into a meaningful jump in flow. The crew typically moves the packer to treat several zones along the depth of the well, and the entire job usually takes about four to eight hours for a residential well.

Signs a Poway Well Needs Hydrofracturing

Wells rarely fail overnight. More often they decline gradually, and the warning signs creep in over a season or two. If your property sits in Poway's granite country, watch for these patterns:

  • Declining yield over time. A well that comfortably filled the storage tank a few years ago now struggles to keep up with the same household demand.
  • Slow recovery after pumping. The water level drops quickly when you run the pump and takes a long time to rebound, a classic sign that fractures feeding the well have become restricted.
  • Dry-season shortfalls. The well gets you through winter and spring but runs short in late summer and fall, when the surrounding water table is at its lowest and demand is highest.
  • An under-producing new well. A recently drilled well that hit fractured granite but never delivered the flow you expected is often an excellent candidate, because the fractures are there but poorly connected.
  • High irrigation demand outpacing supply. Horse properties, pastures, orchards, and large landscaped lots place heavy seasonal demand on a well. When a property's water needs grow, a once-adequate well can suddenly feel marginal.

Because Poway's wells draw from fractured crystalline rock rather than a sandy aquifer, these symptoms frequently point to fracture blockage or limited fracture connectivity—precisely the conditions hydrofracturing is designed to address.

Why the Geology Matters

Not every well is a good candidate for hydrofracturing, and being honest about that is part of doing the job right. The technique succeeds because hard, brittle rock holds open the fractures that high-pressure water creates and cleans. Poway's Woodson Mountain granodiorite and decomposed granite are textbook examples of this kind of fractured crystalline bedrock. When water is forced into the formation under pressure, the cracks widen and stay open, and the connections to surrounding water-bearing zones persist.

By contrast, wells set in loose sand, silt, or gravel do not respond the same way. In unconsolidated formations there are no rigid fractures to open—the material simply shifts and collapses back, so the pressure has nothing durable to act on. This is why hydrofracturing is so well suited to the back-country and semi-rural areas of San Diego County and far less useful in the sedimentary basins closer to the coast. If your Poway well was drilled into granite, as the overwhelming majority in the area are, the geology is working in your favor.

Check These Things First—Safety and Diagnosis

Before you assume the well itself is the problem, it is worth ruling out the simpler and far cheaper culprits. A drop in water at the tap does not always mean the aquifer is failing. Often the issue lives in the equipment between the well and your house. We always encourage Poway homeowners to start with a few safety-first checks:

  • The pump. Confirm the well pump is actually running and drawing the expected amperage. A failing or undersized pump can mimic a low-yield well.
  • The pressure tank. A waterlogged tank that has lost its air charge causes short cycling and weak pressure that has nothing to do with the well.
  • The pressure switch. A worn or miscalibrated switch can shut the system down early or fail to call for water when it should.
  • Leaks. A hidden leak in a buried supply line, an irrigation valve, or a stuck float can quietly drain your storage and make a healthy well look starved.

Once the obvious mechanical issues are eliminated, a professional flow test is the decisive step. By measuring how much water the well produces and how quickly it recovers, a flow test tells us whether you are dealing with an equipment problem or a genuine well-yield problem—and whether hydrofracturing is likely to help.

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Hydrofracturing is not a do-it-yourself project. It involves high-pressure equipment, downhole packers, and a working knowledge of well construction and local geology. In California, water well work must be performed by a contractor holding the proper C-57 Water Well Drilling license, which ensures the job is done safely and in compliance with county standards. A qualified contractor will also know how to protect your casing and pump during treatment and how to verify results with proper testing.

If your well is showing the warning signs above, if a new well is under-producing, or if you simply want a straight answer about whether your situation is fixable, that is the moment to bring in a professional. The goal is not to sell you a treatment you do not need—it is to diagnose the well accurately and recommend the option that makes the most sense for your property.

Realistic Success Rates and Results

It is fair to ask how often hydrofracturing actually works. In our experience with San Diego County's decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock, roughly 70 to 80 percent of fractured-rock wells see improved yield after treatment. When the technique succeeds, the typical improvement is on the order of two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate—enough to turn a marginal well back into a dependable household supply.

Just as important as the result is how it is documented. A responsible contractor measures your well's output with a flow test before treatment, performs the hydrofracturing, and then runs a second flow test to verify the change, usually within 24 to 48 hours once the well has settled. That before-and-after comparison gives you objective proof of what the treatment accomplished, rather than asking you to take anyone's word for it.

Will hydrofracturing work on my Poway well in granite bedrock?
