How Your Pressure Tank Works

Your pressure tank is arguably the most important component of your well system after the pump itself — and it's the one most homeowners know least about. It serves as the buffer between your well pump and your household plumbing, storing pressurized water so the pump doesn't have to kick on every time someone opens a faucet or flushes a toilet.

Inside a modern bladder-type pressure tank, a rubber bladder separates the water from a pre-charged air pocket. When the pump fills the tank, it compresses the air pocket, building pressure. When you open a faucet, the compressed air pushes water out of the tank and into your plumbing. The pump stays off until the tank pressure drops to the cut-in setting — typically 30 PSI for a standard 30/50 system — then the pump kicks back on to refill.

This cycle is called the pump duty cycle, and it's critical to pump longevity. A properly sized pressure tank keeps pump starts to a minimum — typically 6 to 8 cycles per hour during peak use. Without a functioning pressure tank, your pump would start and stop with every glass of water, every faucet, every appliance that draws water. That rapid cycling — called short cycling — burns out pump motors, damages wiring and pipe connections, and dramatically increases your electric bill.

Many properties in Crest have wells that have been in service for decades. We regularly encounter older galvanized steel tanks, undersized diaphragm tanks, or even original equipment from when the home was built in the 1960s and 1970s. While these tanks may still hold water, they're typically operating well below their design capacity and putting unnecessary stress on the well pump.

Signs Your Pressure Tank Is Failing

Pressure tanks rarely fail catastrophically overnight. Instead, they degrade gradually, and homeowners often adjust to the worsening symptoms without realizing there's a problem until the pump burns out or they lose water entirely. Here are the warning signs we see most often when servicing pressure tanks in the Crest area:

  • Pump short cycling: Your pump turns on and off every few seconds or every time any water is used in the house. This is the number one sign of a waterlogged pressure tank. The bladder has ruptured, the air charge has leaked out, or the tank has otherwise lost its ability to store pressurized water. Short cycling is extremely hard on pump motors — it can reduce a pump's lifespan from 15 years to just 2 or 3.
  • Fluctuating water pressure: Pressure spikes when the pump kicks on, then drops off quickly. You might notice it as a pulsing effect in the shower or water that surges strong for a moment then weakens before the pump restarts.
  • Waterlogged tank: Tap on the side of your pressure tank from top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow near the top (where the air pocket should be) and solid near the bottom (where water sits). If the entire tank sounds solid and uniformly heavy, it's waterlogged — the air cushion is gone and the tank isn't functioning properly.
  • Visible rust or corrosion: Crest's warm, dry summers and occasional Santa Ana wind conditions can accelerate external corrosion, especially on tanks installed outdoors or in open wellhouses. Check for rust at the base of the tank, around pipe connections, and at the Schrader valve on top.
  • Leaking from the tank body: Any water dripping from the tank shell itself (not pipe connections) means the tank has corroded through and needs immediate replacement. Do not attempt to patch or weld a leaking pressure tank.
  • Air spurting from faucets: If you get bursts of air mixed with water when you open a tap, the bladder inside the tank may have ruptured, allowing the air charge to mix directly with the water supply instead of staying separated.
  • Pump running constantly: If your pump runs for extended periods without building pressure, the tank may have a ruptured bladder or major air charge loss, meaning the pump is trying to fill a tank that can't hold pressure.

Well Challenges Specific to Crest

Crest is a semi-rural community in San Diego's East County, situated in the hills east of El Cajon. The area has a high concentration of private well systems, and the local geology and climate create specific challenges that affect pressure tank performance and longevity.

Hard water and mineral content: Groundwater in the Crest area tends to be moderately hard, with calcium and magnesium levels that can cause mineral buildup inside pressure tanks over time. This buildup reduces the effective capacity of the tank and can coat and stiffen the rubber bladder, leading to premature failure. We recommend that Crest homeowners with particularly hard water consider a water softener upstream of the pressure tank, or at minimum, plan on more frequent tank inspections.

Hilly terrain and elevation changes: Many Crest properties are on hillsides or at varying elevations, which affects the pressure requirements of the well system. A home at a higher elevation than the well needs the pump and pressure tank to work harder to deliver adequate pressure. We see a lot of systems in Crest where the pressure settings are too low for the elevation differential, resulting in weak flow at uphill faucets. Proper pressure switch adjustment and tank sizing can solve these issues.

Summer heat exposure: While Crest doesn't get as hot as the desert communities we serve, summer temperatures regularly reach the 90s and occasionally top 100°F. Pressure tanks installed outdoors or in uninsulated wellhouses are exposed to significant temperature swings that stress the bladder rubber and cause air charge fluctuations. A tank that checks fine in winter may be over-pressured by summer afternoon heat.

Wildfire risk and power outages: Crest sits in a high fire risk zone, and the area experienced significant wildfire impacts during the 2003 Cedar Fire. Extended power outages during fire events leave well owners without water — but a properly charged, oversized pressure tank gives you a critical buffer of stored water even when the pump can't run. This is one reason we recommend the largest practical tank size for Crest properties.

Aging well infrastructure: Many Crest homes were built in the 1960s and 1970s, and their well systems haven't been significantly upgraded since. We frequently encounter original galvanized steel piping, undersized tanks, outdated pressure switches, and deteriorating wellheads. When we replace a pressure tank in Crest, we always inspect the surrounding components and recommend any upgrades that would prevent the next failure.

Pressure Tank Sizing Guide for Crest Homes

One of the most common mistakes we see — not just in Crest but throughout San Diego County — is undersized pressure tanks. A tank that's too small for your household causes excessive pump cycling, which shortens pump life, wastes energy, and creates the annoying pressure fluctuations that make showers miserable.

The goal is to limit pump cycling to no more than 6 to 8 starts per hour during peak demand. Here's our general sizing guidance for Crest-area homes:

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: 30 to 50 gallon tank (minimum). A 44-gallon Well-X-Trol WX-203 is our standard recommendation for smaller Crest homes.
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: 50 to 85 gallon tank. The WX-250 (62 gallon) or WX-302 (86 gallon) handles the higher demand of larger households with multiple fixtures running simultaneously.
  • Properties with acreage and irrigation: Many Crest properties sit on larger lots with landscape or garden irrigation. These systems need 85+ gallon tanks, or a dedicated second tank for the irrigation circuit. Running drip irrigation and household water off a small residential tank is one of the most common causes of premature pump failure we see in East County.
  • Properties with livestock or small agricultural operations: Some Crest properties maintain small hobby farms, horses, or chickens. These require higher water capacity — often 85 to 120 gallon tanks or parallel tank configurations to handle the combined domestic and animal watering demand.

Remember that a pressure tank's rated size isn't the same as its drawdown capacity. A 50-gallon tank on a 30/50 pressure system actually delivers only about 14 to 16 gallons of water between pump cycles. That's why seemingly "large" tanks are necessary even for modest households. We calculate the exact drawdown needed based on your pump's GPM rating and your actual peak usage.

Pressure Tank Maintenance Tips for Crest Well Owners

Regular maintenance extends the life of your pressure tank and protects your pump from the damage caused by undetected tank failure. Here's what we recommend for Crest-area homeowners:

  • Check air charge every 6 months: Turn off the pump and drain the tank completely by opening a faucet. Then check the air pressure at the Schrader valve on top with a standard tire gauge. It should read 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure (28 PSI for a 30/50 system). Add air with a bicycle pump or small compressor if needed. Never check air pressure with the tank full — you'll get a false reading.
  • Listen for short cycling: Stand near your pressure tank and run water in the house. If you hear the pump clicking on and off more than once per minute, your tank likely needs service. You can also watch the pressure gauge for rapid bouncing instead of a steady decline.
  • Inspect for corrosion every season: Check pipe connections, the tank base, and the Schrader valve area for rust, mineral buildup, or moisture. Summer heat and hard water accelerate corrosion on Crest well systems.
  • Protect outdoor tanks from heat: If your pressure tank is installed outdoors or in an open wellhouse, consider adding shade cloth or an insulated cover. Reducing temperature extremes extends bladder life and keeps air charge more stable throughout the year.
  • Keep the wellhouse clean and accessible: Clear brush and debris from around your well equipment, especially before fire season. Good access means faster service when you need it, and clear surroundings reduce fire risk to your well infrastructure.
  • Schedule annual professional inspection: A trained technician can detect problems you'd miss — subtle signs of bladder degradation, pressure switch wear, electrical issues, or early corrosion. Annual inspections are especially important for tanks over 8 years old and for properties in high-fire-risk areas.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size pressure tank do I need for my Crest home?

For most residential wells in the Crest area, we recommend a minimum 30-gallon pressure tank. Homes with 3 or more bathrooms benefit from 50-85 gallon tanks. If your well also supplies irrigation or livestock watering, a larger tank or dual-tank setup is essential to prevent excessive pump cycling. We size every tank based on your pump's GPM rating and your household's peak demand.

How do I know if my pressure tank is failing?

The most common signs include: pump short cycling (turning on and off every few seconds), fluctuating water pressure, a tank that sounds solid when tapped all the way up (waterlogged), air bursting from faucets, visible rust or corrosion, and leaking from the tank body. If you notice any of these, call us for a diagnostic before the problem damages your well pump.

How long do pressure tanks last in the Crest area?

Quality bladder-type pressure tanks (like Well-X-Trol) typically last 10 to 15 years with proper maintenance. In Crest's climate — with summer heat and moderately hard water — tanks may need replacement closer to the 10-year mark, especially if installed outdoors without shade. Annual air charge checks and professional inspections can extend tank life and catch problems early.

Should I upgrade my pressure tank when replacing it?

Almost always yes. The cost difference between a 30-gallon and a 50-gallon tank is modest — usually $100 to $200 in materials — but the benefits are significant. A larger tank means fewer pump cycles, longer pump life, more stable water pressure, and a bigger buffer during power outages. For Crest properties with irrigation or multiple bathrooms, upgrading is especially worthwhile.
