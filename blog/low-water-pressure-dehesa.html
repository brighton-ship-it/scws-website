How a Residential Well and Pressure System Actually Works

Understanding the basics makes it much easier to recognize what is going wrong. A typical Dehesa well system has four main parts working together. First, a submersible pump sits deep in the well casing, often hundreds of feet down in fractured granite, and pushes water up to the surface. Second, that water flows into a pressure tank, which stores water under pressure using a captive cushion of air behind a rubber bladder. The tank is what lets you run a faucet without the pump kicking on every few seconds. Third, a pressure switch acts as the brain: it senses when pressure drops to a set "cut-in" point (commonly 40 psi) and starts the pump, then shuts it off again at the "cut-out" point (commonly 60 psi). Fourth, the water travels through service lines, filters, and any treatment equipment before it reaches your taps.

When all four parts are healthy, you get steady, comfortable pressure in that 40 to 60 psi range. When any single component drifts out of spec, the whole system feels weak. A waterlogged tank makes the pump short-cycle, a tired pump cannot build pressure against Dehesa's deep lift, a misadjusted switch caps your pressure too low, and a clogged screen or scaled-up pipe strangles flow no matter how strong the pump is. The trick is figuring out which one, and that is where local experience pays off.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in Dehesa Wells

Based on decades of service calls in this corner of San Diego County, here are the culprits we see most often:

  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: If the bladder ruptures or the tank loses its air charge, pressure swings wildly and the pump cycles on and off rapidly. Dehesa's hard water accelerates corrosion and shortens tank life, so a tank older than 7 to 10 years is a prime suspect.
  • Worn or failing submersible pump: Pumps out here work hard, lifting water from deep, fractured-granite wells against significant elevation. After 10 to 15 years, impellers wear and output drops, leaving you with weak pressure even though the pump still runs.
  • Misadjusted or faulty pressure switch: A switch set to a low range such as 20/40 psi instead of 40/60 psi directly limits your household pressure. Rural power fluctuations common in the Dehesa foothills can also pit the switch contacts and cause erratic behavior.
  • Sediment and clogged well screens: Decomposed granite is sandy and porous, so fine sediment migrates into the well and packs against intake screens. This restricts flow and mimics low pressure even when the pump is healthy. Iron bacteria can add slimy buildup on top of that.
  • Mineral scale and hard water: The granite geology produces hard, mineral-rich water. Over time, calcium and magnesium deposits coat the inside of pipes, the tank, and pump components, narrowing the path water has to travel.
  • Low well yield in dry seasons: Fractured-bedrock aquifers recharge slowly. During drought, the well may simply not produce enough water to keep up with demand. This is not a pressure fault, but it feels exactly like one as flow fades during heavy use.
  • Leaks and aging pipe: Older Dehesa properties still have galvanized lines that corrode and leak, bleeding off pressure before water reaches your taps. Buried lines disturbed by shifting, rocky soil or tree roots are common here too.

What to Check Before You Call a Pro

A few safe, simple checks can help you describe the problem and sometimes pinpoint an easy fix:

  1. Is it the whole house or just one fixture? If only one faucet or showerhead is weak, the problem is likely a clogged aerator or cartridge, not your well. If every tap is affected, the issue is at the pump, tank, or switch.
  2. Watch the pressure gauge near the tank. Note the pressure where the pump turns on and where it shuts off. If it never climbs above the low 30s, your switch may be set low or your pump may be struggling.
  3. Listen to the pump cycle. If the pump rapidly clicks on and off every few seconds while water runs, that short-cycling almost always points to a waterlogged tank that has lost its air charge.
  4. Tap the side of the pressure tank. With the system off, the top should sound hollow and the bottom dull. If the whole tank sounds full and dull, the bladder has likely failed.
  5. Look for wet spots. Damp ground near the wellhead, pump house, or along buried line routes can reveal a leak that is stealing your pressure.
  6. Note the timing. Pressure that fades only during long irrigation runs or late in a dry summer can signal a yield problem rather than a hardware failure.

What you should not do is open the well cap, pull wiring, or attempt to adjust the pressure switch springs while the system is energized. Well systems combine high-voltage electricity and pressurized water, and a wrong move can be dangerous or costly. When the simple checks point past your taps, it is time to bring in a licensed technician.
