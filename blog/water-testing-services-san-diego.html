Ensuring the quality and safety of your well water is crucial, whether you're using it for drinking, cooking, or irrigation. In San Diego County, specific environmental factors make regular water testing an essential part of maintaining a healthy water supply. Groundwater contamination from agricultural runoff, industrial pollutants, and old plumbing systems can introduce harmful substances into your water. SCWS offers comprehensive testing services to identify potential contaminants and ensure the water you use daily is clean, safe, and compliant with health standards.

⭕ The Importance of Testing: Regular testing not only identifies existing problems but prevents future health risks by monitoring changes over time. A proactive approach to water quality maintenance protects your family's health, sustains property value, and ensures compliance with local health regulations.

Importance of Water Testing

The need for water testing extends beyond compliance; it's about ensuring the safety and well-being of everyone who uses the water.

Untreated well water can harbor various bacteria, viruses, and chemical contaminants that pose significant health risks. Regular testing helps detect and address issues before they escalate into serious problems.

Water quality testing may identify indicators of contamination, such as unusual taste, odor, or discoloration. Early detection of such anomalies can prevent expensive repairs and health issues.

Testing helps evaluate the effectiveness of filtration and water treatment systems, ensuring they continue to work as intended over time.

🛡️ Protect Your Investment: Your well is a critical asset. Regular testing safeguards your investment and ensures ongoing water quality suitable for all intended uses.

Types of Water Tests

Several types of water tests are available, each evaluating specific aspects of water quality.

  • Bacteriological Testing: Identifies bacterial contamination, ensuring water safety against pathogens like E. coli and fecal coliforms.
  • Nitrate Testing: Detects high nitrate levels, common in areas with significant agricultural activity, due to fertilizers.
  • Heavy Metal Testing: Particularly important for older systems where lead pipes and fittings may introduce heavy metals into water.
  • Mineral Testing: Determines water hardness, pH, and TDS levels, affecting taste and appliance longevity.
  • Comprehensive Chemical Testing: Uncovers a wide array of chemical pollutants, including VOCs, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals.

🌿 Proactive Testing: Encouraging regular testing can prevent exposure to emerging contaminants often overlooked in routine analyses.

San Diego County-Specific Water Quality Issues

San Diego County's diverse geology creates distinct water quality challenges depending on your location:

  • North County (Ramona, Valley Center, Fallbrook, Escondido): Iron is the most common issue — wells in decomposed granite formations frequently test at 0.5-5+ ppm. Hardness runs 10-25 grains. pH is generally neutral (6.8-7.5). Iron bacteria are common in shallow wells.
  • Mountain Areas (Julian, Palomar Mountain, Pine Valley): Low pH (5.5-6.5) causing copper pipe corrosion is the primary concern. Lower hardness but higher risk of surface water infiltration and bacterial contamination in shallow wells.
  • East County (Alpine, Jamul, Descanso, Boulevard): Variable water quality depending on specific aquifer. Some areas have elevated TDS (800-1,500 ppm). Uranium has been detected in scattered locations along the eastern boundary — worth testing if you're in this area.
  • Borrego Springs/Desert: High TDS (1,000-3,000+ ppm), high hardness (25-50+ grains), alkaline pH (7.5-8.5+). Arsenic occurs naturally in some desert aquifers — the EPA MCL is 10 ppb and some desert wells exceed this.
  • Near Agricultural Areas: Nitrate contamination from fertilizer runoff. EPA MCL is 10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen. Common in parts of Fallbrook, De Luz, Pauma Valley, and Valley Center where avocado and citrus groves are irrigated with fertilizer.
  • Near Former Gas Stations/Industrial Sites: VOC contamination possible. Check California's GeoTracker for known contamination sites within 1 mile of your well.

How to Collect a Water Sample (Step-by-Step)

Whether you're collecting a sample to send to a lab or to prepare for our in-field testing, proper collection is critical for accurate results:

  1. Choose the right faucet: Use a cold water faucet close to where the well enters the house — before any treatment systems (softener, filter, UV). Kitchen sink is usually ideal.
  2. Remove the aerator: Unscrew the aerator from the faucet. Aerators harbor bacteria that can contaminate your sample.
  3. Run the water 2-3 minutes: This flushes standing water from pipes so you're testing well water, not pipe water.
  4. Use a sterile container: For bacteria testing, use only the lab-provided sterile bottle. Don't touch the inside of the cap or bottle. For mineral testing, a clean plastic bottle works.
  5. Fill to the line: Don't overfill. Leave a small air space if the lab bottle doesn't indicate a fill line.
  6. Keep it cold: Put the sample in a cooler with ice. Bacteria testing samples must reach the lab within 24-48 hours for accurate results.
  7. Label clearly: Your name, address, date, time, and which faucet. Note any treatment systems the water passed through.

How Often Should You Test?

  • Bacteria (coliform/E.coli): Annually at minimum. Also test after any well work, flooding, or if anyone in the household gets unexplained gastrointestinal illness.
  • Nitrates: Annually if you're within 1 mile of agricultural operations or have a septic system.
  • Full mineral panel: Every 3-5 years, or when you notice changes in taste, odor, or staining.
  • Heavy metals: At least once if you've never tested. Repeat every 5 years or after well modifications.
  • Before buying a home: Always get a comprehensive panel + bacteria test before purchasing property with a well. This should be non-negotiable in your purchase contract.
  • After any well work: Test 7-10 days after shock chlorination, pump replacement, or any well repairs to confirm water quality is acceptable.
