What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Oak Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Many Oak Hills wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing for Wells in Oak Hills, California

Oak Hills is a semi-rural High Desert community in San Bernardino County, spread across the rolling terrain between Hesperia and the Cajon Pass at the southern edge of the Victor Valley. Unlike the gridded subdivisions down in Hesperia and Victorville, Oak Hills is a place of larger lots, scattered ranch properties, and homes that often sit on their own private wells rather than district water. That mix of fractured-rock foothills along the San Bernardino Mountain front and deeper alluvial ground out toward the valley floor makes hydrofracturing a genuinely useful tool here — for the right wells.

If your Oak Hills well has lost production, the key question is what your well is drilled into. Properties up against the mountain front, on the granitic and metamorphic bedrock that rises toward the Cajon Pass and the San Bernardino range, are excellent hydrofracturing candidates. Wells out on the deep Mojave River alluvium are a different story. We will help you tell which you have before recommending anything.

Two Kinds of Oak Hills Wells

Oak Hills straddles a geologic transition, and that shapes everything about well rehabilitation here.

  • Foothill bedrock wells — on the upper benches and slopes nearer the mountains, wells are bored into fractured granite and crystalline rock. Their yield depends on the cracks they intersect, and when those clog or run thin, hydrofracturing is often the ideal fix. These are the wells we most commonly treat in this area.
  • Deep alluvial wells — lower and out toward the valley, wells tap the thick sand-and-gravel aquifer of the Mojave River basin, where water tables can sit a few hundred feet down. These wells are gravel-packed and screened, not fractured, so a clogged screen or a worn pump — not a sealed fracture — is usually the culprit, and rehabilitation or pump work serves them better than hydrofracturing.

This is why an honest diagnosis matters so much in Oak Hills. The same symptom — falling yield — calls for completely different treatments depending on which side of that line your well sits.

How Hydrofracturing Works

On a bedrock well, the process is precise and chemical-free. Our crew lowers an inflatable packer into the borehole and inflates it to seal off a target section. Clean water is injected into that isolated zone at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes silt, clay, and mineral scale out of the rock's natural fractures and widens those seams, extending them into surrounding rock so groundwater flows into the well more freely. We flow-test before and after, and on a good fractured-rock candidate, improvements of two to five times the starting yield are common. A typical Oak Hills job runs four to eight hours.

Common Causes of Low Yield in Oak Hills

  • Fracture clogging (bedrock wells) — mineral precipitate and fine sediment seal the cracks feeding a granite well.
  • Screen and gravel-pack fouling (alluvial wells) — sand, scale, and iron bacteria choke a screened valley well.
  • Drought drawdown — the High Desert's recurring dry cycles pull the regional water table down, leaving shallow producers short.
  • Mineral encrustation — the area's notoriously hard, mineral-rich groundwater builds scale on screens and borehole walls.
  • A worn pump or motor — sometimes the equipment, not the formation, is the limit.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Find your well completion report: a report showing an open bedrock hole points toward hydrofracturing; a screened, gravel-packed completion points toward rehabilitation.
  • Note your approximate well depth and whether you sit on the foothill benches or the lower valley ground.
  • Check the pressure switch and the pressure tank's air charge first — a $150–$350 switch or a waterlogged tank often imitates a failing well.
  • Track whether the decline was gradual (rehabilitation or hydrofrac territory) or sudden (likely pump or controls).

When to Call a Professional

Call us when production has steadily slipped, when the well runs low under normal demand and recovers slowly, or before you spend on a replacement. We start with a $125 diagnostic (credited toward any work) that includes a flow test and, where useful, a downhole camera survey. In Oak Hills, that inspection does double duty: it tells us your well's condition and confirms which formation you are in, so we recommend hydrofracturing only where the bedrock supports it and a rehabilitation or pump solution everywhere else.

Living on Well Water in the High Desert

Owning a private well in Oak Hills is part of the appeal of the area — independence from district rates and the freedom of a larger rural lot — but the High Desert asks more of a well than gentler climates do. Annual rainfall is low, summers are long and hot, and the Mojave River groundwater basin that underlies much of the Victor Valley depends on episodic recharge from mountain runoff and managed imported water. In dry years, regional water levels drop, and wells that performed fine for a decade can suddenly come up short during peak irrigation season. Understanding that rhythm helps owners read their well correctly: a late-summer slowdown after a dry winter may be drawdown rather than a failing well, while a steady multi-year decline points to a real capacity problem worth treating.

The other constant out here is mineral content. Decades of percolation through mineral-rich desert sediments leave Oak Hills groundwater very hard, often well above 300 parts per million, and frequently carrying iron and manganese. That chemistry not only stains fixtures and shortens appliance life — it actively encrusts well screens and borehole walls, contributing to the gradual yield loss many owners experience. Part of a good rehabilitation plan here is anticipating that encrustation and pairing any downhole work with the right treatment so the gains last.

Why Treating Your Existing Well Usually Wins

New wells in the High Desert are not cheap. Reaching reliable water can mean drilling several hundred feet, and on a foothill bedrock site the drilling is slow and costly. That is precisely why hydrofracturing is so attractive for Oak Hills' rock wells: for a few thousand dollars it can rescue a borehole you already own, often restoring it to better-than-original performance. Even when the answer turns out to be rehabilitation rather than fracturing, repairing and redeveloping an existing well is almost always less disruptive than starting over. We work through that comparison with you transparently, because the goal is reliable water at the lowest sensible cost — not the biggest invoice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Oak Hills well a good hydrofracturing candidate?

It depends on your geology. Foothill wells drilled into fractured bedrock are strong candidates; deep wells in the Mojave River alluvium generally are not. Our $125 diagnostic confirms which you have before we recommend anything.

How can I tell what my well is drilled into?

Your completion report is the fastest clue: an open bedrock hole suggests a hydrofrac candidate, while a screened, gravel-packed completion indicates an alluvial well better served by rehabilitation. Position on the landscape — foothill bench versus valley floor — is another strong hint.

How much improvement can hydrofracturing deliver?

On a qualifying bedrock well, two to five times the pre-treatment flow is common. We flow-test before and after so the gain is measured, not guessed.

My Oak Hills water is very hard — will hydrofracturing fix that?

No. Hardness is a treatment issue, not a yield issue. High Desert groundwater is mineral-rich, and a softener or iron/manganese filter ($1,500–$3,500) is the right answer for water quality.

What does hydrofracturing cost here?

Typically $3,000 to $8,000 for a qualifying bedrock well — far less than the $18,000 to $42,000 for a new well drilled into High Desert ground.

Is the process safe for my well?

Yes. We isolate the treatment zone with packers, use only clean water, and work within safe limits for your casing. It is a routine method performed by a licensed C-57 contractor.
