Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

Common Reasons a Well Pump Won't Start in San Pasqual

A pump that won't start almost always traces back to one of a handful of culprits. Here are the ones we see most often on San Pasqual properties:

  • Tripped breaker or blown fuse: The simplest and most common cause. A power surge, a nearby lightning strike, or an aging panel can trip the double-pole breaker that feeds a 240-volt well pump.
  • Failed pressure switch or clogged sensing port: The pressure switch is the brain that tells the pump when to run. Burned or pitted contacts, or a sediment-clogged sensing port—common with the mineral-rich groundwater along the San Dieguito River—will keep the pump from ever getting the signal to start.
  • Burned-out capacitor or control box: Submersible pumps rely on a start capacitor and control box at the surface. When a capacitor fails, the motor often hums but won't turn over.
  • Failed or seized motor or pump: Years of running through gritty, sandy valley water can wear bearings and windings until the motor finally quits or seizes.
  • Wiring and corrosion faults: Rodents, moisture, and corroded splices in the wellhead or pressure tank area can break the circuit.
  • Stuck check valve: A check valve frozen open or shut can confuse the system and prevent normal pump cycling.
  • Waterlogged or air-bound pressure tank: A tank that has lost its air charge causes rapid cycling and can leave the pressure switch unable to call for a start.
  • Rural power problems: Much of San Pasqual is fed by long rural distribution lines. Voltage sags, brownouts, and outages are more common out here than in the city, and an undervoltage event can drop a pump offline or damage its motor.

Safety-First Step-by-Step Checks

Before you touch anything, understand that residential well pumps run on 240 volts. That is enough to cause serious injury or death. Work methodically and never open a control box or pressure switch while power is live.

  1. Turn the power OFF at the breaker first. Before any inspection of the pressure switch, control box, or wellhead, switch off the pump's double-pole breaker. This is non-negotiable—240V is dangerous.
  2. With power off, open your main panel and look for a tripped or blackened breaker. Reset it once only.
  3. Restore power and listen at the pressure tank for a click from the pressure switch, then watch the pressure gauge to see whether it moves.
  4. Check whether other 240V appliances on the property are working, which helps tell a whole-home power problem from a pump-only fault.
  5. Inspect visible wiring at the wellhead and tank for chew marks, scorching, or corrosion—without touching it while energized.
  6. If the breaker trips again immediately, stop. Do not keep resetting it. Repeated resets of a faulting circuit can start a fire.

Electrical Causes Worth Understanding

The majority of no-start calls in San Pasqual come down to electrical issues rather than the pump itself. A pressure switch with burned contacts, a failed start or run capacitor, a tripped breaker, or a corroded wire connection are all electrical faults that mimic a dead pump. Because San Pasqual properties often sit at the end of long rural feeders, they are also more exposed to voltage fluctuation. Low or unstable voltage makes a motor draw excess current, overheat, and shut down on its thermal overload—or trip the breaker. A technician with a multimeter and clamp meter can measure incoming voltage, capacitor microfarads, and motor resistance in minutes to pinpoint whether the problem is the supply, the controls, or the pump.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't my well pump start even though the breaker isn't tripped?

A pump can fail to start with the breaker intact when the pressure switch contacts are burned, the start capacitor has failed, or a wire connection has corroded open. These faults interrupt the signal or current to the motor without tripping the breaker. A technician can isolate the cause with a few quick electrical tests.

Is it safe to reset my well pump breaker myself?

You can safely reset a tripped breaker one time. If it trips again right away, stop and call a professional. Repeatedly resetting a breaker on a faulting circuit risks an electrical fire and can further damage the motor.

How deep are wells in the San Pasqual Valley?

Well depths vary across the valley depending on location relative to the San Dieguito River and the underlying decomposed granite. Many San Pasqual wells fall in the moderate-depth range typical of inland San Diego County, but your specific depth determines the pump and hoisting equipment needed for service.

Could a power outage have damaged my pump?

Yes. San Pasqual's rural feeders are prone to outages and voltage sags. A brownout or surge can damage a capacitor, control box, or the motor itself. If your pump stopped after a power event, have the electrical components tested before assuming the pump is fine.

Do you replace pressure tanks too?
