What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Good Hope Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

How a Hydrofrac Restores Flow

Hydrofracturing injects clean water at high pressure into your existing well to clean and widen the natural fractures in the surrounding rock. The crew isolates a target zone with an inflatable packer, then pumps water against it — commonly between 1,500 and 3,000 PSI. That pressure clears silt and mineral buildup from the existing cracks and links the borehole to fractures that were too tight to produce. In the hard granitics underlying Good Hope, where essentially all the water moves through fractures, this is the single most effective way to raise yield.

It is a clean-water process from start to finish — no chemicals, nothing added to the aquifer.

Why Good Hope Wells Lose Capacity

In this granitic country southwest of Perris, declining yield usually comes down to:

  • Fracture plugging from weathered granite. Decomposed granite sheds fine, clay-like grit that drifts into the fractures and screen, steadily choking flow.
  • Drought-driven drawdown. Western Riverside County rides through dry multi-year cycles; as the water table falls, a marginal well runs short during summer demand.
  • A new well that under-delivered. Even in good fracture country, a borehole can miss the best fissures. Hydrofracturing such a well is routine.
  • Mineral scale over time. Years of hard-water deposits narrow the flow paths until the household outpaces the well.

Domestic wells around Good Hope commonly run 250 to 700 feet deep, depending on where the property sits over the granitic basement.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Note when shortages hit. Running dry only under several fixtures at once points to a yield issue — what hydrofracturing addresses.
  • Watch the pump cycle. Fast on-off cycling usually signals a waterlogged tank or worn pressure switch, both inexpensive.
  • Watch for a pump that never stops. Continuous running without building pressure may mean the well is drawn down faster than it recovers.
  • Listen for air in the lines. Sputtering faucets indicate the level is dropping to the pump intake during use.
  • Dig out your well log. The driller's depth, static level, and tested yield are your baseline for comparison.

When to Call a Professional

If recovery has slowed, your well runs dry under normal demand, or production has fallen well below the original log, it is time for a professional flow test. Hydrofracturing requires specialized high-pressure gear and an experienced operator, so it is not a do-it-yourself fix. A licensed contractor baselines your yield, inspects the casing with a camera, and confirms your Good Hope well is a candidate before any pressure is applied.

Cost vs. Drilling a New Well in Good Hope

A hydrofracturing treatment typically runs $3,000 to $8,000, with the $125 diagnostic credited toward the job if you proceed. A new turnkey well in this part of Riverside County generally costs $18,000 to $42,000 once drilling, casing, pump, and surface equipment are included.

Should the issue prove to be equipment instead, the repairs are far cheaper: $2,500 to $5,500 for a pump, $600 to $1,500 for a tank, and $150 to $350 for a pressure switch. Because Good Hope's granitic wells respond so well to fracturing and the cost is a fraction of a new well, hydrofracturing is almost always the right first move.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Good Hope good ground for hydrofracturing?

Yes. Good Hope sits over the granitic rock of the Perris block, where groundwater moves almost entirely through fractures. That fracture-controlled geology is ideal for hydrofracturing, which cleans and opens those fractures to raise yield.

How much does hydrofracturing cost here?

Most treatments run $3,000 to $8,000 depending on depth and zones. That is far below the $18,000 to $42,000 cost of a new well, and the $125 diagnostic is credited toward the work.

Why did my granite well slow down?

Usually because weathered granite sheds fine grit that plugs the fractures, mineral scale builds up over the years, or the water table drops during drought. Hydrofracturing reopens the plugged fractures and often restores flow.

Could it just be my pump?

Possibly. Rapid cycling or pressure loss can mean a waterlogged tank, a failing switch, or a worn pump. We diagnose the whole system so you do not pay for hydrofracturing if a cheaper repair solves it.

How much can yield improve?

In hard fractured granite, owners commonly see 2 to 5 times their previous flow. We baseline your yield first and document the before-and-after numbers.

Is the treatment safe for my well?

Yes, when the casing is sound. We video-inspect the borehole before any pressurized work and tell you immediately if we find a problem.
