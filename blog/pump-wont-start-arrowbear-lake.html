Well Pump Won't Start in Arrowbear Lake?

Turn on the faucet and nothing happens? A well pump that won't start is one of the most common—and stressful—well problems Arrowbear Lake homeowners face. Let's troubleshoot.

Common Causes

  • Tripped Breaker: Check your electrical panel first
  • Pressure Switch Failure: Switch that tells pump to turn on
  • Control Box Issues: Capacitor or relay failure (submersible pumps)
  • Motor Burnout: Pump motor has failed
  • Wiring Problems: Damaged wires or loose connections
  • Low Water: Pump may have protection that shuts it off

Quick Checks You Can Do

  1. Check if circuit breaker is tripped (reset once only)
  2. Listen at pressure tank for clicking from pressure switch
  3. Check pressure gauge reading on tank
  4. Look for any visible wire damage
  5. Check if other electrical items on same circuit work

Caution: Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker—this can cause electrical fire or further damage.

When to Call a Professional

Well pump electrical systems use 240 volts—dangerous for DIY repairs. Call us if:

  • Breaker trips repeatedly
  • You smell burning or see scorch marks
  • Quick checks don't reveal the problem
  • You're not comfortable with electrical diagnosis

Well Pump Won't Start in Arrowbear Lake? A Mountain Owner's Guide

Arrowbear Lake is a small, wooded community strung along Highway 18 in the San Bernardino Mountains, sitting at roughly 6,000 feet between Running Springs and Green Valley Lake within the San Bernardino National Forest. Up here in San Bernardino County, winters bring real snow, summers stay mild, and many of the cabins and year-round homes rely on private wells punched into the fractured granite of the range. When a mountain well pump won't start, the timing always feels worst — a cold morning, a full cabin of guests, or the middle of a snowstorm when getting help up the grade is hardest. Knowing what is likely wrong, and what to check, puts you back in control.

The encouraging part is that a no-start pump nearly always comes down to one identifiable component. This guide explains how your Arrowbear Lake well system functions, the failures most common at elevation, the checks you can safely make, and when to bring in a licensed technician.

Understanding a Mountain Well System

Wells in the San Bernardino Mountains draw from water held in cracks and fractures within crystalline granite rather than a deep sandy basin. Most Arrowbear homes use a submersible pump set down in the casing. The system works as a loop: when you use water, the pressure tank empties and its pressure drops; the pressure switch senses that drop and closes, sending 240-volt power to the pump; a control box or capacitor gives the single-phase motor the kick it needs to start; the motor drives water up through the drop pipe; the tank refills; and the switch opens again once pressure is restored. The water level in the fracture system has to stay above the pump intake for any of this to work. A failure in any one of those parts — power, switch, capacitor, motor, wiring, or water level — leaves you with dry taps.

Why Pumps Quit at Elevation

Cold, storms, and power interruptions

Mountain weather is the wild card in Arrowbear Lake. Winter storms knock out power, tree limbs take down lines, and surges from restored power can trip breakers or damage components. A pump with no power cannot start. Reset the well's breaker once; if it trips again immediately, leave it off — something is wrong beyond a simple trip.

Pressure switch failure

The pressure switch cycles constantly and is a frequent failure point. Its contacts can corrode or burn, and the small sensing tube can clog with mineral sediment from the granite aquifer. When the switch stops closing, the pump never receives the start signal.

Freezing-related problems

At 6,000 feet, freezing is a genuine hazard. Exposed pressure tanks, switches, or above-ground plumbing in an unheated pump house or crawlspace can freeze, blocking flow and confusing the pressure-sensing that tells the pump to run. Seasonal cabins left unwinterized are especially vulnerable.

Capacitor or control box failure

The start capacitor in a single-phase system can weaken with age and temperature swings. A common symptom is a motor that hums or buzzes but never spins. Swapping a failed capacitor or control box often revives a pump that appeared completely dead.

Motor burnout and wiring faults

A submersible motor can fail after years of service or electrical stress, requiring the pump to be pulled and replaced. Wiring faults are also common in the mountains, where long wire runs, moisture, and rodents in pump houses all conspire to corrode connections or chew insulation.

Low water in the fracture system

Fractured-rock wells can be sensitive to dry years and heavy seasonal use. If the water level drops near the intake, low-water protection may shut the pump off to keep it from running dry.

What You Can Check Before Calling

  1. Make sure the cabin has power and check the dedicated well breaker; reset it only once.
  2. Listen at the pressure tank for a click from the pressure switch when you open a faucet.
  3. Read the pressure gauge — stuck at zero or pinned high both signal trouble.
  4. In cold weather, check exposed plumbing, the switch, and the tank for signs of freezing.
  5. Inspect visible wiring in the pump house for chew marks, corrosion, or scorching.

Stay safe: These systems carry 240 volts and the pump sits deep in the well. Limit yourself to looking and listening. Opening the control box or pulling the pump is a job for a licensed professional.

Winterizing and Year-Round Care

Because so many Arrowbear Lake properties are seasonal cabins or weekend homes, freeze protection deserves special attention. A pump system that sits idle through a hard mountain winter is far more likely to greet you with frozen, cracked components than one that is properly winterized or kept minimally heated. If you leave your cabin unattended in the cold months, make sure the pump house, pressure tank, and any exposed plumbing are insulated and, ideally, protected with thermostatically controlled heat tape. Drain vulnerable lines if the home will be empty and unheated for an extended period. For year-round residents, a short annual inspection before the first snowfall pays for itself many times over: a technician can confirm the pressure tank holds its air charge, that the switch and capacitor are healthy, and that no wiring has been compromised by moisture or rodents over the summer. Small steps like these are the difference between reliable mountain water and an emergency call during the next storm, when the grade is icy and response takes longer.

Frequently Asked Questions

My Arrowbear Lake pump hums but no water comes out. What's going on?

A humming pump that won't turn typically means a failed start capacitor or control box, though it can also signal a seized motor. Proper electrical testing tells us which, and a capacitor swap often fixes it.

Could my pump have frozen up?

At 6,000 feet it's very possible. Exposed switches, tanks, or plumbing in an unheated space can freeze, blocking flow and disrupting the pressure signal. We can thaw and inspect the system and advise on freeze protection.

Why does my well breaker keep tripping after storms?

Power surges from outages can stress pump components, and a breaker that trips repeatedly is flagging a real fault like a shorted motor or damaged wiring. Don't keep resetting it — shut it off and call us.

How much is a replacement pump in the mountains?

Submersible pump replacement runs $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth and horsepower. A pressure-switch fix is much less at $150 to $350. We always diagnose before recommending a pump.

Is it safe to troubleshoot the wiring myself?

No. The 240-volt circuit and deep pump make DIY electrical work hazardous. Visual and listening checks are fine; leave the rest to a licensed technician.
