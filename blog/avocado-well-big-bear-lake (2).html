Why Big Bear Lake Avocado Wells Are Different

Big Bear Lake sits at roughly 6,750 feet in the San Bernardino Mountains, near communities like Fawnskin, Sugarloaf, and Moonridge. Most local groundwater here is stored in fractured granite and decomposed-granite zones rather than deep, generous sedimentary aquifers, so yields can be modest and inconsistent from one parcel to the next. Wells in this part of San Bernardino County commonly run anywhere from about 200 to 600 feet deep, and the elevation means pumps must work hard against significant lift to push water uphill to grove blocks and storage tanks. Cold mountain winters add another wrinkle: above-ground components such as pressure tanks, switches, and exposed lines can freeze if they are not protected, and a freeze during bloom or fruit set can be costly. Avocados are a marginal crop at this altitude to begin with, so every gallon of dependable, good-quality irrigation water matters more than it would in a milder, lower-elevation grove.

How an Avocado Grove Well System Works

A grove irrigation system is a chain, and the weakest link sets your water pressure and flow. It starts at the well and submersible pump, which draws groundwater from the fractured rock and lifts it to the surface. From there water typically moves to a storage tank that buffers demand, since most mountain wells cannot keep up with peak summer irrigation on flow alone. A booster or constant-pressure pump then pressurizes water from the tank into the distribution lines. Pressure regulation and filtration protect the emitters, and finally the water reaches the trees through drip lines or micro-sprinklers. Mature avocados can drink 40 to 70 gallons per day each in peak summer heat, and a planted acre can need roughly 4 to 6 acre-feet of water per year. When pressure drops at the emitters, the cause is almost always somewhere in that chain, and pinpointing it is what a proper diagnostic is for.

Common Grove Water Problems We See in Big Bear Lake

Based on our years of service in the San Bernardino Mountains and surrounding San Bernardino County, the issues avocado growers call us about most often include:

  • Insufficient well yield during peak demand: Fractured-granite aquifers recharge slowly, so a well that is fine in spring can fall short in August when trees need the most water. A storage tank and smart irrigation scheduling usually solve this.
  • Pump wear from high lift: At Big Bear's elevation, submersible pumps work against heavy head pressure and wear faster, gradually losing the output a grove depends on.
  • Mineral and scale buildup: Granite-derived groundwater is often hard, and scale narrows pipes, fouls emitters, and shortens pump and tank life.
  • Chloride and salinity sensitivity: Avocados are unusually sensitive to chloride and salt buildup in the root zone. Elevated chloride in well water can scorch leaves and reduce yield, so we test irrigation water for the parameters that actually matter to avocados.
  • Freeze damage to exposed components: Winter cold cracks tanks, switches, and above-ground lines that were never insulated for mountain conditions.
  • Clogged emitters and filtration: Sediment from weathered rock and biological growth plug drip emitters and micro-sprinklers, creating dry spots and uneven tree growth.

What to Check Before You Call

A few simple checks can tell you a lot about what is happening with your grove's water, and they help us arrive prepared:

  1. Read the pressure gauge at your pressure tank or booster pump. Note whether pressure is steady, cycling rapidly, or sitting low across the whole system.
  2. Check the storage tank level and watch how fast it refills. A tank that drains faster than it fills points to a yield or pump problem rather than an irrigation one.
  3. Walk the drip and micro-sprinkler lines and look for clogged emitters, blown fittings, or wet spots that suggest a leak.
  4. Inspect the filter housing. A clogged sediment filter is one of the most common and cheapest causes of weak grove pressure.
  5. Listen to the pump. Short-cycling usually means a waterlogged pressure tank, while constant running with low output can signal a leak, a dropping water table, or a failing pump.
  6. Note any freeze exposure on tanks, switches, and above-ground piping after a hard winter night.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does an avocado grove in Big Bear Lake actually need?

A mature avocado tree can use 40 to 70 gallons per day during peak summer, and a planted acre commonly needs about 4 to 6 acre-feet per year. At Big Bear's elevation, a storage tank is usually essential so your well can refill between peak irrigation cycles.

My well runs but pressure to the grove is weak. What is wrong?

Weak pressure with the pump running usually points to a clogged filter, a waterlogged pressure tank, scaled-up emitters, a leak in the distribution lines, or a booster pump that is no longer keeping up with uphill blocks. A pressure test at the wellhead, tank, and emitters isolates which it is.

Is chloride really a problem for avocados here?

Yes. Avocados are among the most chloride-sensitive crops, and elevated chloride or salinity in well water can scorch leaves and cut yield. We test irrigation water for avocado-critical parameters and can recommend blending, leaching, or treatment if levels are high.

How deep are wells around Big Bear Lake?

It varies with the parcel, but wells in this part of San Bernardino County commonly fall in the range of about 200 to 600 feet because groundwater is stored in fractured granite. We always recommend a site-specific evaluation rather than assuming a depth.

Can you protect my grove's well equipment from winter freezes?

Yes. We insulate and, where appropriate, relocate or heat-trace exposed pressure tanks, switches, and above-ground lines so a hard mountain freeze does not crack components or interrupt irrigation during a critical period.

Do you offer same-day service if my grove loses water?

We offer same-day emergency response. Losing irrigation during the growing season can stress or kill trees fast, so call us right away and shut the pump off to avoid running it dry against a depleted aquifer.
