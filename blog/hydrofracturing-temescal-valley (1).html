What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Temescal Valley Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing Wells in Temescal Valley

Temescal Valley is a long, narrow community in western Riverside County, running southeast from Corona along Interstate 15 toward Lake Elsinore, framed by the Temescal Mountains on one side and the Santa Ana foothills on the other. Geologically it is a graben — a dropped block of crust in the Elsinore Trough — floored with alluvial sediment but flanked and underlain by the granitic and metamorphic rock of the Peninsular Ranges Batholith. Neighborhoods like Sycamore Creek, Trilogy/Glen Ivy, Dos Lagos, and the older rural stretches near Temescal Canyon Road sit on this mix of soils, and many outlying and hillside properties still rely on private wells.

That granitic-and-fractured-rock setting is good news if your well is struggling, because hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) works best in exactly this kind of formation. Southern California Well Service has rehabilitated wells across Riverside County's foothills since 2020, and Temescal Valley's geology makes it one of the more promising areas we work.

Why Temescal Valley's Geology Favors Hydrofracturing

The valley's foundation traces back to the Cretaceous, when the Peninsular Ranges Batholith intruded into the crust, leaving a mix of granitic, metamorphic, and sedimentary rock. The valley floor carries fertile alluvial deposits — loamy sands and clay loams weathered from those granitic and metamorphic parents in the surrounding mountains. But the hard rock is never far below, and on the slopes flanking the valley it is at or near the surface.

Wells that draw from this fractured bedrock produce water through natural fractures, joints, and fault seams rather than from a uniform sandy aquifer. Yield depends on how many of those seams the borehole connects to. When they clog with sediment and mineral scale, the well chokes down — and hydrofracturing reopens them. A practical distinction matters here: a hillside or canyon well finished in granite is a strong fracturing candidate, while a well finished only in the deep, loose alluvium of the central valley floor may respond better to other rehab methods such as redevelopment or a new screen. We assess which situation you are in before recommending anything.

Water History of the Valley

Temescal Valley has a long groundwater story. More than a century ago the Temescal Water Company bought up the water-bearing lands here and drilled artesian wells, with the earliest flowing from around 300 feet. Corona-area wells in the northern part of the subbasin still account for much of the basin's groundwater discharge. That history is a useful reminder that water availability in this valley varies considerably from parcel to parcel and depth to depth — which is exactly why a careful, well-by-well assessment matters more here than a one-size-fits-all answer.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Well hydrofracturing uses only clean water under pressure — no chemicals, sand, or additives, and nothing like oil-and-gas fracking. The steps:

  1. Baseline flow test to measure current yield in gallons per minute.
  2. Downhole camera survey to check casing and locate fracture zones.
  3. Packer isolation of the target zone below the casing.
  4. High-pressure injection of clean water at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI to flush and extend existing fractures.
  5. Development and re-test to surge the well clean and confirm the gain, usually within 24 to 48 hours.

On-site treatment typically takes four to eight hours.

Common Causes of Low Yield in Temescal Valley

  • Clogged fractures — the most common cause in foothill wells, as sediment and mineral scale seal the rock seams over years of use.
  • Declining water table during Southern California's recurring droughts, which lowers the saturated zone.
  • Seasonal demand — landscape irrigation and summer use that outpace a marginal well's recovery rate.
  • A borehole that only grazed the fracture network and never reached the more productive deeper seams.
  • Water-quality issues — the Temescal subbasin runs moderately high in dissolved solids, so some owners deal with hardness and TDS that call for treatment rather than rehab.

A Typical Temescal Valley Scenario

Consider a hillside property off Temescal Canyon Road whose well has been dependable for two decades. The owner notices the well no longer keeps the pump satisfied during summer, pressure fades in the evenings, and recovery after a heavy draw takes far longer than it used to. The instinct is to fear the well is finished. But on a granite-finished foothill well, the far more likely explanation is that the fracture network has silted in over the years and a dry-cycle water table has dipped.

A flow test and downhole camera confirm the picture in an afternoon. In most such cases, hydrofracturing reopens the clogged seams and restores or improves the original yield for a fraction of a new well's cost. The whole point of trying rehabilitation first is that it tells you whether the rock can still deliver before you commit to the time, permitting, and expense of drilling a replacement.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Is the shortage constant or only at peak summer demand? Drawdown that recovers overnight points toward a good fracturing candidate.
  • Is the pump short-cycling? Frequent on/off clicking usually means the well cannot keep up with the pump.
  • Do you know your well's total depth and whether it is finished in rock or alluvium? This tells us whether fracturing or another method is the better tool.
  • Is the water hard, cloudy, or high in minerals? That may be a treatment issue, separate from yield.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my Temescal Valley well a good fracturing candidate?

If it is finished in the granitic or fractured rock of the surrounding hills and canyons, very likely yes. Wells finished only in the deep alluvium of the valley floor may be better served by redevelopment or a new screen — we check before recommending.

Does hydrofracturing use chemicals?

No. Only clean water under pressure. Nothing is left in the well, and we clean and flow-test it afterward.

How much improvement can I expect?

Typically two to five times the pre-treatment flow, documented with before-and-after measurements.

Will it help with hard water or high TDS?

No — fracturing increases flow, not water quality. The Temescal subbasin can run high in dissolved solids; for that you would want filtration or a softening/treatment system, which we can also provide.

How long do results last?

Often a decade or more. Heavy use or a deep drought can eventually call for re-treatment.

How long is the process?

On-site work is usually four to eight hours, with confirmed results within a day or two.
