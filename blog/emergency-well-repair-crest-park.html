Most Common Well Emergencies in Crest Park

At over a mile high in the forest, Crest Park wells contend with cold, snow, and seasonal swings in demand. These are the failures we are called out for most often in this part of the mountains:

  • Sudden total loss of water — the most common emergency call, often a failed pump or a frozen, split line.
  • Submersible pump failure or burnout — pumps set deep in granite wells wear out over time, and failures during a storm are especially urgent.
  • Pressure tank failure — a collapsed or waterlogged bladder produces erratic pressure and constant pump cycling.
  • Frozen and burst pipes — at 5,600-plus feet, hard freezes routinely crack exposed lines and damage unheated pump enclosures.
  • Low or intermittent yield — fractured-rock wells can deliver unevenly, and demand surges when seasonal homes fill on holiday weekends.
  • Control box or electrical failure — cold and moisture take a toll on control components and connections.
  • No water after a power outage — frequent mountain outages can leave a pump unable to restart cleanly.

What to Check First

A handful of safe checks can sometimes restore your water, and even when they do not, the information helps our technician show up ready to fix the real problem. Work through them in this order:

  1. Check the breaker. Look in your electrical panel for the well or pump breaker. Reset it one time. If it trips again instantly, stop — that points to an electrical fault that should be left to a technician.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Locate the gauge near the pressure tank. A normal system reads roughly 40 to 60 psi; a reading of zero generally means no water is reaching the tank.
  3. Check the pressure tank. Tap along the tank. A hollow sound near the top and a solid sound near the bottom is healthy; a uniformly heavy, full sound usually means the bladder has failed.
  4. Inspect the pressure switch. The small switch box on the line near the tank is a frequent failure point. Corrosion or burned contacts from mountain moisture can stop the pump from starting.
  5. Confirm the grid. Mountain storms cause widespread outages, so check whether the power loss extends beyond your home before concluding the well is at fault.

What to Do While You Wait

With help on the way, prepare for the cold and conserve what water you have. Draw any remaining water from your pressure tank into containers for drinking, cooking, and flushing. If you suspect frozen pipes, never use a torch or open flame to thaw them — in a forest community at this elevation, that is a real fire danger. Instead, open cabinet doors so household heat reaches the plumbing and wait for our technician. Switch off the pump breaker if you suspect an electrical fault so the motor cannot keep cycling. If snow has piled up, clear safe access to your wellhead, pump enclosure, and electrical panel so we can reach the equipment without losing time.
