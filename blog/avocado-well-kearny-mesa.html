Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Kearny Mesa well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

How Well Water and Irrigation Work in an Urban Setting

In a built-out area like Kearny Mesa, the realistic water questions are usually about irrigation and any existing wells rather than drilling new agricultural wells on small urban lots. The mesa's coastal sage scrub canyons and clay-and-sandstone soils drain and hold water differently than the mesa top, and that affects how landscape and trees should be watered. For the homeowner with backyard avocado, citrus, or fruit trees, the goal is efficient, consistent delivery: a properly zoned drip or micro-spray system on a timer, with filtration to keep emitters clear and pressure regulation so every zone gets even coverage.

Where an older well or a dedicated irrigation pump exists, a typical setup pairs a pump with a pressure tank and, sometimes, a storage tank for larger landscapes. Keeping that equipment healthy, the pressure switch, the tank's air charge, the filtration, is what prevents the short-cycling and pressure swings that quietly damage pumps and starve irrigation. Even on city water, a booster or constant-pressure system can solve weak pressure on a large or sloped property, ensuring your trees and landscape stay healthy through San Diego's dry summers.

Common Water and Irrigation Scenarios in Kearny Mesa

Across three decades serving San Diego County, the issues we see most often on urban and canyon-edge properties include:

  • Pressure problems. Weak or uneven pressure on larger lots and slopes leaves parts of the landscape dry; a booster or constant-pressure system fixes it.
  • Pressure-tank and switch wear. A waterlogged tank or failing switch causes short-cycling that burns out pumps.
  • Clogged filtration and emitters. Sediment and scale block drip systems, causing uneven watering and stressed trees.
  • Aging or abandoned wells. Older properties sometimes have legacy wells that need servicing, rehabilitation, or proper decommissioning.
  • Overwatering and runoff. Urban irrigation is often poorly zoned, wasting water and harming root health.

What to Check Before You Call

If your irrigation is weak or your trees look stressed, a few quick checks help:

  1. Confirm power and the timer. Check the breaker, any pump disconnect, and that your irrigation controller is running its program.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Note whether pressure builds normally, never builds, or builds and crashes.
  3. Listen to the pump. Constant running without pressure, or rapid clicking, points to a tank or switch issue.
  4. Inspect filters and emitters. Clogged filters or blocked drip emitters are a common, quick fix.
  5. Walk the zones for leaks. Look for wet spots, broken heads, or blown emitters.
  6. Note any water changes. On a well, cloudiness or new odor may warrant a test.

Legacy Wells and Decommissioning in Built-Up Areas

One issue that surprises many Kearny Mesa-area property owners is the discovery of an old, forgotten well on a parcel that long ago switched to city water. As the mesa filled in over the decades, plenty of former agricultural and ranch wells were simply abandoned in place rather than properly sealed. An unsealed or improperly capped well is a genuine hazard: it can be a conduit for surface contaminants to reach groundwater, a physical safety risk, and a problem that surfaces during a property sale or development review. We assess these legacy wells, determine whether one is worth rehabilitating for irrigation use or should be formally decommissioned, and perform the destruction to San Diego County standards with the required permitting and documentation. If your well is still serviceable, rehabilitating it for landscape irrigation can offset rising water costs; if it is not, sealing it correctly protects both the aquifer and your liability.
