How a Pressure Tank Works

A pressure tank stores energy, not just water. Inside the steel shell sits a rubber bladder full of water, wrapped in a pocket of compressed air. Your pump fills the bladder and squeezes the air; when you open a tap, the air drives the water out through your pipes with no pump running. The pump only restarts when tank pressure falls to the cut-in point (often 40 PSI) and shuts off at cut-out (often 60 PSI). The water you get between those two numbers is called drawdown, and it is what lets you run a faucet, flush a toilet, or fill a glass without the pump firing every time.

The air side carries a pre-charge set about 2 PSI below cut-in. When that air leaks out, or the bladder tears and water invades the air space, the tank stops cushioning the system — and the pump pays for it.

Waterlogged and Ruptured-Bladder Tanks

A waterlogged tank has lost its air cushion, so it holds only a trickle of usable water. The result is short-cycling: the pump snaps on and off every few seconds. This is the fastest way to burn out a well pump, because each start pulls a heavy jolt of current. In Corona’s wells — which tend to be shallower than the county’s desert wells — a replacement pump is still a serious expense you do not want to bring on early.

Diagnosing it is simple. Kill the pump breaker, open a faucet to release pressure, and depress the Schrader air valve on the tank. Air is good; a spray of water means a ruptured bladder and a tank that must be replaced. A knuckle-tap that sounds solid top to bottom tells the same story — the air is gone.

Signs of Pressure Tank Trouble in Corona

  • Short-cycling: the pump kicks on the instant you open a tap
  • Fluctuating pressure: strong flow that dips and surges
  • Water hammer: pipes knock when fixtures shut off
  • Air spitting from faucets: a hallmark of a failed bladder
  • Rust or moisture at the tank’s seams or base
  • A tank that feels dead-heavy when you try to rock it

Sizing and Drawdown for Corona-Area Homes

Good sizing keeps the pump running in long, gentle cycles — ideally a minute or more per start. That means matching drawdown to the pump’s flow, not just counting bedrooms. Typical Corona-area guidance:

  • 1-2 bathroom homes: a 20-32 gallon tank fits most 5-10 GPM pumps
  • 3-4 bathroom homes: a 44-86 gallon tank handles higher demand and 10-20 GPM pumps
  • Canyon and ranch properties toward Temescal and Sycamore Creek: 86-120 gallon tanks or paired tanks for irrigation

DWR records list Corona with 631 wells on record and an average depth around 194 feet (ranging from roughly 6 to nearly 1,100 feet). The relatively shallow Santa Ana River-fed water table means many Corona wells run at moderate depth — but a mis-sized tank will still short-cycle and cook the pump regardless of how deep the well is.

Setting the Air Pre-Charge

Check the pre-charge once a year. Turn off the pump breaker, open the lowest faucet to drain the tank, and read the Schrader valve with a tire gauge: it should be 2 PSI below cut-in (about 38 PSI on a 40/60 system). Top it up in short bursts if it is low. If the charge bleeds right back out, you have a leaking valve core or a failed bladder, and it is time for service. This is the single cheapest habit that extends both tank and pump life.

Diagnosis and When to Call a Pro

We never condemn a tank without testing the whole system. On a Corona service call we check the pressure switch and its port, confirm cut-in and cut-out, read the pre-charge, inspect the bladder, and verify the pump is still delivering its rated gallons per minute — a weakening pump can imitate a bad tank. Homeowners can safely check the pre-charge and tap-test the shell. Bring in a licensed contractor when water sprays from the air valve, when the pump runs but pressure never climbs, when the switch smells hot, or when rust is weeping. Repair or Replace? Making the Right Call in Corona

A pressure complaint does not automatically mean a new tank. The pressure switch is a common and inexpensive failure — at $150-350, replacing a switch that sticks or arcs often restores normal operation without touching the tank. A leaking air-valve core is an even smaller fix. Where replacement becomes the right call is a ruptured bladder: once the internal membrane tears, the tank cannot hold an air charge, and no repair will bring it back. For Corona homes we generally recommend replacing any bladder tank that has failed after ten-plus years of service, because a healthy tank is the cheapest insurance you can buy for a $2,500-plus submersible pump. We lay out exactly which components can be repaired versus replaced before any work begins.

One thing we watch for on Corona’s Temescal Valley wells is sediment. Grit carried up from the aquifer can score a bladder and foul a switch, so if we replace a tank on a well with a history of cloudy water, we usually recommend adding or upgrading sediment filtration at $300-900 so the new tank lasts its full 10-15 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes a pressure tank to fail?

The two big causes are a slow loss of air pre-charge and a torn bladder. Both leave the tank waterlogged and force the pump to short-cycle. Sediment and hard water speed up the failure by wearing the bladder and fouling the pressure switch.

How do I check my tank’s air pressure?

Shut off the pump, drain the tank through the lowest faucet, and read the Schrader valve with a tire gauge. It should be 2 PSI below cut-in — about 38 PSI on a 40/60 system. If it will not hold air, the tank needs service.

Why is my water pressure surging up and down?

Surging pressure usually points to a waterlogged tank with no air cushion, or a pressure switch that is sticking. When the tank cannot hold a steady charge, pressure rises and falls with each short pump cycle.

How long do pressure tanks last in Corona?

Most quality bladder tanks last 10-15 years. Corona’s harder well water can shorten that, so checking the pre-charge annually and filtering sediment helps you reach the upper end of that range.

Is a waterlogged tank an emergency?

It is not a flood risk, but it is urgent for your pump — every hour of short-cycling shortens the motor’s life. Catching it early can save you a $2,500-plus pump replacement.
