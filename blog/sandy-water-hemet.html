Sand in Your Hemet Well Water?

Finding sand, grit, or sediment in your Hemet well water? This is often a sign that something has changed in your well—and it needs attention before it damages your pump.

Why Sand Appears in Well Water

  • Damaged Well Screen: Screen that keeps sand out has failed
  • Pump Set Too Low: Pump drawing from bottom sediment
  • Over-pumping: Pulling water faster than aquifer recharges
  • New Well Break-in: Normal for first few weeks of new well
  • Casing Breach: Hole in casing letting sand enter
  • Aquifer Changes: Shifts in underground formations

Dangers of Sandy Well Water

Sand in your well water is more than an annoyance—it can:

  • Destroy your submersible pump (sand is abrasive)
  • Clog pressure tanks and water heaters
  • Damage appliances (dishwasher, washing machine)
  • Indicate serious well structural problems

Fixing Sandy Well Water

  • Screen Replacement: Install new stainless steel screen
  • Pump Repositioning: Raise pump above sediment level
  • Spin-Down Filter: Centrifugal separator removes sand
  • Well Rehabilitation: Clean and redevelop well
  • Casing Repair: Seal or line damaged casing

Don't Ignore Sand in Your Water

Sandy water won't fix itself, and running a pump with sand in the water dramatically shortens pump life. Call us for a professional evaluation of your Hemet well.

Sandy Well Water in Hemet: A Local Owner's Guide to Sand, Grit, and Sediment

If you own a private well anywhere in the San Jacinto Valley, from the older neighborhoods near downtown Hemet out toward Valle Vista, East Hemet, and the ranch parcels stretching toward Winchester and Lakeview, sand in your water is a problem you cannot afford to shrug off. One morning the water at the kitchen tap looks slightly cloudy; a week later you are scraping a gritty film out of the bottom of the bathtub and hearing your pump cycle harder than it used to. That grit is not cosmetic. It is a message from your well telling you something underground has changed, and in Hemet the causes are usually specific and diagnosable.

Southern California Well Service has spent more than thirty years pulling pumps, redeveloping wells, and setting sand separators across Riverside County. We know the San Jacinto Valley aquifer because we work in it constantly, and this guide walks you through why Hemet wells produce sand, how we track down the true cause, and what it realistically costs to fix.

Why Hemet Wells Start Producing Sand

Hemet sits on top of the San Jacinto groundwater basin, a deep, sediment-filled trough formed along the San Jacinto fault zone. Over millennia the San Jacinto River and its tributaries laid down thick, layered deposits of sand, gravel, silt, and clay. That layering is exactly what makes the basin such a productive place to drill a well, but it is also why so many Hemet wells eventually cough up sand. When a well draws water through unconsolidated sand and gravel, the only thing standing between that formation and your plumbing is a well screen and a properly sized gravel pack. When either one fails, sand comes with the water.

The most common culprits we find on Hemet service calls are:

  • A worn or corroded well screen. Slotted screens and perforated casing wear open over decades, especially in older wells drilled in the 1970s and 1980s. Once the slots enlarge, the fine sand the screen was designed to hold back streams right through.
  • A pump set too deep or oversized for the well. A submersible that pulls more gallons per minute than the formation can gently deliver creates high entrance velocities at the screen. That velocity scours fines loose. We see this often when a homeowner replaces a failed pump with a bigger one, assuming more horsepower is always better. In a sand-prone Hemet formation, oversizing is a fast track to a sandy well.
  • A dropping water table. Groundwater levels across the San Jacinto basin have fallen substantially in some areas due to years of extraction exceeding recharge. When the water level drops closer to the pump intake, the pump can begin drawing from a zone that carries more sediment, or it can start pulling air and fines during heavy demand.
  • A breach in the casing. Corrosion pinholes or a split seam in steel casing lets sand bypass the screen entirely and enter at a depth you would never suspect.
  • A brand-new well still settling. A freshly drilled and developed Hemet well can produce a little sand for the first few weeks. That is normal break-in. Sand that appears years later is not.

How the Terrain Around Hemet Changes the Picture

Not every Hemet well is drilled into the same material. Wells on the valley floor near the San Jacinto River channel typically tap sand-and-gravel alluvium at depths of roughly 150 to 400 feet, and those are the wells most likely to produce fine sand when a screen fails. Wells drilled into the hillsides and granitic terrain rising toward the San Jacinto Mountains behave differently; there you are often dealing with fractured rock rather than loose sediment, and gritty water tends to mean either a collapsing borehole wall or grit washing in from a specific fracture. Knowing which situation you are in changes the fix entirely, and it is one of the reasons a local diagnosis beats a generic recommendation.

Common Sandy-Water Scenarios We See in Hemet

A few situations come up again and again on our Hemet route:

  • The "it got worse after the drought summer" call. A homeowner near Valle Vista runs the well hard through a hot, dry stretch, the water level draws down, and suddenly there is sand every afternoon when irrigation demand peaks. The pump is fine; the well is being over-pumped relative to its current recharge.
  • The "new pump, new problem" call. A pump company installed a larger submersible after the old one burned out, and within days the water turned gritty. The formation cannot feed the new pump at that rate without pulling sand.
  • The "clicking pressure switch and cloudy water" call. Sand has partially clogged the pressure tank and is chewing at the check valve. The pump short-cycles, pressure swings, and the grit is quietly destroying the whole system.

What to Check Before You Call

You can gather useful information in a few minutes, and it helps us arrive prepared:

  1. Catch a sample. Fill a clear glass jar from an outside spigot, let it settle, and note how much grit collects at the bottom and how coarse it is. Fine tan sand points to screen or formation issues; coarse angular grit can point to casing or gravel-pack failure.
  2. Note the timing. Does sand appear constantly, only when demand is high, or only after the pump has been off for a while? Timing narrows the cause dramatically.
  3. Listen to the pump. Rapid on-off cycling or a pump that runs longer than it used to often accompanies sand problems.
  4. Check your filters. If you already have a sediment cartridge, how fast is it plugging? A filter that clogs in days is telling you the sand load is heavy.
  5. Do not keep running it hard. Every hour a pump runs full of sand is measurable wear on the impellers and bearings.

How We Diagnose and Fix a Sandy Hemet Well

A proper diagnosis starts with a service visit and typically a downhole camera inspection so we can actually see the screen, casing, and water level rather than guess. From there the fix depends on the true cause:

  • Sand separators and spin-down filters. A centrifugal sand separator or a spin-down screen filter installed at the wellhead removes sand before it ever reaches your pressure tank and appliances. This is often the right first line of defense and the most affordable, with sediment filtration typically running $300 to $900 installed depending on flow rate and configuration.
  • Correcting pump size and setting depth. If an oversized or badly positioned pump is the cause, we resize and reset it above the sediment-producing zone. When a full submersible replacement is warranted, budget roughly $2,500 to $5,500 depending on depth, horsepower, and wire.
  • Screen or casing repair and well redevelopment. Surging, jetting, and redeveloping the well can restore a partially failed screen and gravel pack. A damaged screen or breached casing may require lining or, in severe cases, a new well.
  • Supporting components. Sand damage often takes the pressure switch ($150 to $350), pressure tank ($600 to $1,500), or control box and capacitor ($400 to $900) with it, so we check those during the repair.

A focused diagnostic visit runs $125 and is credited toward any repair we perform, and a full well inspection with camera work generally falls in the $150 to $400 range. If the well itself has reached the end of its life, a turnkey new well typically runs $18,000 to $42,000 depending on depth and casing.

When to Call a Professional

Call right away if the sand is heavy, if it appeared suddenly, if your pump is cycling erratically, or if your water pressure is dropping along with the grit. Those are the signals that your pump is at real risk, and a submersible is far cheaper to protect than to replace. If you are seeing only a trace of fine sand in a brand-new well, give it a couple of weeks and monitor it; if it persists, have it checked. When in doubt, a quick diagnostic is inexpensive insurance against a five-figure well failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is there suddenly sand in my Hemet well water?

Sudden sand usually means a well screen has worn open, a casing has developed a breach, or the pump is drawing harder than the formation can supply. In the San Jacinto Valley's layered sand-and-gravel aquifer, a failing screen is the most common cause. A downhole camera inspection tells us exactly which it is.

Will a sediment filter alone solve the problem?

A spin-down filter or sand separator protects your pump, tank, and appliances and is often the right immediate step, but it treats the symptom. If the underlying cause is a failed screen or an over-pumped well, you also need to address the well itself. We usually recommend both: a separator for protection now and a proper diagnosis for the root cause.

Can sand actually ruin my well pump?

Yes. Sand is abrasive and grinds down the impellers, bearings, and seals of a submersible pump. A pump pumping sand can fail in a fraction of its normal lifespan, so the grit you see today is quietly shortening a very expensive component.

How much does it cost to fix sandy well water in Hemet?

It depends on the cause. A sand separator or spin-down filter typically runs $300 to $900 installed. Resetting or resizing a pump, replacing a screen, or redeveloping the well costs more, and a full submersible pump replacement generally falls between $2,500 and $5,500. Our diagnostic visit is $125 and is credited toward the repair.

Is sandy water safe to drink?

Sand itself is not usually a health hazard, but it signals that your well's protective barriers have been compromised, which can also let in bacteria or surface contamination. We recommend fixing the source promptly and testing your water if the sand appeared alongside any change in taste, odor, or color.
