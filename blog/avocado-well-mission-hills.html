Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Well Systems for Avocado Groves

  • Storage tanks for peak demand periods
  • Drip irrigation systems for efficiency
  • Micro-sprinklers for young trees
  • Pressure regulation for uniform coverage

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Mission Hills well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Setting the Record Straight: Mission Hills Is Urban San Diego, Not Avocado Country

Let's be honest about Mission Hills. This is a dense, historic neighborhood of Craftsman bungalows, Spanish Revival homes, and tree-lined streets like Fort Stockton Drive and Sunset Boulevard, sitting on the mesa above Presidio Park and the 5 freeway. Nearly every home here is connected to the City of San Diego municipal water supply through the Public Utilities Department. There are no working avocado groves inside the neighborhood, and the lots — while gracious by city standards — are far too small for commercial grove agriculture.

So if you landed here looking for an avocado-grove well, the truthful answer is that Mission Hills proper does not have them. What we can genuinely help with is everything around water systems that does apply to an urbanized San Diego County neighborhood: backup and irrigation wells on larger canyon properties, water pressure and quality problems, and connections out to nearby unincorporated areas where private wells are common. We would rather tell you straight than sell you a service that does not fit your property.

What Mission Hills Property Owners Actually Deal With

The terrain here matters. Mission Hills wraps around the steep canyons that drop toward Mission Valley and the San Diego River. Homes along the canyon rim — in pockets near Pioneer Park and the western edge of the neighborhood — often have extensive terraced landscaping, mature citrus, jacaranda, and ornamental gardens that demand a lot of irrigation water during San Diego's long dry season. When that water comes off a city meter, summer bills climb quickly.

A handful of larger or older estate properties in and around Mission Hills, and especially in the adjacent unincorporated canyons, do have legacy private wells or could benefit from a dedicated irrigation well to take landscape watering off the municipal bill. That is where our work begins for clients in this part of the county.

Common Reasons Mission Hills-Area Owners Call Us

  • Low household water pressure — often a worn pressure regulator, a failing booster, or sediment in the line rather than the well itself
  • High summer irrigation costs — owners with big landscapes ask whether an irrigation well or a booster-fed storage system makes financial sense
  • Water quality complaints — chlorine taste, hardness scale on fixtures, or rusty staining from old galvanized plumbing
  • Inherited or abandoned wells — older Mission Hills and canyon-edge parcels sometimes have a forgotten well that needs inspection, reactivation, or proper decommissioning
  • New construction and remodels — we advise on whether a property can support a private well under current San Diego County rules

How a Residential Well and Pressure System Works

If you do have a private well on a canyon property near Mission Hills, the system is straightforward in concept. A submersible pump sits down in the well casing and pushes water up to the surface. From there it fills a pressure tank that stores water and maintains steady household pressure, controlled by a pressure switch that tells the pump when to cycle on and off. Treatment equipment — sediment filters, softeners, or carbon filters — conditions the water before it reaches your fixtures or irrigation valves.

When something goes wrong, the symptom usually points to the part. Rapid pump cycling and pressure that surges and drops typically means a failed pressure tank bladder or a misadjusted switch. No water at all often means a tripped breaker, a burned-out pump, or a dropped pipe. Dirty or sandy water suggests the well is drawing down too far or the screen is failing. A trained technician can usually isolate the cause in a single visit.

San Diego County Geology Under Mission Hills

The mesa Mission Hills sits on is largely old marine sedimentary terrace deposits over the region's deeper bedrock. Closer to the river and out into the unincorporated canyons, water-bearing zones become more variable, and decomposed granite and fractured crystalline rock dominate as you move east and north in San Diego County. Wells in those formations can be productive but are unpredictable from one parcel to the next, which is exactly why local experience matters more than a generic out-of-area crew that does not know our ground.

What to Check Before You Call a Pro

  • Confirm the well or pump breaker has not tripped at your panel
  • Note the pressure-gauge reading when the problem happens, if you have a gauge
  • Listen for the pump — constant running or rapid clicking are useful clues
  • Check whether the problem affects the whole house or just irrigation zones
  • Look for visible leaks at the tank, switch, or pressure-relief valve

When to Call a Licensed Professional

Anything involving the well casing, the submersible pump, electrical wiring at the pressure switch, or water-quality treatment should go to a licensed C-57 contractor. These systems combine high-voltage electricity and pressurized water, and a wrong move can damage an expensive pump or create a safety hazard. If you have lost water entirely, if your pump is short-cycling, or if testing shows a contamination problem, call us the same day.

Protecting Your Water System Through San Diego's Dry Season

Mission Hills runs on a Mediterranean rhythm: a brief wet winter followed by many rainless months. For homeowners with significant landscaping, that means irrigation systems carry the entire summer load, and any weakness in a pump, regulator, or pressure tank shows up exactly when you can least afford it. A simple annual check — verifying tank pressure, inspecting the switch, and clearing sediment from filters — prevents most mid-summer failures. If you rely on a private or irrigation well on a canyon-edge parcel, we recommend a pre-season inspection in late spring before peak demand arrives.

It also pays to think about water efficiency on these older, established lots. Mature trees and terraced gardens lose a surprising amount of water to runoff on sloped ground. Converting overhead spray to drip and micro-spray, adding a pressure-regulated zone, or installing a small storage-and-booster setup can cut waste and stabilize pressure at the same time. We are happy to walk a property and give an honest assessment of what will actually make a difference versus what is unnecessary.
