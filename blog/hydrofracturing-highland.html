What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Highland Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing for Low-Yield Wells in Highland

Highland sits in the foothills of San Bernardino County, with the San Bernardino Mountains rising directly to the north and the Santa Ana River drainage shaping the ground to the south. The terrain is defined by alluvial fans spilling out of the mountain canyons and by the transition into fractured bedrock as you climb toward East Highlands and the higher ground. Wells here typically run 200 to 500 feet deep, and the area has strong groundwater potential — though foothill wells often carry elevated iron or manganese that leaves rust-colored staining on fixtures and laundry. The key for yield, though, is the fractured rock beneath the alluvial fans, and that is exactly where hydrofracturing does its work.

When a Highland well slows down, drilling a new well is the expensive last resort, not the first step. Hydrofracturing reopens and connects the natural fractures feeding a bedrock well and can multiply its output for a fraction of the cost. Southern California Well Service has rehabilitated wells across San Bernardino County's foothills for more than 30 years. Here's how it works and when it makes sense.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing uses clean water at high pressure — and only water, no chemicals. We seal a target zone in the open bedrock borehole with an inflatable packer and inject water at roughly 1,000 to 3,000 PSI. The pressure flushes the silt, mineral scale, and fine debris clogging the rock's fractures and opens and links those cracks so groundwater flows more freely into the well.

In Highland's foothill geology, the productive water-bearing rock is fractured bedrock beneath the alluvial fans, and water there travels through fractures rather than solid stone. A well that caught a thin or poorly connected fracture set can be transformed by connecting it to a richer one — the essence of hydrofracturing. The process generally takes four to eight hours on site, with the well back to service within a day or two.

Why Highland Wells Lose Yield

  • Fracture plugging: Mineral precipitate — including iron and manganese, common in this area — can seal the fractures feeding a bedrock well.
  • Alluvial-fan variability: Yield changes sharply across the fans depending on whether a well reaches good fractured rock.
  • Sediment infiltration: Fine material from the fans accumulates in the borehole over time.
  • Dry-year drawdown: When the regional table drops, marginal foothill wells run short under summer demand.

Signs Your Highland Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Production has fallen noticeably over recent years.
  • The pump short-cycles or runs for very long stretches.
  • You get air, sputtering, or sediment at the taps during heavy use.
  • Recovery after pumping has slowed from minutes to hours.
  • You've been quoted a new well and want a cheaper option first.

Fracturing is for wells finished in hard, fractured rock. A shallow well drawing only from loose alluvial-fan material usually needs redevelopment or pump work instead. Our diagnostic determines which applies before any pressure equipment is deployed. (Note that fracturing addresses yield, not water quality — if iron or manganese staining is your issue, we can also recommend appropriate treatment.)

What to Check Before You Call

  1. Find the well log — total depth, casing depth, static water level. San Bernardino County and the state DWR keep records if you don't have the driller's report.
  2. Rule out cheap failures — a pressure switch ($150–$350) or a waterlogged tank ($600–$1,500) can mimic a dying well.
  3. Time the recovery — run the well hard, shut it off, and note how long pressure takes to return.
  4. Track the history — gradual decline points to the formation; a sudden drop usually means equipment.

When to Call a Professional

Hydrofracturing requires pressure rigs, packers, and the experience to read a well's response — it isn't a DIY job. Call when yield has clearly declined, when a new well has been recommended, or when you want a straight answer. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward any work we perform.

Foothill Wells: Yield and Water Quality Together

Highland sits in a sweet spot for groundwater — the alluvial fans and fractured bedrock at the base of the San Bernardino Mountains hold real water-bearing potential — but foothill wells here come with two distinct considerations that we address separately. The first is yield, which is a function of how well the borehole connects to the fractured rock beneath the fans. That's the part hydrofracturing improves. The second is water quality, specifically the iron and manganese that give many Highland and East Highlands wells their telltale rust-colored staining. That's a treatment-system question, not a fracturing one.

It's worth keeping these straight, because a well can have both problems at once. We've seen owners assume their staining and their declining flow were the same issue when they weren't. Our diagnostic separates them: we measure yield and recovery to gauge whether the formation needs fracturing, and we look at the water chemistry to determine whether a filtration or oxidation system is warranted. You get a clear picture and a plan that fixes the actual problems rather than guessing.

On the yield side, a new well in the Highland foothills can run $18,000 to $42,000 turnkey, while hydrofracturing the existing 200-to-500-foot well typically runs $3,000 to $8,000. For a candidate well that's lost output to fracture plugging, that's an easy decision to start with the rehabilitation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will hydrofracturing fix my rust-colored water?

Fracturing restores yield, not water chemistry. Iron and manganese staining — common in Highland foothill wells — is best handled with a treatment system, which we can recommend separately. If both yield and staining are issues, we'll address them together.

Does it work on wells at the base of the mountains?

Yes, where the well reaches fractured bedrock beneath the alluvial fans. A camera survey confirms suitable rock before we treat.

Are chemicals used?

None — only clean water under pressure. We disinfect the well afterward.

How soon will I see results?

We baseline the flow first and re-test within 24–48 hours, giving you a documented before-and-after.

How long do results last?

Often many years, depending on the rock and pumping demand. Some wells stay improved for a decade or more.

What if my well doesn't respond?

We flag poor candidates before treating. If a treated well underperforms, we'll explain the next options honestly, including whether a new well is justified.
