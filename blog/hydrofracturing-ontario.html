What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Ontario Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition

  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Hydrofracturing in Ontario: An Honest Look at Whether It Fits

When water production drops at a property in Ontario, hydrofracturing (hydrofracking) sometimes comes up as a possible fix. It is a legitimate technique — but it only works in a specific kind of geology, and Ontario's is mostly the wrong kind. Rather than sell you a treatment unlikely to last, we would rather explain plainly where things stand in this corner of San Bernardino County and point you toward what will actually help.

What Hydrofracturing Does — and What It Needs

Hydrofracturing rehabilitates bedrock wells. We seal off a section of the borehole with an inflatable packer and inject clean water at high pressure, commonly 1,500 to 3,000 PSI. The water forces open the natural fractures in solid rock, flushes out the silt and scale plugging them, and widens that network so groundwater reaches the well more easily. It uses water only, no chemicals, and takes about four to eight hours. The one requirement it cannot do without is fractured rock. Without solid rock to crack, there is nothing for the pressure to open.

Why Ontario Is a Weak Hydrofracturing Area

Ontario sits squarely on the Chino Basin — specifically the Chino Subbasin of the Upper Santa Ana Valley Groundwater Basin. This is a broad, alluvial-filled valley between the San Gabriel Mountains to the north and the Perris Block to the south. The Pleistocene alluvium beneath Ontario, Montclair, Upland, and Chino is roughly 600 to 700 feet thick and supplies most of the water local wells draw. That is a lot of sand, silt, and gravel — productive groundwater, but loose sediment, not rock. High-pressure water cannot build durable fractures in unconsolidated material, so for a typical Ontario well finished in Chino Basin alluvium, hydrofracturing is the wrong tool.

What Actually Helps an Ontario Well

If your Ontario well is losing yield, you have solid options that fit the geology:

  • Deepen the existing well. Reaching a deeper, more productive zone in the basin can restore output without a full replacement.
  • Drill a new, properly screened well. The Chino Basin is a strong aquifer; a correctly sited and screened well is often a reliable long-term producer. A new turnkey well runs $18,000–$42,000.
  • Repair the equipment. A failing pump ($2,500–$5,500), a waterlogged pressure tank ($600–$1,500), or a worn pressure switch ($150–$350) can imitate a dying well at a fraction of the cost.
  • Rehabilitate the screen. Iron and manganese scale or sediment can choke a well's intake; filtration ($300–$900) or iron/manganese treatment ($1,500–$3,500) often restores both flow and quality.

Common Causes of Low Yield in the Chino Basin

  • Falling or fluctuating basin levels. Managed pumping and recharge across the Chino Basin mean water levels move; a shallow well can fall behind the table.
  • Screen encrustation. Hard water minerals build up on the screen and gravel pack over the years, slowly throttling flow.
  • Pump or pressure-system wear. Equipment ages, and a tired pump often gets blamed on the well.
  • Sediment intrusion. Sand in the water points to a screen or gravel-pack issue rather than an aquifer that needs fracturing.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Locate your well completion report. If it shows nothing but sand, gravel, and clay, you are in alluvium — a non-candidate for fracturing.
  • Track the symptoms. Air at the tap and slow recovery suggest the well; steady weak pressure across the house suggests the pump or pressure system.
  • Look at the pressure tank. Rapid pump cycling usually means the tank or switch, not the aquifer.
  • Check water clarity. Sand or staining points to screen and scale problems with their own affordable fixes.

Living on the Chino Basin

The Chino Basin beneath Ontario is one of Southern California's most actively managed groundwater basins, shared by cities, agencies, and agricultural users across Ontario, Montclair, Chino, Chino Hills, Upland, and Rancho Cucamonga. Its alluvium is hundreds of feet thick and stores water that is recharged in wet years and drawn down in dry ones. For a private well owner, that managed environment means your water level can move with basin-wide conditions, not just your own pumping. It also means the productive resource is sediment all the way down — excellent for a properly built well, but the opposite of what hydrofracturing needs. There is no shallow bedrock here to fracture, which is why we steer Ontario owners toward well construction and equipment solutions instead.

What a Properly Screened Well Looks Like Here

Because the Chino Basin is a sediment aquifer, the right answer to a weak well is usually a well that is engineered for sediment: correctly sized casing, a screen set across the most productive zones identified from the drilling log, and a graded gravel pack that holds back fines while letting water in. Many older or hastily built wells underperform not because the basin is failing but because the screen and gravel pack were never matched to the formation, or because years of mineral encrustation have clogged them. A new turnkey well runs $18,000 to $42,000, but rehabilitating or replacing the screen on an otherwise sound well can sometimes restore much of the lost capacity for far less.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydrofracturing a good fit for a well in Ontario?

Usually not. Ontario sits on the Chino Basin, a deep alluvial valley filled with hundreds of feet of sand and gravel rather than solid rock. Hydrofracturing needs fractured bedrock to work, so most Ontario wells are weak candidates. We will tell you honestly if yours is the exception.

What should I do instead if my Ontario well is weak?

For a well in deep alluvium, the better paths are deepening the borehole to a more productive layer or drilling a new, properly screened well. Often, though, the real issue is the pump, pressure tank, or a clogged screen — all far cheaper to address than the aquifer.

What kind of geology is under Ontario?

Ontario, along with Montclair, Upland, and Chino, sits on the Chino Subbasin of the Upper Santa Ana Valley. The Pleistocene alluvium here is roughly 600 to 700 feet thick — productive sediment, but not the fractured rock hydrofracturing relies on.

How do I know whether I have a rock well or a sediment well?

Your well completion report lists the materials drilled through. If it is all sand, gravel, and clay, you are in alluvium. A downhole camera survey during our diagnostic confirms it.
