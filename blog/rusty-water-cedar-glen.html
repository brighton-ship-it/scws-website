Rusty Water from Your Cedar Glen Well?

Seeing rusty, orange, or red-tinted water from your Cedar Glen well? This common issue is usually caused by iron in your groundwater or corroding well components. The good news: it's fixable.

What Causes Rusty Well Water?

  • Dissolved Iron: Naturally occurring iron in San Bernardino County groundwater
  • Iron Bacteria: Bacteria that feed on iron, creating rusty slime
  • Corroding Casing: Old steel well casing breaking down
  • Corroding Pipes: Galvanized or iron plumbing in your home
  • Failing Water Heater: Rusting anode rod or tank

Is Rusty Water Safe to Drink?

Iron itself isn't a health hazard at typical well water concentrations. However, rusty water:

  • Tastes metallic and unpleasant
  • Stains sinks, toilets, and laundry
  • Can clog pipes and fixtures over time
  • May indicate other water quality issues

We recommend water testing to identify the specific cause and concentration of iron in your Cedar Glen well.

Solutions for Rusty Well Water

Iron Removal Systems

Water treatment systems designed specifically for iron removal, including oxidizing filters, water softeners, and chemical injection systems.

Well Rehabilitation

If iron bacteria is the culprit, shock chlorination and well cleaning can eliminate the bacteria colony.

Casing Replacement

Severely corroded steel casing may need to be lined or replaced to stop rust contamination.

Plumbing Updates

Replacing old galvanized pipes with PEX or copper eliminates household sources of rust.

Rusty Well Water in Cedar Glen

Cedar Glen is a forested mountain community in the San Bernardino Mountains, around 5,200 feet on the eastern shoulder of Lake Arrowhead near Skyforest and Blue Jay. Among the oaks and pines, many homes and cabins here — beyond the reach of the mountain water systems — draw from private wells drilled into the granite and weathered rock of the range. When that well water turns rusty, orange, or tea-colored, the cause is almost always iron pulled from the fractured bedrock and the organic forest soils, and at this elevation, with snow, storms, and seasonal use, mountain wells call for an experienced hand.

Where Cedar Glen's Iron Comes From

The San Bernardino high country is built from granitic and metamorphic rock, and most Cedar Glen wells draw water from fractures and weathered seams within it. As groundwater lingers in those cracks, it dissolves iron and manganese from the rock and from the iron-rich clays that form as it weathers. The forest setting adds to it: decaying pine and oak litter in the shallow mountain soils can pull additional iron into solution, so high-country wells sometimes show more iron than their depth would suggest. Snowmelt and storm recharge then move that mineral-laden water through the system on a strongly seasonal cycle.

Cedar Glen homeowners usually notice one of these patterns:

  • Clear water that rusts as it sits — dissolved iron oxidizing in air, the most common form here.
  • Rusty water straight from the tap — iron already oxidized inside an aging casing, drop pipe, or pressure tank.
  • Seasonal color — staining that worsens during spring snowmelt or after big storms.

Iron Bacteria at Altitude

The cold, low-oxygen, organically rich water of Cedar Glen wells is excellent habitat for iron bacteria. A rusty slime in the toilet tank, a slick film in the pressure tank, or a musty odor usually means a colony has taken hold. They are harmless to health but foul screens, pumps, and filters, and they cannot be filtered away — the well itself must be disinfected and cleaned.

Is Cedar Glen's Rusty Water Safe?

Iron and manganese are aesthetic contaminants, so rusty Cedar Glen water is generally safe to bathe and wash with even though it tastes metallic and stains fixtures and laundry. The practical costs are clogged aerators, stained porcelain, and shortened appliance life. Because mountain granite wells can occasionally carry naturally occurring uranium, radon, or arsenic, and because high-country wells can be influenced by surface water, a noticeable change in color, taste, or clarity is a good reason to run a full water test.

Checks You Can Do First

  1. Hot vs. cold. Rust only in the hot water points to the water heater, not the well.
  2. Sample at the wellhead. Clean there but rusty indoors means plumbing or a fouled filter or softener.
  3. Watch the season. Worse during snowmelt or after storms suggests surface or shallow water reaching the well.
  4. Check the cabin's pipes. Many older mountain cabins have aging galvanized plumbing that adds its own rust.
  5. Inspect filters and the tank. An orange cartridge or sludgy tank confirms iron is reaching your system.

How We Clear Up Cedar Glen Wells

Iron Filtration

An oxidizing iron filter sized from your water test is the core fix, converting dissolved iron to a filterable solid and flushing it on backwash. A sediment pre-filter ($300–$900) handles granitic grit and organic fines from the forest soils.

Softening

Where hardness accompanies iron, a softener ($1,500–$3,500 installed) controls scale and low iron levels while protecting fixtures and water heaters.

Well Rehabilitation and Disinfection

For iron bacteria, organic fouling, or an encrusted screen, shock chlorination and mechanical cleaning are the real cure — and on a low-yield mountain well can also recover flow that has dropped off.

Casing and Plumbing Repair

A corroding steel casing, a failing pressure tank, or aging galvanized cabin plumbing will keep adding rust. Repairing or replacing those components addresses the source. Our $125 diagnostic is credited toward the repair and evaluates the whole system.

Cabins, Freeze Protection, and Seasonal Use

Cedar Glen's mix of full-time homes and weekend cabins creates water issues you do not see at lower elevations. A cabin that sits empty for weeks lets water stand in the pipes, pressure tank, and water heater, giving iron time to oxidize and settle — so the first water drawn after a long absence often runs especially rusty before it clears. Add the freeze-thaw cycles of a 5,200-foot winter, which stress casings, fittings, and above-ground plumbing, and seasonal mountain properties genuinely need a different maintenance rhythm. We help owners with the whole picture: treatment sized to the water, wellhead and pipe protection against freezing, and guidance on flushing and disinfecting a system that has sat idle. Getting these details right is what keeps a part-time Cedar Glen cabin from becoming a rusty-water headache every time you arrive.

Storms, Wildfire History, and Wellhead Condition

The Lake Arrowhead area, Cedar Glen included, has lived through major wildfires and the heavy storms that follow them, and both leave a mark on mountain wells. After a big burn, slopes shed more sediment, and intense winter storms can push runoff, ash residue, and organic matter toward shallow or poorly sealed wells, turning the water cloudy or rusty for days. An older or exposed wellhead is especially vulnerable. Protecting a Cedar Glen well is as much about keeping the wrong water out as treating what comes up: a sound sanitary seal, good surface grading that directs runoff away from the wellhead, and a casing in solid condition all matter. When we service a Cedar Glen well, we evaluate the wellhead and its surroundings alongside the water itself, because in this fire-and-storm country the way water reaches the well is often the real story behind the rust.

Frequently Asked Questions About Rusty Water in Cedar Glen

Why does my Cedar Glen well water turn rusty after it sits?

That is dissolved iron from the fractured granite and organic mountain soils your well draws on. It is clear under pressure and oxidizes into rust when it meets air. An oxidizing iron filter sized to your water test resolves it.

Why is the first water especially rusty when I arrive at my cabin?

When a cabin sits unused, water stands in the pipes and tank and the iron has time to oxidize and settle. The first draw flushes that out. If it does not clear quickly, or recurs, the source is likely in the well or plumbing and worth inspecting.

Is rusty mountain well water safe to use?

Iron is an aesthetic concern, not a health hazard at typical levels, so it is safe for bathing and washing. Because mountain granite can occasionally carry uranium, radon, or arsenic, and surface water can influence high-country wells, we recommend a full water test when the water changes.

Could my old cabin plumbing be the cause?

Often, yes. Many older mountain cabins still have galvanized steel pipe that corrodes from the inside and adds rust independent of the well. Sampling at the wellhead versus an indoor tap tells us which it is.

What does it cost to fix?

Sediment filtration runs about $300–$900, softeners $1,500–$3,500, and iron filtration depends on your test. A $125 diagnostic, credited toward the work, identifies exactly what your mountain well needs.
