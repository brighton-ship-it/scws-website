Avocado Water Demands

Avocados are thirsty trees:

  • Mature tree: 40-70 gallons per day in summer
  • Per acre: 4-6 acre-feet per year
  • Critical periods: Fruit set and sizing

A reliable well is essential for profitable avocado production in San Diego County.

Chloride Sensitivity

Avocados are highly sensitive to chloride in irrigation water. If your Solana Beach well has elevated chloride:

  • Blending with lower-chloride water source
  • Leaching irrigation to flush salts
  • Rootstock selection for salt tolerance
  • Regular soil and leaf testing

Coastal Bluff Conditions That Shape Water Systems

Solana Beach sits on uplifted coastal terraces of sandstone and the Torrey Sandstone and related marine formations, with the lagoon and Pacific defining its edges. Any shallow groundwater near the coast is salt-influenced, with elevated chloride - which is exactly why coastal landscapes, and avocados most of all, demand low-salt irrigation water. Marine air also shortens the life of pumps, tanks and fittings, so corrosion-resistant components matter. On bluff-top and multi-story coastal homes, a booster pump is often the difference between strong, even irrigation and a weak trickle to the upper zones.

How Coastal Irrigation and Booster Systems Work

A booster or constant-pressure system takes municipal supply and raises and steadies its pressure for properties the street main cannot fully serve. A pump, a pressure tank or constant-pressure controller, and a pressure switch or transducer work together: when pressure drops under demand, the controller runs the pump to hold a target pressure. For a Solana Beach property with terraced landscaping or rooftop and upper-level demand, the right system delivers uniform coverage to every zone and protects the equipment from the rapid cycling that marine conditions already make harder on hardware.

Common Coastal Scenarios We See

  • Salt-air corrosion shortening the life of tanks, switches and fittings.
  • Weak pressure on bluff-top or upper-level zones that a booster resolves.
  • Brackish shallow groundwater on the rare legacy coastal well, needing testing and treatment.
  • Irrigation timer and valve faults mistaken for supply problems.
  • Pressure-tank waterlogging causing booster pumps to short-cycle.

What You Can Check Before You Call

  • Power. Check the breaker and fuse for any booster or irrigation pump first.
  • Controller. A tripped timer or stuck valve is a common, simple cause of "no water to the yard."
  • Gauge. See whether a booster system cycles between two pressures or sits flat.
  • Tank. A short-cycling, solid-sounding pressure tank is usually waterlogged.
  • Water quality. Salty or metallic taste and rust staining tell us about chemistry before testing.

Designing for Bluff-Top and Terraced Properties

Coastal Water Quality and Corrosion Control

Two coastal realities shape every system we install near the beach. First, salt air corrodes hardware: steel tanks, switch contacts and fittings simply do not last as long here, so we favor stainless and bronze components and sealed enclosures. Second, any shallow coastal groundwater is salt-influenced, with elevated chloride that scorches salt-sensitive plants - which is precisely why true avocado growing belongs inland on low-chloride well water rather than on the coast. For Solana Beach landscapes, we test irrigation water, recommend treatment only when it is warranted, and choose hardware built to survive the marine environment.
