What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your your well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

The Hydrofracturing Process

  1. Initial Assessment: We evaluate your well's current yield and condition
  2. Equipment Setup: Specialized high-pressure equipment is positioned at your well
  3. Isolation: Packers seal off sections of the well for targeted treatment
  4. Pressurization: Water is pumped at 500-3,000 PSI to fracture bedrock
  5. Development: Well is cleaned and tested for improved yield

Cost vs. New Well Drilling

In your area, drilling a new well can cost $15,000-$50,000+ depending on depth. Hydrofracturing typically costs a fraction of that—often $3,000-$8,000—making it an excellent first option before considering a new well.

Many your area wells that appear to be "going dry" can be successfully rehabilitated through hydrofracturing, saving homeowners tens of thousands of dollars.

Hydrofracturing vs. Drilling a New Well: How to Decide

When a domestic well in eastern San Diego County starts producing less water than it used to, homeowners face one of the most expensive decisions in rural property ownership: should you hydrofracture the existing well to coax more flow out of it, or walk away from it and drill a brand-new replacement well? The answer is rarely obvious. In the decomposed-granite and fractured-crystalline-rock country around Ramona, Julian, Santa Ysabel, Warner Springs, and Descanso, the same low-yield symptoms can point to two very different solutions, and choosing wrong can cost you many thousands of dollars.

This guide walks through how each option actually works, the scenarios that favor one over the other, what to check before you commit, and realistic cost ranges so you can budget honestly. Southern California Well Service has spent more than 30 years working these aquifers, and the most valuable thing we do is help owners avoid spending money in the wrong place. Sometimes that means recommending hydrofracturing; sometimes it means telling you the honest truth that a replacement well is the smarter long-term investment.

How Hydrofracturing Works

Hydrofracturing (often shortened to hydrofracking) is a well-rehabilitation method that uses clean, high-pressure water to reopen and extend the natural fractures in the bedrock around an existing well. Most wells in our region draw water not from a sandy aquifer but from a network of thin cracks running through granite and other hard crystalline rock. Over years of pumping, those cracks gradually clog with fine sediment, mineral scale, and drilling residue. When that happens, the well still holds water but recharges slowly, leaving you short during peak summer demand.

To treat the well, a crew lowers an inflatable device called a packer into the casing and seals off a target zone below it. Clean water is then injected at pressures that typically run from 1,000 to over 3,000 PSI. That pressure flushes the existing fractures clean and, just as importantly, opens new hairline pathways that connect your borehole to a wider web of water-bearing cracks. No chemicals are used. The entire process usually takes four to eight hours, and many wells can be put back into service within a day or two.

The appeal is straightforward: you keep your existing borehole, casing, pump, and electrical setup. You are paying only to improve the rock's ability to feed the well, not to build a whole new water source from scratch.

How a Replacement (New) Well Differs

Drilling a replacement well is a fundamentally different project. Instead of improving the rock around your current borehole, a drill rig bores an entirely new hole at a new location on your property, hoping to intersect a more productive fracture system. In our service area, domestic wells are commonly drilled anywhere from 200 to 600 feet deep, though some go deeper depending on where reliable water-bearing zones are found.

A new well is a complete build. It includes drilling, installing new steel or PVC casing, sealing the upper annulus with a sanitary grout seal to protect groundwater, developing the well to clear out drilling debris, and then installing a pump, wiring, and a pressure system. Because you are starting over, a replacement well carries the inherent uncertainty of any drilling project: until the bit is in the ground, no honest contractor can guarantee exactly how much water you will hit. That said, when an old well is truly failing, a new well sited in a better fracture zone can deliver a dramatically better long-term water supply.

The trade-off is clear. Hydrofracturing is lower cost and lower risk but works only if your existing well sits in rock that can be improved. A replacement well costs far more and carries drilling uncertainty, but it can solve problems that no amount of fracturing will fix, such as a collapsed casing, a contaminated source, or a borehole that simply landed in poor rock.

Common Scenarios That Favor Each Option

When hydrofracturing usually makes sense

  • Declining yield in a once-good well — your well produced plenty for years and has gradually tapered off, which often points to clogged fractures rather than a dry aquifer.
  • A new well that disappointed — a recently drilled well that hit fractured rock but never delivered the flow you expected is frequently a strong fracturing candidate.
  • Seasonal shortfalls — the well keeps up in winter but runs low during dry summers in places like Ramona and Santa Ysabel.
  • Sound casing and structure — a video inspection shows the casing is intact and the borehole is in solid fractured rock.
  • You want to try the cheaper fix first — because fracturing costs a fraction of a new well, it is a sensible first step before committing to drilling.

When a replacement well usually makes sense

  • Collapsed or corroded casing — if the borehole itself is failing structurally, no fracturing can save it.
  • The well is genuinely in poor rock — some boreholes simply landed in a section of granite with very few water-bearing fractures, and improving them yields little.
  • Water-quality problems — persistent contamination or unacceptable mineral content may require a new source rather than more flow from the same one.
  • A previous fracturing attempt underperformed — if the well has already been fractured without lasting improvement, drilling new is often the realistic path.
  • Major change in demand — adding livestock, a second home, or irrigation can outgrow what even a rehabilitated well can supply.

What to Check Before You Decide

A confident decision rests on data, not guesswork. Before choosing between fracturing and replacing, it pays to gather the following:

  1. A measured flow test. Know your current yield in gallons per minute and how quickly the well recovers after pumping. This baseline tells you how far you have to go and whether the problem is the well or the pump.
  2. A downhole video inspection. A camera survey reveals casing condition, the depth of water-bearing fractures, and whether sediment or scale is the real culprit. This single step often settles the fracture-versus-replace question.
  3. Original well records. If you have the driller's log, it shows the original depth, casing, and yield, which helps reveal how much performance has been lost.
  4. Pump and pressure-system health. Sometimes a "weak well" is actually a worn pump, a failing pressure switch, or a waterlogged pressure tank. Ruling these out first can save you from an expensive misdiagnosis.
  5. Your real water demand. Knowing how much water your household, garden, or livestock actually needs sets the target any solution has to meet.

When to Call a Pro

High-pressure well work is not a do-it-yourself project. Injecting water at thousands of PSI requires specialized packers, pumps, and safety controls, and a misjudged treatment can damage casing or push contaminated surface water into your aquifer. Drilling a replacement well is even more involved and is regulated work in California. As a licensed C-57 water well contractor (CSLB #1086994), we handle permitting, proper sanitary seals, and the testing needed to verify results. If your well is running dry, cycling on and off, losing pressure, or simply not keeping up, that is the point to call a professional rather than wait for a complete failure during the hottest part of summer. Realistic Cost Ranges

Every property is different, but these ranges reflect what owners in our rural service area typically encounter:

  • Hydrofracturing: $3,000–$8,000 for a typical domestic well, depending on depth and the number of zones treated.
  • New (replacement) well, turnkey: $18,000–$42,000, including drilling, casing, sanitary seal, and a basic pump setup, varying with depth and site access.
  • Well pump replacement: $2,500–$5,500 depending on depth and horsepower.
  • Pressure switch: $150–$350.
  • Pressure tank: $600–$1,500.
  • Constant-pressure / booster system: $2,000–$4,500.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I try hydrofracturing before drilling a new well?

In most cases, yes, provided your casing is sound and the well sits in fractured rock. Because fracturing costs a fraction of a new well and keeps your existing borehole and pump, it is the logical first step. We confirm it is a good candidate with a flow test and video inspection before recommending it.

How much can hydrofracturing improve my well's yield?

In our local fractured-granite formations, hydrofracturing improves yield in roughly 70 to 80 percent of wells, with typical gains of two to five times the pre-treatment flow rate. Wells in hard fractured rock respond best; wells in loose sand or gravel are not good candidates and may be better served by a different approach.

How long does hydrofracturing take and is the water safe to use?

The treatment itself usually takes four to eight hours, and most wells are back in service within a day or two. Because only clean water is injected and no chemicals are used, the process does not contaminate your supply. We recommend a standard water test after any major well work, as we do with new wells.

When is a replacement well clearly the better choice?

Drilling new makes sense when the casing has collapsed or badly corroded, when the borehole landed in genuinely poor rock with few water-bearing fractures, when there is a water-quality problem at the source, or when a prior fracturing attempt failed to deliver lasting improvement. A video inspection usually makes the right answer clear.

How deep are wells in eastern San Diego County?

Domestic wells in areas like Ramona, Julian, and Warner Springs are commonly drilled between 200 and 600 feet, depending on where reliable water-bearing fractures are found. Depth alone does not determine yield; the number and connectivity of fractures the borehole intersects matters far more, which is exactly what hydrofracturing aims to improve.
