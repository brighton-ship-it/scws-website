How a Residential Well and Pressure System Works

Understanding the basics makes it easier to see where pressure is lost. A typical private well system in the San Diego area has four main parts working together. First, a submersible pump sits deep in the well casing and pushes water to the surface. Second, that water travels into a pressure tank — usually a bladder tank — that stores water under compressed air so the pump does not have to run every time you open a faucet. Third, a pressure switch monitors the tank and tells the pump when to turn on (the "cut-in" pressure, often 40 PSI) and off (the "cut-out," often 60 PSI). Finally, the household plumbing, fittings, filters, and any treatment equipment carry water to your fixtures. When all four parts are healthy, you get steady, strong flow. When any one of them fails or drifts out of spec, you feel it as low or fluctuating pressure at the shower, the kitchen sink, or the irrigation lines.

Common Causes of Low Water Pressure in University Heights

The San Diego mesa-and-canyon landscape shapes the problems we see most often. University Heights sits on a coastal mesa underlain by sandstone and the older marine sediments of the San Diego Formation, with steep canyon walls dropping toward Mission Valley. Wells in and around this terrain are typically moderate in depth compared to the deep granite-country wells of the inland county, but they still face a familiar set of pressure-robbing issues:

  • Waterlogged or failed pressure tank: When the air bladder inside the tank ruptures or loses its charge, the pump short-cycles and pressure swings wildly. This is one of the most common culprits behind sputtering taps.
  • Pressure switch out of adjustment or corroded: A switch with pitted contacts, the wrong cut-in setting, or a clogged sensing port will start and stop the pump at the wrong points, leaving you with chronically weak flow.
  • Worn or undersized pump: Pumps lose efficiency as impellers wear, and San Diego's mineral content accelerates scale buildup. A pump near the end of its life cannot maintain the pressure it once did.
  • Clogged sediment filters or scaled pipes: Fine sand from the surrounding sediment formations and calcium scale from hard water narrow pipe diameters and choke filters, restricting flow before it ever reaches the house.
  • Hidden leaks on the line: On larger canyon-edge lots, a cracked underground supply line or a leaking irrigation valve quietly bleeds pressure away.
  • Low well yield in dry years: During Southern California's recurring droughts, the water table can drop and reduce how much your well delivers, especially on shallower mesa wells.

What You Can Check Before Calling a Pro

A few safe checks can help you describe the problem or even resolve a minor issue:

  1. Confirm it is the whole house, not one fixture. If only one faucet is weak, the problem is likely a clogged aerator or fixture, not the well. If every tap is low, the issue is system-wide.
  2. Read the pressure gauge. Most systems have a gauge near the pressure tank. Watch it through a pump cycle. If it never reaches the cut-out pressure or swings rapidly, note that for your technician.
  3. Tap the pressure tank. Gently rock or tap the tank. A healthy tank sounds hollow on top and solid (full of water) on the bottom. If it feels heavy and full throughout, the bladder may have failed.
  4. Check your filters. If you have an inline sediment or whole-house filter, a clogged cartridge is an easy, common cause of low pressure. Replace it and see if flow improves.
  5. Listen to the pump. Rapid clicking of the pressure switch (short-cycling) or a pump that runs constantly both point to a problem worth a professional look.

Avoid adjusting the pressure switch springs or opening electrical components yourself — those carry shock hazards and can damage the system. When the simple checks do not restore pressure, it is time to call us.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my well water pressure suddenly low in University Heights?

The most common sudden causes are a failed pressure tank bladder, a stuck or corroded pressure switch, or a pump that has lost efficiency. A clogged sediment filter is also a frequent and easy-to-fix culprit. If pressure dropped to nothing at once, check your breaker first, then call us for a diagnostic.

How do I know if it is my pump or my pressure tank?

Watch the pressure gauge and listen to the pump. If the pump short-cycles (rapid on-off) and the tank feels heavy and full when tapped, the tank bladder has likely failed. If the pump runs continuously but pressure stays low, the pump or well yield is more likely the issue. Our technicians confirm the exact cause with gauges and an amp draw test.

What pressure should my home well system run at?

Most residential systems are set to cut in at 40 PSI and cut out at 60 PSI, giving a comfortable 40/60 range. The pressure tank's air charge should sit about 2 PSI below the cut-in setting. If your system never reaches the cut-out point, something is restricting flow or the pump is underperforming.

Can hard water cause low pressure?

Yes. San Diego's mineral-rich water leaves scale inside pipes, fixtures, and pump components over the years. That buildup narrows the path water flows through and wears pumps down, gradually reducing pressure. A softener or filtration system protects the whole system and restores flow.
