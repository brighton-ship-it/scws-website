What is Hydrofracturing?

Hydrofracturing is a well rehabilitation technique that uses high-pressure water to clean and expand existing fractures in the bedrock surrounding your well. This process:

  • Clears mineral deposits and sediment blocking water flow
  • Opens new pathways for groundwater to reach your well
  • Can increase well yield by 2-10 times in many cases
  • Extends the life of your existing well

Unlike oil and gas fracking, well hydrofracturing uses only clean water—no chemicals are involved.

Signs Your Emerald Hills Well Needs Hydrofracturing

  • Declining water production over time
  • Well runs dry during peak usage
  • Slow recovery rate after pumping
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently
  • Lower water pressure than previous years

How the Treatment Works

Hydrofracturing pumps clean water into your well under high pressure to clean and widen the natural fractures in the surrounding rock. The crew seals off a portion of the borehole with an inflatable packer and drives water against it — frequently in the 1,500-to-3,000-PSI range. That pressure clears silt and mineral scale from existing cracks and connects the well to fractures that were previously too constricted to yield water. In the fractured crystalline rock typical of the San Diego region, that fracture-cleaning is precisely what unlocks more flow.

No chemicals are used at any point. A water-well hydrofrac is mechanical work performed with water alone.

Why Hard-Rock Wells Here Slow Down

Around the hills east and south of Emerald Hills, declining yield usually comes down to a handful of causes:

  • Clay and grit from weathered granite. Decomposed granite constantly produces fine particles that drift toward the well and seal off the cracks feeding it.
  • Extended dry cycles. San Diego County's recurring droughts lower the regional water table, and a borderline well begins to fall short.
  • An underperforming new well. Drilling in fractured rock is luck-of-the-draw on which fissures you hit; a weak new well is a routine candidate for treatment.
  • Accumulated mineral scale. Years of hard-water deposition gradually pinch the flow paths until the well cannot keep up.

Wells in this terrain commonly reach 200 to 650 feet deep as drillers chase productive fracture zones through the rock.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Note the timing of shortages. Running dry only under simultaneous heavy use points to a yield problem rather than a plumbing one.
  • Observe the pump cycling. Fast on-off cycling usually means a waterlogged tank or a failing switch — both inexpensive to fix.
  • Watch for non-stop pump running. A pump that runs without building pressure may be outpacing the well's recovery.
  • Check faucets for air. Spitting and sputtering signals the water level dropping to the intake during use.
  • Find your well log. Depth, static level, and the original tested yield give you a baseline to measure today's performance against.

When a Professional Should Step In

If recovery has slowed, the well runs dry under normal demand, or production trails the original log significantly, get a professional flow test. Hydrofracturing demands specialized high-pressure equipment and an experienced operator reading the borehole, so it is not a DIY task. A licensed contractor baselines your yield, inspects the casing with a downhole camera, and verifies your well is a genuine candidate before applying any pressure.

Cost Compared to Drilling New

Hydrofracturing usually costs $3,000 to $8,000, with the $125 diagnostic credited toward the job if you go ahead. A new turnkey well in San Diego County's hard-rock country typically runs $18,000 to $42,000 once drilling, casing, pump, and surface equipment are accounted for.

If the real issue turns out to be hardware, those repairs are far cheaper: $2,500 to $5,500 for a pump, $600 to $1,500 for a pressure tank, and $150 to $350 for a pressure switch. Since hydrofracturing costs a fraction of a new well and frequently multiplies output, it is the logical first step.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there well-water properties near Emerald Hills?

Emerald Hills proper is on city water, but the surrounding hills and canyons toward Lemon Grove, Spring Valley, and the back-country still include private wells drilled into decomposed granite and fractured rock. Those wells benefit most from hydrofracturing.

What makes hydrofracturing work in this geology?

San Diego County's hard-rock aquifers store water in fractures rather than porous sand. Hydrofracturing cleans and widens those fractures, which is the most direct path to more yield in crystalline-rock wells.

How much does it cost?

Treatments generally run $3,000 to $8,000 based on depth and zones treated. That is well under the $18,000 to $42,000 cost of a new well, and the $125 diagnostic is credited toward the work.

How do I know it is the well and not the pump?

We diagnose the entire system. Rapid cycling or pressure loss can mean a waterlogged tank, a worn pump, or a failing switch, all cheaper to fix. You only pay for hydrofracturing if the formation is the issue.

What yield improvement is realistic?

Owners in hard fractured rock commonly see 2 to 5 times their previous flow. We baseline your yield and document the before-and-after so the gain is measured, not assumed.

Will the treatment harm my well?

No, when the casing is sound. We run a camera down the borehole first to confirm it can handle the pressure, and we tell you before doing any work if something looks wrong.
