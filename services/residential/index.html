What Homeowners Need to Know About Well Ownership

If you recently moved to a property with a private well — or you've been on well water for years but never fully understood your system — here's what matters:

Your Well System Has 4 Key Components

  1. The well itself: A hole drilled into the ground (typically 150-800 feet deep in Southern California) lined with steel or PVC casing. The bottom is screened to allow groundwater to enter while keeping sand and sediment out.
  2. The pump: Usually a submersible pump installed inside the well, 100-500 feet below ground. Powered by electricity, it pushes water up to your house. Typical lifespan: 10-15 years for quality brands like Franklin Electric or Grundfos.
  3. The pressure tank: A steel tank (20-85 gallons) that stores pressurized water so your pump doesn't have to run every time you turn on a faucet. This is your buffer — it maintains consistent pressure and extends pump life by reducing cycling.
  4. The control system: Pressure switch, wiring, and sometimes a control box. These tell the pump when to turn on (pressure drops) and off (pressure reaches target).

When something goes wrong, it's usually the pump, pressure tank, or control system — not the well itself. That's good news, because pump and tank repairs are far less expensive than well work.

Annual Well Maintenance Checklist

Most well problems are preventable with basic annual maintenance. Here's what we recommend for every homeowner on well water:

  • Water quality test ($50-$300/year): At minimum, test for bacteria (coliform) and nitrates annually. Add a full mineral panel every 3-5 years. This is especially important in San Diego and Riverside counties where agricultural activity and septic systems can affect groundwater.
  • Visual well inspection (free, do it yourself): Check the wellhead — is the cap secure and sealed? Is the casing sticking up at least 12 inches above ground? Is the ground sloping away from the well? Any standing water near the wellhead? Takes 5 minutes.
  • Pressure tank check ($0 DIY): Check the air pressure at the Schrader valve with a tire gauge every 6 months. Should be 2 PSI below your pressure switch cut-in setting (28 PSI for a 30/50 system). If low, add air with a bicycle pump.
  • Electrical check ($150-$250 professional): Have a technician check pump amperage annually. Rising amps indicate a pump working harder — an early warning sign of wear or declining well production.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my well pump needs repair?

Common signs include: no water or low pressure, pump cycling on and off rapidly (short cycling), unusual noises, dirty or sandy water, and higher-than-normal electric bills. Any of these warrant a professional inspection.

Can I repair my well pump myself?

Surface-level issues like pressure switch adjustment or breaker resets are safe DIY tasks. However, anything involving pulling the pump from the well requires specialized equipment and should be handled by a licensed contractor to avoid damaging the well casing.
