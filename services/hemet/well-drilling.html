Hemet's Unique Geology: Two Different Drilling Worlds

Hemet sits in the San Jacinto Valley—a broad alluvial basin filled with sediment eroded from surrounding mountains over millions of years. This creates two distinct drilling environments depending on where your property sits:

Valley Floor Properties (Downtown, East Hemet, Florida Ave corridor):

  • Well depth: 150-300 feet — Drilling through alluvial sand, gravel, and clay layers
  • Higher yields: 15-30 GPM typical — Sedimentary aquifers store more water than fractured rock
  • Hard water is the norm — 15-25 grains hardness, high TDS (500-1,200 ppm). Plan for water softener.
  • Faster, cheaper drilling — Mud rotary works well in sediment. Less wear on equipment than granite.
  • Water quality challenges — High TDS, occasional arsenic in deep alluvial wells, nitrates from historical agriculture

Hillside/Foothill Properties (Diamond Valley, Seven Hills, Stetson Ave hills):

  • Well depth: 400-600 feet — Drilling into granitic bedrock once alluvium thins out
  • Lower yields: 8-15 GPM typical — Fractured rock aquifers produce less than valley sediments
  • Harder, more expensive drilling — Air rotary with down-the-hole hammers required for granite
  • Better water quality — Lower TDS, less hardness, minimal agricultural contamination
  • Deeper = more cost — 500-foot granite well costs significantly more than 200-foot valley well

Bottom line: Where you drill in Hemet matters more than almost any other city we serve. Valley vs. hillside determines everything—depth, cost, yield, and water quality.

🏡 Hemet's Retirement Community & Well Rehabilitation Reality

Hemet is home to one of Southern California's largest retirement communities. Many properties have wells drilled in the 1970s-1990s when the area developed as a retirement destination. Those wells are now 30-50 years old, and here's the reality:

  • Pumps are dying — Original pumps from the 80s/90s are beyond their 15-20 year lifespan. Replacement common.
  • Casings corroding — Steel casings in hard water (15-25 grains) corrode after 35-40 years. Some need relining or replacement.
  • Yields declining — Older valley wells clog with mineral scale, reducing flow rates from 25 GPM to 10 GPM.
  • Water tables stable (good news) — San Jacinto groundwater basin is managed, so water levels haven't dropped dramatically like some mountain areas.
  • Rehab vs. drill new decision — For wells 40+ years old with declining yields and corroded casings, drilling new often makes more financial sense than expensive rehab.

Buying a retirement-era home in Hemet? Get a well inspection before closing. We've seen buyers inherit 45-year-old wells that need $20,000+ in work within a year. Know what you're getting.

Our Well Drilling Process in Hemet

We drill both valley sediment wells and hillside granite wells. Different geology = different equipment and approach:

1. Site Assessment & Geology Evaluation

We determine if you're in valley alluvium or transitioning to granite foothills. Pull nearby well completion records from County database. This tells us expected depth and yield before we drill.

2. Drilling Method Selection

Valley floor: Mud rotary drilling through sediment layers. Hillside granite: Air rotary with down-the-hole hammers. Using the right method saves time and money.

3. Real-Time Yield Testing

As we drill, we monitor water entry zones. Valley wells usually hit multiple water-bearing sand/gravel layers. Granite wells rely on fracture zones. We test flow rates before finalizing depth.

4. Steel Casing & Grouting

We case through the upper unstable layers and grout to seal out surface contamination. Valley wells typically need deeper casing (120-200 feet) than granite wells.

5. Pump Sizing & Installation

Valley wells with high yield get 1.5-2 HP pumps. Lower-yield granite wells get 3/4-1 HP pumps. We size to well capacity and household demand, not arbitrary "bigger is better."

6. Water Quality Testing & Treatment Recommendation

Hemet valley wells almost always need water softeners (15-25 grains hardness). We test for TDS, hardness, iron, arsenic, and provide honest treatment recommendations—no upselling.

💧 Hard Water Reality in Hemet Valley Wells

If you drill a well on the Hemet valley floor, you WILL have hard water. Not "might"—will. The alluvial aquifer dissolves calcium and magnesium from ancient lake bed sediments, resulting in 15-25 grains hardness and TDS often exceeding 800 ppm.

  • Water heaters fail early — Scale buildup reduces efficiency and causes premature tank failure (7-10 years instead of 12-15).
  • White crusty buildup everywhere — Shower heads, faucets, toilets, dishwashers all get mineral deposits.
  • Soap doesn't lather — Hard water prevents proper soap action, requiring more detergent and leaving residue.
  • Plumbing clogs over time — Pipes narrow from mineral buildup, reducing flow and increasing pressure.

Solution: Budget $1,500-$2,800 for a water softener when you drill a valley floor well in Hemet. It's not optional—it's infrastructure. We install them or recommend trusted local partners. Don't skip this.

📋 Hemet Well Drilling FAQs

How deep do wells typically go in Hemet?

Depends on location. Valley floor properties (downtown, East Hemet, Florida Ave): 150-300 feet through alluvial sediment. Hillside/foothill properties (Diamond Valley, Seven Hills, Stetson hills): 400-600 feet into granitic bedrock. Where you drill matters more than city boundaries.

Will I have hard water?

If you're on the valley floor: yes, guaranteed. Expect 15-25 grains hardness and 600-1,200 ppm TDS. Plan for a water softener. Hillside granite wells have less hardness (6-12 grains typical) but still benefit from treatment. No such thing as soft water in Hemet valley wells.

Why do hillside wells cost so much more?

Depth and drilling difficulty. A 220-foot valley well drills in 1-2 days through sediment. A 500-foot granite well takes 3-5 days using specialized air rotary equipment, costs more per foot ($80-$120 vs. $55-$75), and requires heavier pump motors. Geology isn't negotiable.

Should I rehab my old well or drill new?

Depends on well age and condition. Well under 25 years old: Pump replacement and cleaning often works. Well 35-50 years old with declining yield and corroded casing: Drilling new usually makes more financial sense than expensive rehab. We'll assess honestly and give you both options with pricing.

Do I need a permit to drill in Hemet?

Yes. Riverside County requires a well permit, which we handle as part of our service. Some areas served by Eastern Municipal Water District may prohibit new wells—check with the County before buying property if you plan to drill.

Can I irrigate a large property from a Hemet well?

Valley wells yes (15-30 GPM yield supports irrigation). Hillside granite wells maybe (8-15 GPM is enough for residential + small landscape, but not extensive agriculture). If you're planning vineyard or orchard irrigation, valley floor properties are better candidates.
